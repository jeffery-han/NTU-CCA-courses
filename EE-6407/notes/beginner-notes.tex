\section{\enspace Evolutionary computing in one idea}
Evolutionary computing (EC) is a family of methods for searching for good solutions by maintaining and improving a population of candidates. Genetic algorithms (GAs) are an important member of this family. In lecture, the names \emph{evolutionary computing}, \emph{evolutionary algorithm}, and \emph{genetic algorithm} may be used informally, but strictly a GA is one specific type of evolutionary algorithm.

EC is useful when a problem has a large, irregular, discrete, mixed, or non-differentiable search space, or when exact optimisation would be too expensive. It does not automatically guarantee the best possible answer. It is a stochastic method for finding high-quality candidates within a computational budget.

\section{\enspace Classifying the problem before choosing a method}
Represent a system abstractly as $y=M(x)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item If $M$ and desired output $y$ are known but $x$ is unknown, we have an \textbf{optimisation} problem.
\item If input--output pairs are known but $M$ is unknown, we have a \textbf{modelling} or learning problem.
\item If $x$ and $M$ are known but $y$ is unknown, we have a \textbf{simulation} problem.
\end{itemize}
Many machine-learning tasks can be written as optimisation: select model parameters to minimise prediction error. The distinction is still useful because it clarifies what information is available and what the method must find.

The \emph{search space} $S$ contains all candidates the algorithm may consider. The \emph{feasible set} $\mathcal F\subseteq S$ contains those satisfying every hard constraint. An objective function $f:S\to\R$ measures quality. A constraint-satisfaction problem asks for any member of $\mathcal F$; a constrained optimisation problem asks for the best member.

\section{\enspace Why search can become difficult}
The number of candidate solutions can grow much faster than intuition suggests. For a route through $n$ labelled cities, there are $n!$ possible orders before considering symmetries. Exhaustive enumeration is therefore impractical even at modest $n$.

Complexity words provide context. A decision problem is in $\mathbf P$ if it can be solved in polynomial time. It is in $\mathbf{NP}$ if a proposed yes-answer can be verified in polynomial time. NP-complete problems are the hardest known problems in NP under polynomial reductions, and NP-hard problems are at least as hard but need not be decision problems. Whether $\mathbf P=\mathbf{NP}$ is unresolved. These labels motivate heuristic search; they do not prove that an EA will solve a particular instance well.

\section{\enspace The simplified evolutionary metaphor}
Evolutionary algorithms borrow four ideas from a simplified account of natural evolution: resources are limited, individuals differ, some differences affect reproductive success and are heritable, and random variation supplies new differences. In computing, \emph{fitness} is a deliberately designed numerical score rather than a biological fact.

The terms below connect the problem to its representation:
\begin{itemize}
\item A \emph{phenotype} is the candidate solution in the original problem domain.
\item A \emph{genotype} is the encoding manipulated by the algorithm.
\item A \emph{chromosome} is a complete genotype; a \emph{gene} is one component; an \emph{allele} is a possible value of that component.
\end{itemize}
The representation is an engineering decision. It should make valid solutions reachable and should work naturally with the variation operators introduced later. A poor representation can make a simple problem unnecessarily difficult.

\section{\enspace Where evolutionary algorithms stand}

\subsection{Heuristics, metaheuristics, and the two inspirations}
A \emph{heuristic} is a rule of thumb: a rule that decides what to try next
without promising that the choice is optimal. A \emph{metaheuristic} sits one
level above that. It is a general search scheme that organises such rules,
and in the lecture's phrasing it is ``a heuristic about heuristics''. The
point the lecturer stresses is the source of the rules: a genetic algorithm
is a metaheuristic whose guiding rules are borrowed from \emph{nature} rather
than from a human expert's knowledge of one particular problem. Ant-colony
optimisation, particle-swarm optimisation, and related nature-inspired
schemes are metaheuristics in exactly the same sense.

Two separate strands of natural inspiration are worth being able to name
separately:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Darwinian natural evolution.} Resources are insufficient, so
      there is competition; selection retains the fitter individuals; and
      reproduction is the \emph{reward} for having survived selection.
\item \textbf{Genetics.} DNA supplies the metaphor of an encoded description
      (the genotype) whose physical expression is the organism (the
      phenotype).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deterministic versus heuristic problem solving}
Before evolutionary methods, the standard tools were exact deterministic
searches such as branch and bound. The two families fail in opposite ways.

\medskip
This is the practical reason NP-hard problems pushed the field towards
heuristics: an exact method that is guaranteed but unaffordable is not a
usable method.

\subsection{Building the picture that leads to no free lunch}
The lecture derives ``no free lunch'' from a picture rather than from the
formal proof, and the picture is the examinable part. Draw performance on the
vertical axis and \emph{all possible problems} on the horizontal axis, in some
arbitrary but fixed order.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Random search.} A low, flat line. It is never good, but it is
      never specifically bad either, because it assumes nothing.
\item \textbf{A method tailored to one problem.} A tall, narrow spike. It is
      excellent on the class it was customised for, and poor elsewhere.
\item \textbf{A classical EA with no problem knowledge.} A broad, moderately
      high curve: generally better than random search over a wide range of
      problems, and generally worse than a custom method on that method's own
      problem.
\item \textbf{EAs with increasing domain knowledge (EA$_1$ to EA$_4$).} Each
      increment of knowledge -- a better representation, a repair rule, a
      specialised operator, a seeded population -- pushes the curve
      \emph{up} on the intended class and \emph{down} elsewhere. The curve is
      deformed, not lifted.
\end{enumerate}

Now ask the lecturer's question. If you want to solve one specific problem,
the most specialised algorithm whose peak sits over that problem is clearly
the best choice. But what is the \emph{average} performance of each curve
across \emph{all} problems? If you accept the picture -- each algorithm
redistributes a fixed amount of performance rather than creating more -- then
every curve encloses the same area, so every algorithm has the same average.

\paragraph{The statement.} This is the \emph{no free lunch} (NFL) theorem of
optimisation: averaged over all possible objective functions, no search
algorithm outperforms any other. Gains on one class of problems are paid for
by losses on another. The result is due to Wolpert and Macready (1997); the
lecture refers to it as ``the classic paper in optimisation''.

\paragraph{What NFL does and does not say.} It does \emph{not} say that
algorithm choice is irrelevant. Real engineering problems are a tiny,
structured subset of ``all possible objective functions'', and on that subset
matching the algorithm's bias to the problem's structure is exactly what
works. NFL says there is no universally best optimiser, so the honest
question is never ``which algorithm is best?'' but ``which algorithm is
biased in a way that suits \emph{this} problem?''

\subsection{Global search, local search, and what a neighbourhood is}
\emph{Global} optimisation means searching over all possibilities with the
best solution in the whole feasible set as the objective. Stating that as the
objective is not the same as achieving it: a global search may still return a
local optimum.

\emph{Local} search means looking only in the vicinity of the current
solution: ``where do I go next?'' is answered by inspecting neighbours. The
lecture's warning is that ``neighbour'' is not a fixed notion. Depending on
the design it can mean
\begin{itemize}
\item a small difference in gene values, such as a single changed allele;
\item closeness after decoding, that is, similarity in the phenotype;
\item a distance measure declared in advance, such as Hamming distance on bit
      strings.
\end{itemize}
Consequently \emph{local optimum} is meaningless until the neighbourhood is
stated. A point can be a local optimum under one-bit flips and not under
two-bit flips. Whenever a question asks about local optima, name the
neighbourhood first.

\section{\enspace Representations and their variation operators}

\subsection{Why representation is the first design decision}
Representation is the bridge between the problem as it exists in the world
and the strings the algorithm can manipulate. It comes first because
everything downstream depends on it: the size and shape of the search space,
what counts as a valid candidate, how fitness is computed, and -- above all
-- \emph{which variation operators are even legal}.

The lecture's rule is short and is worth memorising verbatim: \textbf{the
operators must suit the representation.} A crossover that works perfectly on
bit strings can destroy a permutation. The purpose of both operators is the
same in every representation -- create variation, and hence diversity -- but
the mechanism must respect the encoding.

\subsection{Binary representation}

\paragraph{Encoding and decoding.}
Holland's 1975 genetic algorithm was demonstrated mainly on bit strings, and
a great many problems were solved this way. \emph{Encoding} maps the
phenotype space (the problem) into the genotype space $\{0,1\}^L$, where $L$
is the string length. \emph{Decoding} is the inverse map, applied before
every fitness evaluation.

\paragraph{Mutation: bit flip.}
Mutation is a \emph{unary} operator: it takes one chromosome and changes it.
Walk along the string gene by gene; at each gene, toss a coin that comes up
``mutate'' with probability $p_m$; if it does, flip that bit. In binary this
is unambiguous, because the only other value is the complement: $0\to1$ and
$1\to0$.

\paragraph{How large should $p_m$ be?}
The lecture's guide is that the mutation rate is \emph{small}, typically
between $1/\mu$ (one over the population size) and $1/L$ (one over the
chromosome length). The reason is easy to state: at a large rate, say
$p_m=0.8$, you are flipping most bits every generation, which is
indistinguishable from random search. Mutation is meant to inject randomness
\emph{sporadically}, not continuously.

\paragraph{A subtlety: mutation is not as unbiased as it looks.}
Suppose the bit string decodes to an integer. Flipping the most significant
bit changes the decoded value enormously; flipping the least significant bit
barely moves it. So although every gene is chosen with the same probability,
the \emph{effect} of mutation is heavily biased towards the early, more
significant genes. If that bias is a problem, the standard remedy named in
the lecture is \textbf{Gray coding}, in which numerically adjacent values
differ by exactly one bit, so a single flip is always a small phenotype move.

\paragraph{Crossover.}
Crossover (recombination) is the operator that implements inheritance: it
combines material from two parents that selection has already judged to be
comparatively fit.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One-point.} Choose one crossover site, split both parents
      there, and build children from complementary halves.
\item \textbf{Multi-point.} Choose $n$ sites, split into $n+1$ segments, and
      take segments alternately from the two parents. Useful for long
      strings, where a single cut is a very coarse operation.
\item \textbf{Uniform.} Decide gene by gene, at random, which parent
      contributes.
\end{itemize}
Nothing guarantees a child is fitter than its parents; the hope is that
useful partial structures occasionally combine, and selection keeps the
successes.

\paragraph{Do we need both operators?}
This was an open debate in early GA research. The settled answer in the
lecture is that both are needed, and the cleanest argument is about
information:
\begin{center}
\emph{Only crossover can combine information from two parents; only mutation
can introduce new information.}
\end{center}

\paragraph{The allele-frequency argument.}
Here is the lecture's concrete demonstration that crossover alone is not
enough. Start a binary population in which zeros and ones occur equally often,
a ratio of $1:1$ over the whole population. Crossover only \emph{moves}
existing genes between strings; it never creates a value that was not already
present at that locus. So after ten generations, or a hundred, of crossover
alone, the population-wide ratio of zeros to ones is still $1:1$. If some
allele has been lost entirely, crossover can never recover it. Mutation can,
because it manufactures values rather than shuffling them.

\paragraph{Exploration and exploitation (lecture convention).}
Searching a landscape needs two opposing forces: \emph{exploration}, covering
new regions, and \emph{exploitation}, refining what already looks good. The
lecture assigns them as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Crossover is explorative.} It makes a large jump to a point
      somewhere ``between'' the two parents -- the lecture's analogy is
      interpolation.
\item \textbf{Mutation is exploitative.} It takes an existing solution and
      perturbs it slightly, hoping the small variation is an improvement.
\end{itemize}
So under this convention, the advice for a GA that is under-performing is to
adjust the balance -- typically by raising the mutation rate to get more
local refinement, or the crossover rate for more coverage.

\paragraph{Caution, worth knowing but not worth arguing in the quiz.} Several
standard textbooks assign these labels the other way round, calling mutation
the explorative operator (it can reach anywhere) and crossover the
exploitative one (it recombines material already in the population). Use the
lecture's convention above in this course, and if a question asks you to
justify the assignment, argue from \emph{step size}: crossover makes the large
jump, mutation the small local move.

\subsection{Integer representation}

\paragraph{When to use it.}
Instead of $\{0,1\}$, each gene draws from a finite set of $K$ labels,
$\{1,2,\ldots,K\}$, giving a genotype space $\{1,\ldots,K\}^L$. This suits
problems whose decisions are categorical: assigning one of three colours,
sorting items into classes, choosing a machine for a job.

\paragraph{Mutation.} With more than two values, ``flip it'' is no longer
defined, so there are two standard choices.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Creep mutation.} Add a small positive or negative amount to the
      gene with probability $p$. It is a small, local move -- and that is
      also its weakness. Because every step is small, individuals tend to
      \emph{creep} towards one another over the generations, and the
      population loses diversity. This is the mechanism of
      \textbf{premature convergence}: the run settles before it has explored
      enough.
\item \textbf{Random resetting.} With some probability, replace the gene by a
      value drawn uniformly from $\{1,\ldots,K\}$. This is the true analogue
      of the binary bit flip, because binary mutation is always a complete
      change of value, never a small step.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Crossover.} Everything from the binary case carries over
unchanged: one-point, multi-point, and uniform crossover all produce valid
integer strings, because any label is legal at any position.

\paragraph{Worked example: graph colouring.}
Imagine you must print a map of the United States so that the regions are
distinguishable. Adjacent regions must therefore get different colours.
Abstract each region to a \emph{vertex} and each shared border to an
\emph{edge}, giving a graph $G=(V,E)$ with $|V|=L$ regions. A colouring
assigns one colour to each vertex such that adjacent vertices differ.

With $k$ available colours, the encoding is immediate: a chromosome is
\[
  c=(c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_L),\qquad c_i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},
\]
where $c_i$ is the colour of region $i$, so the genotype space is
$\{1,\ldots,k\}^L$.

The objective function counts broken adjacency rules:
\[
  V(c)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\mathbf 1\!\left[c_u=c_v\right],
\]
which is to be minimised, with $V(c)=0$ exactly at a valid colouring. For a
maximisation convention use $f(c)=|E|-V(c)$. Note what the encoding does
\emph{not} do: unlike the $N$-queens permutation, it cannot make the
constraint impossible to violate, so the constraint must be paid for in the
objective.

The associated question is the \emph{$k$-colouring} question: what is the
minimum $k$ such that no two adjacent regions share a colour? Two facts from
the lecture:
\begin{itemize}
\item $k=3$ is \emph{not} enough for the map of the United States.
\item $k=4$ is enough for any such map -- the four-colour theorem, proved for
      planar maps.
\end{itemize}
A good self-test is to take the map of Singapore's regions and check by hand
whether three colours suffice.

\subsection{Real-valued (floating-point) representation}

\paragraph{When to use it.}
Many engineering problems ask for continuous parameter values: the gains of a
PID controller, the coefficients of a filter, the shape parameters of a
design. If the answer is a vector of real numbers, the natural encoding is a
vector of real numbers, $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\in\R^n$, usually with each
variable boxed into an interval.

\paragraph{Benchmark functions.}
The GA community demonstrates robustness on \emph{benchmark} functions:
standard, published test problems $f:\R^n\to\R$ with known optima, so that
different algorithms can be compared. The lecture's example is the
\textbf{Ackley function}, a multimodal benchmark whose global minimum is at
the origin with value $0$:
\[
  f(x)=-20\exp\!\left(-0.2\sqrt{\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^{2}}\right)
       -\exp\!\left(\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\cos(2\pi x_i)\right)
       +20+e .
\]
Here $n$ is the \emph{dimension} -- the number of variables being solved for.
At $n=2$ it can be plotted as a surface, with the two variables horizontal
and $f(x)$ vertical, and you can see the single global basin surrounded by
many local wells. Benchmarks are deliberately scalable: researchers run the
same function at $n=10$, $100$, or $1000$ to show an algorithm survives high
dimension.

\paragraph{You may still use binary for a real problem.}
Real-valued encoding is natural, but it is not compulsory. To encode a
bounded real variable $z\in[x,y]$ as a bit string $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_L)$,
decode with
\[
  \Gamma(a)=x+\frac{y-x}{2^{L}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_i\,2^{\,L-i}.
\]
The important structural point: you are mapping an \emph{infinite} space (the
real interval) onto a \emph{finite} one ($2^L$ strings). $L$ therefore sets
the \emph{resolution}. A longer string gives finer accuracy but a larger
space and slower evolution, so resolution is a design trade-off, not a free
improvement.

\paragraph{Worked conversion.}
Take the interval $[0.25,\,1.88]$ and $L=4$, and decode $a=1010$.
\[
  \sum a_i2^{4-i}=8+2=10,\qquad
  \Gamma(a)=0.25+\frac{1.88-0.25}{2^4-1}\times 10
          =0.25+\frac{1.63}{15}\times 10 .
\]
Since $1.63/15=0.108\overline{6}$, we get $\Gamma(a)=0.25+1.0867=1.3367$ to
four decimal places. The step between adjacent codes is $0.1087$; going to
$L=10$ would shrink it to $1.63/1023\approx0.00159$.

\paragraph{Mutation in a real vector.}
Flipping is meaningless here, so mutation must \emph{generate a new value}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Uniform (random resetting).} Replace $x_i$ by a value drawn
      uniformly from $[\mathrm{LB},\mathrm{UB}]$. This is the direct analogue
      of a bit flip or an integer reset.
\item \textbf{Gaussian (non-uniform).} Perturb the current value,
      \[
        x_i'=x_i+N(0,\sigma),
      \]
      where $\sigma$, the standard deviation, acts as the \emph{mutation step
      size}. Small $\sigma$ gives a fine local move; large $\sigma$
      approaches a random reset.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Self-adaptive mutation, and why the order matters.}
Rather than fixing $\sigma$ by hand, put it \emph{inside the chromosome}:
\[
  \langle x_1,\ldots,x_n,\ \sigma\rangle .
\]
The step size then evolves along with the solution, and the algorithm tunes
its own search scale. Two things must now be mutated, and the lecture insists
on a specific order:

\begin{center}
\textbf{Mutate $\sigma$ first, then use the new $\sigma'$ to mutate $x$.}
\end{center}

The rationale is worth reconstructing rather than memorising. Fitness is
evaluated on $x$, never on $\sigma$; $\sigma$ has no quality of its own. So
$\sigma$ can only be judged \emph{indirectly}, through the quality of the
offspring it produced. Mutating $\sigma$ first means the $x'$ that gets
evaluated is the one that $\sigma'$ actually generated, so a good fitness is
evidence that $\sigma'$ was a good step size. Do it the other way round and
the evaluated $x'$ owes nothing to $\sigma'$, so the fitness says nothing
about it. In short: $x'$ is the primary evaluation, $\sigma'$ is judged
secondarily through it, and that dependency dictates the order.

\paragraph{Crossover: arithmetic recombination.}
For real values the natural way to inherit from both parents is to
interpolate. Choose $\alpha\in[0,1]$ and set, for the genes being crossed,
\[
  z_i=\alpha x_i+(1-\alpha)y_i .
\]
The value of $\alpha$ may be held fixed (0.5 is the common default), varied
with the age of the population, or drawn at random each time. Three variants
differ only in \emph{how many genes} are treated this way:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Single arithmetic:} blend at one randomly chosen gene; all
      other genes are copied unchanged.
\item \textbf{Simple arithmetic:} choose a crossover point and blend every
      gene after it.
\item \textbf{Whole arithmetic:} blend every gene.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Worked example.} Let
$x=(0.3,\,0.7,\,0.8,\,0.2)$ and $y=(0.5,\,0.1,\,0.2,\,0.9)$.

\emph{Whole arithmetic, $\alpha=0.3$:}
\[
  z^{(1)}=0.3x+0.7y=(0.44,\,0.28,\,0.38,\,0.69),\qquad
  z^{(2)}=0.7x+0.3y=(0.36,\,0.52,\,0.62,\,0.41).
\]
A useful check: $z^{(1)}_i+z^{(2)}_i=x_i+y_i$ for every $i$, here
$(0.8,0.8,1.0,1.1)$.

\emph{Whole arithmetic, $\alpha=0.5$:} both children equal
$(0.40,\,0.40,\,0.50,\,0.55)$. This is a trap worth noticing:
at $\alpha=0.5$ the two arithmetic children are identical, so the operator
has produced one point, not two.

\emph{Simple arithmetic after gene 2, $\alpha=0.5$:}
$z^{(1)}=(0.3,\,0.7,\,0.50,\,0.55)$ and $z^{(2)}=(0.5,\,0.1,\,0.50,\,0.55)$.

\paragraph{The limitation of arithmetic crossover.}
Ask what values an arithmetic child can take. Since
$z_i=\alpha x_i+(1-\alpha)y_i$ with $\alpha\in[0,1]$, the child always lies
\emph{between} the parents, whatever $\alpha$ is. Over a whole chromosome, the
offspring are confined to the bounding box spanned by the two parents. The
operator can therefore contract the population but never expand it, which is
a real cause of premature convergence.

\paragraph{Blend crossover (BLX-$\alpha$).}
The fix is to let the offspring reach slightly \emph{outside} the parents.
Assume for a gene that $x_i<y_i$, so the parental distance
\[
  d_i=y_i-x_i
\]
is positive. Sample the child value uniformly from the widened interval
\[
  z_i\in\left[\,x_i-\alpha d_i,\ \ y_i+\alpha d_i\,\right].
\]
The square brackets denote the sampling range, and $\alpha$ controls how far
beyond the parents the search may reach; it can be made adaptive as the run
progresses.

\emph{Comparison at one gene.} With $x_i=0.3$, $y_i=0.7$, we have $d_i=0.4$.
Arithmetic crossover can only produce a value in $[0.3,0.7]$. Blend crossover
with $\alpha=0.5$ samples from $[0.3-0.2,\ 0.7+0.2]=[0.1,\,0.9]$ -- twice the
width, and genuinely explorative.

\paragraph{Multi-parent recombination.}
Nothing in the mathematics requires exactly two parents; the restriction is
inherited from the biological metaphor, not from the problem. The lecture's
reminder is that the goal is to solve the problem, and nature is an
inspiration rather than a constraint. Two extensions are named:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Diagonal crossover (``simple type 1'').} With $p$ parents,
      choose $p-1$ crossover points and build each child by taking segments
      \emph{diagonally} across the parents, wrapping around to the first
      parent at the end. This generalises one-point crossover to $p$ parents
      and yields $p$ children.
\item \textbf{Multi-parent arithmetic.} Extend the arithmetic blend to a
      weighted average of $p$ parents.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Permutation representation}

\paragraph{When to use it.}
A large family of problems is about \emph{ordering} or \emph{sequencing}
rather than about values. The travelling-salesman problem asks for a sequence
visiting every city once and only once, at minimum total distance;
$N$-queens, scheduling, and assembly ordering have the same character. Here a
chromosome is a permutation: each of the values $1,\ldots,L$ appears exactly
once.

Two payoffs from the lecture:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{TSP with 30 cities:} encode as a permutation of $1,\ldots,30$,
      where the gene \emph{order} is the travel order. Finding the optimum is
      NP-hard, which is exactly why a metaheuristic is wanted.
\item \textbf{$N$-queens:} a permutation of $1,\ldots,8$ automatically places
      one queen per row and one per column, so those two constraints cannot
      be violated. Only the diagonal conflicts remain to be optimised. This
      is representation doing the work that a penalty term would otherwise
      have to do.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The governing rule.}
Every operator must return a valid permutation. Ordinary one-point crossover
does not: it duplicates some values and omits others. Permutation operators
are therefore built specifically to preserve the ``once and only once''
property.

\paragraph{Mutation on a permutation.} Mutation is still unary. Four standard
forms, from most to least disruptive of adjacency:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Swap.} Pick two positions and exchange their values. Note the
      logic: you cannot simply overwrite one gene with a new value, because
      that value already occurs elsewhere -- so you must also move the one it
      displaced.
\item \textbf{Insert.} Pick two genes and move one so that it sits next to
      the other, shifting the rest along.
\item \textbf{Scramble.} Pick a range of genes and shuffle them randomly.
\item \textbf{Inversion.} Pick a range and reverse it. This is the gentlest:
      it changes the order but \emph{preserves most adjacency information},
      because only the two boundary links are broken.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Crossover on a permutation.}
Four operators are covered, and all four are illustrated below with the same
pair of parents used in the lecture:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
$P_1$ & \texttt{1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5\ 6\ 7\ 8\ 9}\\
$P_2$ & \texttt{9\ 3\ 7\ 8\ 2\ 6\ 5\ 1\ 4}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent In each case the chosen segment is positions 4--7, so $P_1$
contributes \texttt{4\ 5\ 6\ 7} and $P_2$ contributes \texttt{8\ 2\ 6\ 5}.

\paragraph{1. Order-1 crossover.}
Copy the segment from $P_1$ into the child at the same positions. Then fill
the remaining positions from $P_2$, taking $P_2$'s values \emph{in $P_2$'s
order}, starting immediately after the segment and wrapping around, skipping
any value already present.

Starting from position 8, $P_2$ reads $1,4,9,3,7,8,2,6,5$. Discarding those
already in the child ($4,5,6,7$) leaves $1,9,3,8,2$, which fill positions
$8,9,1,2,3$:
\[
  \text{child}=\texttt{3\ 8\ 2\ | 4\ 5\ 6\ 7 |\ 1\ 9}.
\]
One parent supplies explicit values; the other supplies relative order.

\paragraph{2. Partially mapped crossover (PMX).}
PMX is the most commonly used permutation crossover. Steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Copy a random segment from $P_1$ into the child at the same positions.
\item Look at the values in the \emph{same} segment of $P_2$. Some already
      appear in the copied segment -- these are the \emph{partially matched}
      ones and need no action. For each value $v$ that does not, follow the
      mapping chain to find its home: look at the value $w$ that $P_1$ holds
      at $v$'s position, find where $w$ sits in $P_2$, and place $v$ there if
      the slot is free; if not, repeat with $w$.
\item Fill every remaining position with $P_2$'s value at that position.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Worked, child 1.} Segment gives \texttt{-\ -\ -\ 4\ 5\ 6\ 7\ -\ -}.
$P_2$'s segment is $8,2,6,5$; the $6$ and $5$ are already present, so only
$8$ and $2$ must be placed.
\begin{itemize}
\item $2$ sits at position 5; $P_1$ has $5$ there; $5$ sits at position 7 in
      $P_2$; position 7 is occupied by $7$; $7$ sits at position 3 in $P_2$;
      position 3 is free, so $2$ goes to position 3.
\item $8$ sits at position 4; $P_1$ has $4$ there; $4$ sits at position 9 in
      $P_2$; position 9 is free, so $8$ goes to position 9.
\end{itemize}
Filling positions 1, 2 and 8 from $P_2$ gives $9,3,1$:
\[
  \text{child}_1=\texttt{9\ 3\ 2\ | 4\ 5\ 6\ 7 |\ 1\ 8}.
\]

\emph{Worked, child 2} (roles reversed; $P_2$ donates \texttt{8\ 2\ 6\ 5}).
The unmatched values are now $4$ and $7$: $4$ maps via $8$ to position 8, and
$7$ maps via $5$, then via $2$, to position 2. Filling from $P_1$ gives
\[
  \text{child}_2=\texttt{1\ 7\ 3\ | 8\ 2\ 6\ 5 |\ 4\ 9}.
\]
Always finish by checking that each of $1,\ldots,9$ appears exactly once.

\paragraph{3. Cycle crossover.}
This operator asks a different question: which genes are locked together by
the two parents? Start at a position, take $P_1$'s value; find where $P_2$'s
value at that position sits in $P_1$; move there; repeat until you return to
the start. That closed loop is a \emph{cycle}.

For our parents:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cycle 1:} $1\to9\to4\to8\to1$, i.e.\ positions $\{1,4,8,9\}$.
\item \textbf{Cycle 2:} $2\to3\to7\to5\to2$, i.e.\ positions $\{2,3,5,7\}$.
\item \textbf{Cycle 3:} position $\{6\}$ alone, since both parents hold $6$
      there.
\end{itemize}
Now build offspring by taking whole cycles alternately from the two parents:
\[
  \text{child}_1=\texttt{1\ 3\ 7\ 4\ 2\ 6\ 5\ 8\ 9},\qquad
  \text{child}_2=\texttt{9\ 2\ 3\ 8\ 5\ 6\ 7\ 1\ 4}.
\]
Because cycles are exchanged whole, validity is automatic, and every gene
keeps a position it held in one of its parents.

\paragraph{4. Edge recombination.}
The three operators above all manipulate \emph{gene values and positions}.
For a routing problem such as TSP, what actually determines the cost is not
where a city sits in the string but \emph{which cities it is adjacent to} --
the \emph{edges}. Edge recombination therefore inherits edges.

\emph{Step 1: build the edge table.} For each city, list its neighbours in
both parents. Treat the tour as a closed circuit, so the last city is
adjacent to the first. Mark with $+$ any neighbour that appears in
\emph{both} parents (a common edge).
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}cl@{\qquad}cl@{}}
\toprule
City & Neighbours & City & Neighbours\\
\midrule
1 & 2, 9, 5, 4      & 6 & 5$^+$, 7, 2\\
2 & 1, 3, 8, 6      & 7 & 6, 8$^+$, 3\\
3 & 2, 4, 9, 7      & 8 & 7$^+$, 9, 2\\
4 & 3, 5, 1, 9      & 9 & 8, 1, 4, 3\\
5 & 4, 6$^+$, 1     &   & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\emph{Step 2: construct the child} one city at a time. Pick a random start;
delete that city from every list; then choose the next city by these rules,
in order:
\begin{enumerate}
\item if a common edge ($+$) is available from the current city, take it;
\item otherwise take the candidate whose own neighbour list is
      \emph{shortest};
\item break ties at random;
\item if the current city's list is empty, jump to any unused city at random.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Worked run, starting at city 1.} From $1$ the candidates are
$2,5,4,9$ with remaining list lengths $3,2,3,3$, so $5$ is shortest: take
$5$. From $5$ the common edge $6^+$ is available: take $6$. From $6$ the
candidates $7$ and $2$ both have two entries, so break the tie at random;
take $7$. From $7$ the common edge $8^+$ is available: take $8$. From $8$ the
candidates are $9$ (two entries) and $2$ (one entry): take $2$. From $2$ only
$3$ remains. From $3$ the candidates $4$ and $9$ each have one entry; break
the tie at random and take $4$, then $9$. The child is
\[
  \texttt{1\ 5\ 6\ 7\ 8\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 9},
\]
a valid permutation built from inherited edges. Note the two random
tie-breaks: a different run would legitimately give a different child.

\emph{Why it is called constructive.} Unlike the other operators, edge
recombination does not copy blocks -- it \emph{builds} the solution
one element at a time under a rule, which is how deterministic constructive
methods work too.

\subsection{Tree representation}

\paragraph{When to use it.}
Some objects are naturally trees rather than strings: an arithmetic
expression, a logical formula, a computer program, a finite-state machine.
Evolving trees is the field of \textbf{genetic programming}, and the object
being evolved is a \emph{structure}, not a list of values.

\paragraph{Structure.}
A tree is built from two declared sets:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \textbf{function set} (internal, non-terminal nodes): the operators,
      e.g.\ $\{+,-,\times,\div\}$ or $\{\wedge,\vee,\neg,\rightarrow\}$;
\item the \textbf{terminal set} (leaves): the variables and constants, e.g.\
      $\{x,y,2,3,\pi\}$.
\end{itemize}
Defining these two sets is the representation decision. Reading the tree is
just operator precedence: the root is the operation applied last.

\emph{Example.} For $2\pi+\bigl((x+3)-\tfrac{y}{5+1}\bigr)$, the root is
$+$; its left subtree is $\times$ with leaves $2$ and $\pi$; its right
subtree is $-$, whose left subtree is $+$ over $x$ and $3$, and whose right
subtree is $\div$ over $y$ and a $+$ node over $5$ and $1$. A logical formula
such as $(x\wedge \text{true})\rightarrow(\neg x\vee y)$ decomposes the same
way, with logical connectives as the function set.

\paragraph{Variation on trees.}
Because the chromosome is a structure, the operators must vary the structure
rather than a value.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mutation:} choose a node at random and replace the subtree
      rooted there with a newly generated subtree -- possibly just a single
      terminal.
\item \textbf{Recombination:} choose a node in each parent and \emph{exchange
      the two subtrees}. This is the direct analogue of exchanging substrings.
\end{itemize}
A consequence worth noticing: tree chromosomes are variable in size and
non-linear, so unlike every other representation in this course, two
chromosomes need not have the same length.

\section{Worked archetypes}

\subsection{Archetype 1: classify the problem before choosing an algorithm}
\textbf{Question.} A factory knows a simulator $M$ that predicts energy use $y$ for a machine setting $x$. Classify each task:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Find a setting that minimises energy while meeting production constraints.
\item Predict the energy used by one specified setting.
\item Infer a predictive relationship from logged settings and measured energy.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Construction.} Write $y=M(x)$ and ask which object is unknown.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $M$ is known and the desired low-energy outcome is specified, but $x$ is unknown: \textbf{optimisation}. The search space contains encodable settings, the feasible set satisfies production constraints, and the objective is predicted energy.
\item $x$ and $M$ are known, while $y$ is requested: \textbf{simulation}.
\item Input--output examples are known, while $M$ is unknown: \textbf{modelling/learning}.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Trap.} The use of a computer model does not automatically make a task simulation. Classification depends on what is unknown.

\subsection{Archetype 2: reason correctly about P, NP, NP-complete, and NP-hard}
\textbf{Question.} Assess the claim that NP means non-polynomial and therefore an NP-hard optimisation problem cannot be checked efficiently.

\textbf{Answer.} The claim combines two errors.
\begin{enumerate}
\item NP means \emph{nondeterministic polynomial time}, commonly characterised by polynomial-time verification of a proposed yes-certificate for a decision problem. It does not mean that the problem is proved to require non-polynomial time.
\item NP-hard is a hardness relation. An NP-hard problem need not itself be a decision problem or belong to NP. For an optimisation problem, checking that a candidate is feasible and calculating its objective may be easy even when proving that it is globally optimal is hard.
\end{enumerate}
If a decision problem is both in NP and NP-hard, it is NP-complete. Every problem in P is also in NP. Whether $\mathbf P=\mathbf{NP}$ remains unresolved.

\subsection{Archetype 3: trace the evolutionary algorithm loop}
\textbf{Question.} A population contains four binary strings. Describe one generation without confusing parent selection and survivor selection.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Represent and initialise:} choose an encoding and create the initial population.
\item \textbf{Evaluate:} decode each genotype, calculate its objective, and convert it to the fitness used by selection.
\item \textbf{Select parents:} sample candidates for reproduction. Better fitness normally means greater probability, not certainty.
\item \textbf{Recombine:} exchange genetic material between selected parents to make offspring.
\item \textbf{Mutate:} randomly change genes, supplying new variation.
\item \textbf{Evaluate offspring:} decode and score the new candidates.
\item \textbf{Select survivors:} decide which parents and/or offspring form the next population.
\item \textbf{Terminate or repeat:} stop at a target, budget, or stagnation condition; otherwise begin the next generation.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Trap.} Parent selection answers who reproduces; survivor selection answers who remains. They can use different rules.

\subsection{Archetype 4: represent and score 8-queens}
For $N=4$, let $q_i$ be the row of the queen in column $i$. The permutation $q=(2,4,1,3)$ automatically gives one queen in each row and column. For every pair $i<j$, compare $|q_i-q_j|$ with $|i-j|$:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccccc}
(i,j)&(1,2)&(1,3)&(1,4)&(2,3)&(2,4)&(3,4)\\
\hline
|q_i-q_j|&2&1&1&3&1&2\\
|i-j|&1&2&3&1&2&1
\end{array}
\]
No pair is equal, so $C=0$ and $f(q)=\binom42-C=6$. A swap mutation exchanging the last two positions gives $q'=(2,4,3,1)$. It remains a valid permutation, but pair $(2,3)$ conflicts, so $C=1$ and $f(q')=5$.

\textbf{Operator lesson.} Swap mutation preserves the permutation. Recombination must likewise preserve it or be followed by repair.

\subsection{Archetype 5: calculate one complete SGA generation}
Maximise $f(x)=x^2$ for an unsigned four-bit integer. Start with:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{chromosome}&x&f(x)&p_i=f_i/\sum f_j\\
\hline
0011&3&9&9/311=0.0289\\
0101&5&25&25/311=0.0804\\
1001&9&81&81/311=0.2605\\
1110&14&196&196/311=0.6302
\end{array}
\]
The fitness sum is $311$. Suppose roulette draws produce the mating pool
$[1110,1001,1110,0101]$. Pair adjacent parents and cross after bit 2:
\[
\begin{aligned}
11|10,\;10|01&\longrightarrow 11|01=1101,\quad 10|10=1010,\\
11|10,\;01|01&\longrightarrow 11|01=1101,\quad 01|10=0110.
\end{aligned}
\]
Suppose mutation flips the final bit of the fourth offspring only, so $0110\to0111$. With generational replacement the next population is
$[1101,1010,1101,0111]$, decoding to $[13,10,13,7]$, with fitnesses $[169,100,169,49]$. Its total fitness is $487$ and its best fitness is $169$.

\textbf{Checks.} The roulette outcomes and mutation event are sampled events, not guarantees. Decode after variation, recompute fitness, and use the correct generation's fitness sum.


\subsection{Archetype 6: choose a representation and legal operators}
\textbf{Task.} A courier must visit 12 drop-off points once each and return to
the depot at minimum distance. State a suitable representation, one mutation
and one crossover operator, and justify both.

\textbf{Answer.} The unknown is an \emph{order}, so use a permutation of
$1,\ldots,12$ where gene position gives visit order; the genotype space is the
$12!$ orderings. Mutation: inversion, which reverses a segment and so changes
the order while preserving most adjacency (i.e.\ most edges), which is what
the distance objective actually depends on. Crossover: edge recombination,
because the cost is a sum over edges and this operator inherits edges rather
than positions. Ordinary one-point crossover is rejected: it would repeat some
drop-offs and omit others, producing an invalid tour.

\textbf{Marking logic.} Name the representation \emph{and} the genotype space;
give one legal operator of each kind; justify against the objective; state
explicitly why a binary-style crossover is invalid here.

\subsection{Archetype 7: real-valued crossover arithmetic}
\textbf{Task.} Parents $x=(0.3,0.7,0.8,0.2)$ and $y=(0.5,0.1,0.2,0.9)$.
(a) Apply whole arithmetic crossover with $\alpha=0.3$. (b) State the range of
values the first gene of any arithmetic child can take. (c) Give that range
under blend crossover with $\alpha=0.5$.

\textbf{Answer.}
(a) $z^{(1)}=0.3x+0.7y=(0.44,0.28,0.38,0.69)$ and
$z^{(2)}=0.7x+0.3y=(0.36,0.52,0.62,0.41)$. Check:
$z^{(1)}+z^{(2)}=x+y=(0.8,0.8,1.0,1.1)$.
(b) $z_1=\alpha(0.3)+(1-\alpha)(0.5)$ with $\alpha\in[0,1]$, so
$z_1\in[0.3,0.5]$: strictly between the parents.
(c) With $x_1=0.3<y_1=0.5$, $d_1=0.2$, so the child is sampled from
$[0.3-0.5(0.2),\,0.5+0.5(0.2)]=[0.2,\,0.6]$.

\textbf{Marking logic.} Correct weighted averages; the sum check; an explicit
statement that arithmetic offspring cannot leave the parental interval while
blend offspring can.

\subsection{Archetype 8: permutation crossover under exam conditions}
\textbf{Task.} With $P_1=\texttt{1\,2\,3\,4\,5\,6\,7\,8\,9}$ and
$P_2=\texttt{9\,3\,7\,8\,2\,6\,5\,1\,4}$ and segment positions 4--7, produce
one PMX child and one cycle-crossover child, and verify validity.

\textbf{Answer.} PMX (segment from $P_1$):
$\texttt{9\,3\,2\,4\,5\,6\,7\,1\,8}$ --- the unmatched values $2$ and $8$ are
relocated by following the mapping chains $2\to5\to7$ and $8\to4$, and the
remaining positions are filled from $P_2$. Cycle crossover: the cycles are
$\{1,4,8,9\}$, $\{2,3,5,7\}$ and $\{6\}$; taking cycles alternately gives
$\texttt{1\,3\,7\,4\,2\,6\,5\,8\,9}$. Both contain each of $1,\ldots,9$
exactly once.

\textbf{Marking logic.} Show the mapping chain or the cycle decomposition, not
just the final string; finish with an explicit validity check. A child with a
repeated value scores zero regardless of method.

\subsection{Archetype 9: binary encoding of a real variable}
\textbf{Task.} A variable lies in $[0.25,1.88]$ and is encoded in $L=4$ bits.
Decode $1010$, state the resolution, and state the effect of moving to
$L=10$.

\textbf{Answer.} $\sum a_i2^{4-i}=8+2=10$, so
\[
  \Gamma=0.25+\frac{1.88-0.25}{2^4-1}(10)=0.25+\frac{1.63}{15}(10)=1.3367 .
\]
The resolution is $1.63/15=0.1087$ per code step. At $L=10$ it becomes
$1.63/1023\approx0.00159$: finer accuracy, at the cost of a genotype space of
$2^{10}$ instead of $2^{4}$ and correspondingly slower evolution.

\textbf{Marking logic.} Use $2^L-1$, not $2^L$, in the denominator; state that
$L$ buys resolution and charges search cost; note that a real interval is
infinite while the code space is finite.

\section{Solutions to the progressive drills}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{D\arabic*.}]
\item (i) Modelling/learning; (ii) optimisation; (iii) simulation.
\item $S$ is every encodable route; $\mathcal F$ is the subset obeying the maximum-distance rule; minimise total fuel use $f$.
\item $8!=40320$. Factorial growth makes exhaustive enumeration rapidly expensive.
\item NP is polynomial-time verification of yes-certificates for decision problems, not non-polynomial. NP-hard problems need not be in NP or be decision problems. NP-complete problems are both in NP and NP-hard.
\item
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{initialise}&\to\text{evaluate population}\to\text{select parents}
\to\text{recombine}\to\text{mutate}\\
&\to\text{evaluate offspring}\to\text{select survivors}
\to\text{test termination}.
\end{aligned}
\]
\item Total $10$; probabilities $0.2,0.3,0.5$, summing to $1$.
\item It can destroy diversity and cause premature convergence around a local optimum.
\item No conflicting pair, so $C=0$ and $f=6$.
\item Children $(1,2,2,1)$ and $(4,3,3,4)$ repeat and omit rows; use a permutation-preserving crossover or repair.
\item $10110_2=16+4+2=22$.
\item $x=10+\frac{22}{31}(10)=17.0968$ approximately.
\item Expected flips $=6(8)(0.005)=0.24$; no-flip probability $=0.995^{48}\approx0.7862$.
\item Darwinian evolution (limited resources cause competition; selection
retains the fitter; reproduction rewards survival) and genetics (DNA as an
encoded description whose expression is the phenotype). A metaheuristic is a
general, higher-level search scheme built from rules of thumb --- here rules
drawn from nature rather than from problem-specific human knowledge.
\item Performance against all problems: random search flat and low; a
custom method a narrow tall spike; a plain EA broad and moderately high; each
added increment of domain knowledge raises the peak and narrows the range.
Equal areas imply equal average performance over all problems, hence no
universally best optimiser. It does \emph{not} claim that algorithm choice is
irrelevant on a particular real problem.
\item Still $1{:}1$. Crossover only redistributes existing alleles between
strings, so it cannot restore a lost value; this is why mutation, which
introduces new information, is necessary.
\item Creep (add a small $\pm$ step with probability $p$) and random
resetting. Creep moves individuals in small steps, so the population drifts
together and converges prematurely.
\item $\sum a_i2^{4-i}=10$, so
$\Gamma=0.25+\frac{1.63}{15}(10)=1.3367$; the step size is
$1.63/15=0.1087$.
\item Mutate $\sigma$ first. Fitness is evaluated on $x$ only, so $\sigma'$
can only be judged through the quality of the $x'$ it generated; mutating
$\sigma$ first makes that dependency hold.
\item Both children are $(0.40,0.40)$. At $\alpha=0.5$ the two arithmetic
children are identical, so the operator returns one distinct point instead of
two.
\item $\texttt{3\,8\,2\,4\,5\,6\,7\,1\,9}$: segment $4\,5\,6\,7$ is kept, and
$P_2$'s remaining values in order from position 8 are $1,9,3,8,2$, filling
positions $8,9,1,2,3$.
\end{enumerate}
