1 · Soft-margin SVM: w, b, margin, slack Exam focus Mock B −11

Start here: the picture

The SVM draws the boundary \(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=0\) and two margin lines \(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=\pm1\). The margin width is \(2/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\). A soft margin lets some points cross their margin line, and pays \(C\) for each unit of crossing. Training gives you one number \(\alpha_i\) per sample. The exam hands you the \(\alpha_i\), so your job is bookkeeping, not optimisation.

\(\mathbf x_i,\ d_i\)sample \(i\) and its label, \(+1\) or \(-1\)
\(\alpha_i\)Lagrange multiplier, \(0\le\alpha_i\le C\). \(\alpha_i>0\) means sample \(i\) is a support vector
\(C\)penalty weight: large \(C\) gives a narrow margin and few violations; small \(C\) gives a wide margin
\(\xi_i\)slack: how far sample \(i\) is on the wrong side of its own margin line, in units of \(1/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\)

Why each formula holds (SVM slides pp. 34–40):

  1. \(\mathbf w=\sum_i\alpha_i d_i\mathbf x_i\). Setting the derivative of the Lagrangian with respect to \(\mathbf w\) to zero gives this. Only \(\alpha_i>0\) terms contribute, so only support vectors shape the boundary.
  2. \(\sum_i\alpha_i d_i=0\). This comes from the derivative with respect to \(b\). Check it first: if it fails, you misread the table.
  3. The bias uses only \(0\lt\alpha_i\lt C\). KKT says: if \(\alpha_i\lt C\) then \(\xi_i=0\), so the point sits exactly on its margin line, \(d_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)=1\). Multiply both sides by \(d_i\) (because \(d_i^2=1\)) to get \(b=d_i-\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i\). If \(\alpha_i=C\), the point may be inside the margin, the equation \(=1\) is false, and it gives a wrong \(b\). Average over the usable points.
  4. Slack: \(\xi_i=\max\big(0,\ 1-d_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\big)\). \(0\lt\xi\le1\) means inside the margin but correctly classified; \(\xi>1\) means misclassified (p. 34).
  5. To classify a new point, take the sign of \(g(\mathbf x)=\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b\). Its distance to the boundary is \(|g|/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\). Compare it with the half-margin \(1/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\): smaller means inside the margin, so low confidence.

2 · Multinomial naive Bayes Exam focus Mock B −8

Start here: one bag of words per class

Pour every training word of a class into one bag. \(P(\text{word}\mid\text{class})\) is the share of that bag taken by the word. To classify a message, multiply the prior by the probability of each token of the message. A word that appears twice is multiplied in twice: that is what “multinomial” means (Bayes slides pp. 64–68).

Laplace smoothing puts one extra copy of every vocabulary word into every bag, so no word has probability 0. One zero would veto a class however strongly the other words support it.

\[ P(w\mid c)=\frac{\text{count of }w\text{ in class }c+1}{\text{total words in class }c+d}, \qquad d=\text{number of distinct words in all training messages}. \]

The trap you fell into: \(P(\text{class}\mid\text{word})\) (“of the messages containing free, how many are spam?”) is not used anywhere. Every number you compute is a share of a class’s bag.

\(n_c\)total word tokens in class \(c\), repeats included
\(d\)vocabulary size of the whole training set, not of one class and not of the test message
score\((c)\)\(P(c)\times\prod_{\text{tokens }t}P(t\mid c)\). Compare the scores; normalising is not needed

Worked example

Pos: “good great good”, “great fun”. Neg: “bad boring”, “bad good bad”. Classify “good good bad fun”.
  1. Vocabulary {good, great, fun, bad, boring}: \(d=5\). Pos bag: good 2, great 2, fun 1, so \(n=5\). Neg bag: bad 3, boring 1, good 1, so \(n=5\). Priors \(1/2,1/2\).
  2. Pos: good \(3/10\), bad \(1/10\), fun \(2/10\). Neg: good \(2/10\), bad \(4/10\), fun \(1/10\).
  3. Pos score \(=0.5\cdot0.3\cdot0.3\cdot0.1\cdot0.2=0.0009\). Neg score \(=0.5\cdot0.2\cdot0.2\cdot0.4\cdot0.1=0.0008\). Pos.
  4. Counting “good” once would give \(0.003\) vs \(0.004\), which is Neg. The repeated token decides the answer.

3 · Gaussian naive Bayes Mock B −8

Start here: one bell curve per feature

For continuous features, naive Bayes fits one 1-D bell curve per feature per class and multiplies them. That is the “naive” independence assumption, and it is why no covariance matrix and no inverse appear (Bayes slides pp. 53–56):

\[ p(x_k\mid\omega_j)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_{jk}}\exp\!\Big[-\frac12\Big(\frac{x_k-\mu_{jk}}{\sigma_{jk}}\Big)^2\Big],\qquad p(\mathbf x\mid\omega_j)=\prod_k p(x_k\mid\omega_j). \]

The slide writes \(\sigma\), the standard deviation. Papers usually give the variance \(\sigma^2\), so take its square root for the front factor, and use \(\sigma^2\) directly in the exponent: \(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\).

Decision: the class with the largest \(P(\omega_j)\,p(\mathbf x\mid\omega_j)\). In logs, dropping the shared \(\ln\sqrt{2\pi}\):

\[ g_j=\ln P(\omega_j)-\tfrac12\sum_k\ln\sigma_{jk}^2-\tfrac12\sum_k\frac{(x_k-\mu_{jk})^2}{\sigma_{jk}^2}. \]

Posterior (two classes): \(P(\omega_1\mid\mathbf x)=\dfrac{P_1p_1}{P_1p_1+P_2p_2}=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{\,g_2-g_1}}\).

What you wrote in Mock B was the full multivariate Gaussian with \(\mathbf C\) and \(\mathbf C^{-1}\) swapped. That formula belongs to the Gaussian Bayes rule (Mock A Q2a), where the front factor has \(|\mathbf C|^{1/2}\) and the exponent is \(-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\mathbf m)^T\mathbf C^{-1}(\mathbf x-\mathbf m)\). With a diagonal \(\mathbf C\) it reduces to the product above.

  1. \(\omega_1\): feature 1 gives \(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-1/2}=0.2420\); feature 2 gives \(\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi}}e^{-1/4}=0.2197\). Product \(0.0532\).
  2. \(\omega_2\): both features give \(\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi}}e^{-1/4}=0.2197\). Product \(0.0483\).
  3. \(g_1=\ln0.7-\tfrac12\ln2-\tfrac12(1+0.5)=-1.4532\); \(g_2=\ln0.3-\tfrac12(2\ln2)-\tfrac12(0.5+0.5)=-2.3971\). So \(\omega_1\).
  4. \(P(\omega_1\mid\mathbf x)=1/(1+e^{-0.9439})=0.7199\).

4 · Cluster quality indices Mock B −13

Start here: tight inside, far apart

A good partition has tight clusters (small spread inside each) that are far apart. Each index is a ratio of these two ideas, and they differ in which distances they use and in which direction is better (clustering-evaluation slides pp. 8–18).

Dunn ↑ better\(\dfrac{\min d_{pq}}{\max\delta_p}\). \(d_{pq}\) = closest pair of points in two different clusters; \(\delta_p\) = cluster diameter, its farthest pair of points. Points only, no centroids.
Davies–Bouldin ↓ better\(\delta_i=\sqrt{\tfrac1{n_i}\sum\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf m_i\rVert^2}\) (RMS distance to the centroid, not a sum); \(S_{ij}=(\delta_i+\delta_j)/d(\mathbf m_i,\mathbf m_j)\); \(DB=\tfrac1K\sum_i\max_{j\ne i}S_{ij}\). With 2 clusters \(DB=S_{12}\).
Calinski–Harabasz ↑ better\(CH=\dfrac{\sum_i n_i\lVert\mathbf m_i-\mathbf m\rVert^2}{\sum_i\sum_{\mathbf x\in D_i}\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf m_i\rVert^2}\), with \(\mathbf m\) the mean of all samples. This is the course form: it has no \((N-K)/(K-1)\) factor.
Rand index (0–1)Compares two partitions. Go through all \(\binom N2\) pairs: \(N_s\) = together in both, \(N_d\) = apart in both. \(RI=(N_s+N_d)/\binom N2\).

6 · Fisher’s criterion and how to judge confidence Exam focus Mock B −4

Start here: project, then compare gap with spread

Fisher LDA projects every sample onto a line: \(y=\mathbf w^T\mathbf x\). A good line puts the two projected means far apart and keeps each projected class narrow (LDA slides pp. 17–19, 24):

\[ J(\mathbf w)=\frac{(\tilde m_1-\tilde m_2)^2}{\tilde s_1^2+\tilde s_2^2}=\frac{\mathbf w^T\mathbf S_B\mathbf w}{\mathbf w^T\mathbf S_W\mathbf w},\quad \mathbf S_B=(\mathbf m_1-\mathbf m_2)(\mathbf m_1-\mathbf m_2)^T,\quad \mathbf S_W=\mathbf S_1+\mathbf S_2,\ \ \mathbf S_i=\sum_{\mathbf x\in D_i}(\mathbf x-\mathbf m_i)(\mathbf x-\mathbf m_i)^T. \]

The numerator is the squared gap between the projected means; the denominator is the total projected spread. The \(\mathbf w\) that maximises \(J\) is \(\mathbf w\propto\mathbf S_W^{-1}(\mathbf m_1-\mathbf m_2)\), and \(w_0=-\mathbf w^T(\mathbf m_1+\mathbf m_2)/2\) puts the boundary at the midpoint.

Confidence: the distance is \(|g(\mathbf x)|/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\), always positive. “Less than 1” means nothing, because \(\mathbf w\) has no natural scale. Compare it with something on the same scale: the distance of a class mean from the boundary. Mock B: \(0.424\) against \(2.226\) for \(\mathbf m_1\), so the point is very close to the boundary and the confidence is low.
