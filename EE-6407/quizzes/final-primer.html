The final-prep protocol

The real paper is 3 h, closed book, with 4 questions of 25 marks: Q1 is GA, Q2–Q4 are ML (all four official EE6407 papers, 2024–26). This semester's quizzes ran harder than the intel, so the mocks are pitched above the past papers. The official papers are held back for the last two steps.

  1. Primer (this page): every examined topic, quick checks.
  2. Guided mock A: a full 4×25 paper broken into checked steps, with hints and worked lines.
  3. Mock B, cold: 3 h, handwritten. Graded against a rubric.
  4. Retrain: diagnose every lost mark, then do a fresh variant until it is right.
  5. Mock C, cold: a re-test on new numbers, including the weak spots from step 4.
  6. Hard mock H, cold: wider range (regression, metrics, feature selection, data prep, DBSCAN, Davies–Bouldin, average linkage) and nastier traps. Retrain after.
  7. Official 2025-26 S1, cold under exam conditions. Graded, then retrained.
  8. Official 2025-26 S2: the dress rehearsal, then a final re-test from the mistakes log.

The 2024-25 papers are the spare: they are reserved for extra retrain drills, and no mock reuses their numbers.

Q1 · Genetic algorithms

G1. Binary decoding and resolution 24-25 S1

An \(n\)-bit gene with decimal value \(D\) maps onto \([a,b]\) as \(x = a + D\,\dfrac{b-a}{2^n-1}\). All zeros gives \(a\) and all ones gives \(b\), so the step (resolution) is \(\dfrac{b-a}{2^n-1}\). To reach a precision \(\varepsilon\), choose the smallest \(n\) with \(\dfrac{b-a}{2^n-1}\le\varepsilon\). For several variables, split the chromosome into one field per variable and decode each separately. Limitation: values between grid points are unreachable, and neighbouring values can differ in many bits (Hamming cliffs; Gray coding helps). GA part 1, pp. 20 & 31

Example: 4 bits on \([0,3]\), gene 1010 → \(D=10\), \(x=0+10\cdot 3/15=2\).

G2. Real-coded crossover and validity 24-25 S2, 25-26 S1

Whole arithmetic: child \(= \alpha\mathbf x + (1-\alpha)\mathbf y\) on every gene, and the other child swaps \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\). Single arithmetic changes one gene \(k\) only; simple arithmetic keeps genes \(1..k\) and blends the rest. Blend (BLX-\(\alpha\)): with \(d_i=|x_i-y_i|\), sample \(z_i\) from \([\min_i-\alpha d_i,\ \max_i+\alpha d_i]\), so it can leave the parents' box. The exam defines any variant (flexible-\(\alpha\), quasi-blend) in the question and gives the random numbers, so read the stated formula and apply it literally. Validity: a convex combination keeps linear constraints such as \(\sum w_i=1\) and keeps every gene inside the parents' range. Blend-type operators do not, so check the result and repair it (clip to the bounds, then divide by the sum). GA part 2, slides 23–28

Example: \(\mathbf x=(0.6,0.4)\), \(\mathbf y=(0.2,0.8)\), \(\alpha=0.5\) → \((0.4,0.6)\), whose sum is still 1.
\(\mathbf x=(0.2,0.5,0.3)\), \(\mathbf y=(0.6,0.1,0.3)\), \(\alpha=0.25\). What is the first gene of \(\alpha\mathbf x+(1-\alpha)\mathbf y\)?

G3. Permutation coding and a problem-specific fitness 24-25 S2, 25-26 S2

For \(n\)-queens, gene \(i\) is the row of the queen in column \(i\). A permutation already rules out row and column clashes, so only diagonal clashes remain: \(C(Q)=\sum_{i<j}\big[\,|q_i-q_j| = j-i\,\big]\). The worst case is \(C_{\max}=\binom n2=\frac{n(n-1)}2\), so a fitness in \([0,1]\) is \(F=1-C/C_{\max}\) (1 means solved). The same recipe works for any permutation problem (tours, schedules): count the violations of each rule, weight them, then normalise by the worst case. GA part 1, pp. 28–29 (8-queens)

Example: \(\langle 2,4,1,3\rangle\) has no pair with \(|q_i-q_j|=j-i\), so \(C=0\) and \(F=1\).
What is the fitness \(1-C/C_{\max}\) of \(\langle 1,3,2,4\rangle\) (4 d.p.)?

G4. Concepts: exploration vs exploitation, problem classes 24-25 S2, 25-26 S1/S2

Exploration discovers promising areas of the search space; exploitation optimises inside one. Too much exploitation (high selection pressure, small mutation) leads to stagnation and premature convergence. The remedies are more mutation, diversity preservation, a larger population or restarts. In the black-box model, given the model and the output, find the input → optimisation; given inputs and outputs, find the model → modelling; given the model and the input, find the output → simulation. GA part 2, slides 11–12

Q2-type · Bayes and naive Bayes

M1. Gaussian Bayes decision rule 24-25 S1

Choose the class with the largest \(g_i(\mathbf x)=\ln P(\omega_i)-\tfrac12\ln|\mathbf C_i|-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\mathbf m_i)^T\mathbf C_i^{-1}(\mathbf x-\mathbf m_i)\). The \((2\pi)^{d/2}\) term is the same for every class, so it drops out. Priors come from class counts (for example 500/300 gives 0.625/0.375). Estimate \(\mathbf m\) as the sample mean and \(\mathbf C\) as the sample covariance. ML slides 3, pp. 10–15

M2. Naive Bayes: Bernoulli and multinomial 3 of 4 papers

Score \(P(\omega)\prod_j p(x_j\mid\omega)\) and pick the largest. Bernoulli (yes/no features): \(p(\text{Y}\mid\omega)\) = the fraction of class-\(\omega\) samples with Y, and use \(1-p\) when the test value is N. Multinomial (word counts): \(p(x_i\mid\omega)=\dfrac{\text{count}(x_i,\omega)+1}{\sum_k\text{count}(x_k,\omega)+d}\), where \(d\) is the number of unique words in all the training data (Laplace add-1). Multiply once per occurrence of the word in the test text. ML slides 3, pp. 56–68

Q3-type · Linear classifiers

M3. Fisher LDA 4 of 4 papers

\(\mathbf S_i=\sum_{\mathbf x\in D_i}(\mathbf x-\mathbf m_i)(\mathbf x-\mathbf m_i)^T\) (a scatter, so no division by \(n\)), \(\mathbf S_W=\mathbf S_1+\mathbf S_2\), \(\mathbf w=\mathbf S_W^{-1}(\mathbf m_1-\mathbf m_2)\), \(w_0=-\tfrac12\mathbf w^T(\mathbf m_1+\mathbf m_2)\), \(g(\mathbf x)=\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+w_0\). Decide \(\omega_1\) when \(g>0\). Distance to the boundary \(=|g(\mathbf x)|/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\): the larger it is, the more confident the decision. 2×2 inverse: \(\frac1{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}\). ML slides 4, pp. 24–27

\(\mathbf S_W=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf m_1=(4,2)\), \(\mathbf m_2=(1,1)\). What is \(w_1\) (4 d.p.)?
Then \(w_0=-2\). What is the distance of \(\mathbf x=(2,3)\) to the boundary (4 d.p.)?

M4. Linear SVM, hard and soft margin 3 of 4 papers

\(\mathbf w=\sum_i\alpha_i d_i\mathbf x_i\) with \(\sum\alpha_i d_i=0\). Bias: \(b=\dfrac1{N_s}\sum_{0<\alpha_i<C}\big[d_i-\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i\big]\), averaged only over the support vectors with \(0<\alpha_i<C\). Those lie exactly on the margin. Support vectors with \(\alpha_i=C\) are inside the margin or misclassified (\(\xi_i>0\)), and using them gives a wrong \(b\). Margin width \(=2/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\). Slack \(\xi_i=\max(0,1-d_i g(\mathbf x_i))\). A large \(C\) penalises slack heavily (narrow margin, fits the training data); a small \(C\) tolerates slack (wide margin). ML slides 5, pp. 9–40

M5. Decision trees: Gini and entropy 25-26 S1

This course's Gini is \(\tfrac12\big(1-\sum_k p_k^2\big)\), so the two-class maximum is 0.25, not 0.5. Entropy \(=-\sum p_k\log_2 p_k\). Score a split by the size-weighted child impurity, \(\sum_c \frac{n_c}{n}I_c\). The best root feature has the largest drop: parent impurity minus weighted child impurity. The paper says which measure to use. ML slides 6, pp. 13–14

Q4-type · Clustering

M6. Hierarchical agglomerative clustering 4 of 4 papers

Start with every sample as its own cluster and repeatedly merge the closest pair. Single linkage uses the minimum pairwise distance, complete the maximum, average the mean, and centroid the distance between the centroids. Write out the updated distance matrix after each merge, and draw the dendrogram with merge heights in proportion to the merge distance (the lecturer asks for both). ML slides 10, pp. 29–35

1-D: what is the complete-linkage distance between \(\{1,2\}\) and \(\{6,9\}\)?

M7. k-means by hand 3 of 4 papers

(1) Assign every point to its nearest centre. (2) Replace each centre by the mean of its points. Repeat until the assignments stop changing, and show a table of distances for each iteration. The objective is the within-cluster sum of squares. Choose K with the elbow of SSE against K, or with a silhouette or other index. k-means assumes compact, roughly spherical clusters, so it fails on irregular shapes (use DBSCAN or single linkage for those). ML slides 10, pp. 13–21

M8. Cluster validity indices 2 of 4 papers

Silhouette (slide convention): \(SC_i=\dfrac{b_i-a_i}{\max(a_i,b_i)}\), where \(a_i\) is the mean distance to the other samples in its own cluster and \(b_i\) is the minimum distance to any sample in another cluster. Many textbooks instead use the mean distance to the nearest cluster; follow the slide. Overall \(SC\) is the average of the \(SC_i\), in \([-1,1]\), and higher is better. Dunn: \(DI=\dfrac{\min_{p\ne q} d_{pq}}{\max_p\delta_p}\), where \(d_{pq}\) is the closest pair across two clusters and \(\delta_p\) is the cluster diameter (farthest pair inside it). Higher is better. Davies–Bouldin: \(\delta_i=\sqrt{\frac1{n_i}\sum\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf m_i\rVert^2}\), \(S_{ij}=\frac{\delta_i+\delta_j}{d(\mathbf m_i,\mathbf m_j)}\), \(DB=\frac1K\sum_i\max_{j\ne i}S_{ij}\). Lower is better. ML slides 11, pp. 3–12

1-D clusters \(\{0,1\}\) and \(\{4,6\}\). What is the Dunn index?

E1. Data preparation

Min–max: \(x'=\frac{x-\min}{\max-\min}\in[0,1]\), which is sensitive to outliers. Standardisation: \(z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\), giving mean 0 and SD 1. Use it for distance-based methods (k-means, k-NN, SVM) whenever features have different units. Missing values: drop the row, or impute with the mean/median (numeric) or the mode (categorical). E2. Evaluation metrics

Precision \(=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}\) (of the predicted positives, how many are right), recall \(=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}\) (of the actual positives, how many are found), \(F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}\). Emphasise recall when a miss is costly (disease screening) and precision when a false alarm is costly (spam filtering). ML slides 7, pp. 16–17

E3. Regression: OLS, ridge, lasso

OLS: \(\boldsymbol\theta=(X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf y\). Ridge (L2): \(\boldsymbol\theta=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^T\mathbf y\), which shrinks the coefficients towards zero and fixes an ill-conditioned \(X^TX\). Lasso (L1) can set coefficients exactly to zero, so it also selects variables. For one feature with no intercept, ridge gives \(\theta=\frac{\sum xy}{\sum x^2+\lambda}\). ML slides 8, pp. 14–16

\(\sum xy=20\), \(\sum x^2=8\), \(\lambda=2\). What is the ridge \(\theta\)?

E4. Curse of dimensionality and feature selection

With \(d\) large relative to \(N\), estimates (covariances, \(\mathbf S_W\)) become unreliable and the classifier overfits. Fisher LDA in particular fails once \(N \le d\), because \(\mathbf S_W\) is singular. The remedies are to reduce \(d\) (feature selection with SFS/SBS, or extraction such as PCA), to regularise (\(\mathbf S_W+\lambda I\)), or to collect more data. SFS starts empty and adds the best feature each step; SBS starts full and removes the worst. ML slides 9, pp. 19–20
