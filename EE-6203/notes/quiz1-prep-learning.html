Module 1

Feedback, computer control, and signal language

0%
In progress
After this module you can:
  • identify the reference, error, controller, plant, sensor, sampler, and hold;
  • separate design questions from analysis questions;
  • distinguish continuous-time, sampled-data, quantised, and digital signals.

1.1 Why feedback exists

A controller does not directly “control the output.” It changes an input to the plant, observes what the plant actually does, and corrects the difference. In unity negative feedback, the comparison is

\[e(t)=r(t)-y(t).\]

\(t\): time

The instant at which the signals are evaluated, usually measured in seconds.

\(r(t)\): reference

The desired output, or setpoint, at time \(t\). It answers: “What do we want?”

\(y(t)\): measured output

The plant's actual measured output at time \(t\). It answers: “What did we get?”

\(e(t)\): tracking error

The gap between the desired and measured outputs at time \(t\). It answers: “How far are we from the target?”

Read the equation aloud: “error now equals desired value now minus measured value now.” The signals \(r\), \(y\), and \(e\) have the same physical unit. For example, if \(r=22^\circ\mathrm C\) and \(y=20.5^\circ\mathrm C\), then \(e=1.5^\circ\mathrm C\).

The minus sign matters. If the output becomes too large, the error becomes smaller or negative, encouraging the controller to reduce its command. That correction mechanism is the heart of negative feedback.

Design

Choose a controller structure and parameters so that the closed loop is expected to meet requirements.

Analysis

Given a controller, determine stability, transient behaviour, final error, robustness, and effort.

1.2 Why a digital controller creates a mixed system

The plant is usually continuous: motors, temperatures, voltages, and positions evolve between samples. The computer sees only numbers at \(t=kT\). A practical chain is

\[r(t)\rightarrow\text{ADC/sampler}\rightarrow e[k]\rightarrow\text{computer}\rightarrow\text{DAC+ZOH}\rightarrow u(t)\rightarrow G_p(s).\]

The sampler converts a continuous signal into a sequence. The zero-order hold converts the sequence back into a piecewise-constant continuous input. Neither operation is the plant.

Worked example 1 — label a room-temperature loop

A thermostat requests \(r=22^\circ\mathrm C\). The sensor reports \(y=20.5^\circ\mathrm C\). The error is \(e=1.5^\circ\mathrm C\). A positive error asks for more heating. The heater and room form the plant; the temperature sensor is the feedback element; the software law is the controller.

Why this matters: if the sensor is moved into a non-unity feedback path, the actuating error \(r-b\) and tracking error \(r-y\) are no longer identical.

Worked example 2 — sampled does not automatically mean digital

A voltage measured every \(0.01\) s produces \(x[k]=x(kT)\), so time is discrete. If each sample may be any real number, amplitude has not yet been quantised. The sequence is sampled-data. After an ADC maps the amplitude to one of finitely many codes, it is digital.

\[T=0.01\text{ s},\qquad f_s=\frac1T=100\text{ Hz},\qquad \omega_s=2\pi f_s=200\pi\text{ rad/s}.\]

Common mistake: sampling quantises time; the ADC also quantises amplitude. These are separate ideas.

Module 2

Sequences, \(Z\)-transforms, inverse transforms, and difference equations

0%
In progress
After this module you can:
  • construct \(X(z)\) directly from a sequence;
  • transform advances with initial conditions;
  • invert rational transforms and check final-value legality;
  • solve causal difference equations without hiding the recurrence logic.

2.1 The transform is a labelled storage system

For a causal sequence,

\[X(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x[k]z^{-k}=x[0]+x[1]z^{-1}+x[2]z^{-2}+\cdots.\]

Each power labels a sample time: \(z^{-1}\) means one-sample delay, \(z^{-2}\) means two-sample delay. This is why a recurrence becomes algebra.

Lab — sequence-to-\(Z\)-transform explorer

Change the first four samples and watch them become coefficients.

2.2 Deriving a transform instead of quoting a table

1
Write the definition and substitute the samples.
2
Recognise a geometric series only after the sequence is correct: \(1+q+q^2+\cdots=1/(1-q)\) for \(|q|<1\).
3
State the region of convergence when it affects uniqueness or stability.
Worked example 1 — direct finite transform

If the finite sequence is

\[x[0]=2,\quad x[1]=2,\quad x[2]=1,\quad x[3]=0,\]

and later samples vanish, then

\[X(z)=2+2z^{-1}+z^{-2}.\]

No transform property is needed. The answer is simply the list of samples written as coefficients.

Worked example 2 — exponential pair from the geometric series
\[x[k]=a^k\mathbf1[k]\quad\Rightarrow\quad X(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(az^{-1})^k=\frac{1}{1-az^{-1}},\quad |z|>|a|.\]

The pole is at \(z=a\), and the causal region lies outside that pole.

2.3 One-sided advances remember initial conditions

Start from the definition rather than memorising:

\[\mathcal Z\{x[k+1]\}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty x[k+1]z^{-k}=z\sum_{n=1}^\infty x[n]z^{-n}=zX(z)-zx[0].\]

The missing \(n=0\) sample is why the initial-condition term appears.

Worked example 3 — solve a first-order forced recurrence

For \(y[k+1]-0.5y[k]=\mathbf1[k]\), \(y[0]=0\):

\[(z-0.5)Y(z)=\frac{z}{z-1},\qquad Y(z)=\frac{z}{(z-1)(z-0.5)}.\]

Expanding \(Y(z)/z\) gives \(2/(z-1)-2/(z-0.5)\), hence

\[y[k]=2\bigl(1-0.5^k\bigr)\mathbf1[k].\]

The steady-state recurrence also gives \(y_\infty-0.5y_\infty=1\), so \(y_\infty=2\).

Lab — recurrence stepper and final-value check

Explore \(y[k+1]=\alpha y[k]+1\), \(y[0]=0\).

2.4 Inverse transforms and the final-value theorem

Use long division for a few samples, recurrence computation for a sample table, and partial fractions of (X(z)/z) for a closed form. The final value formula

\[x[\infty]=\lim_{z\to1}(1-z^{-1})X(z)\]

is legal only if every pole of \((1-z^{-1})X(z)\) is strictly inside \(|z|=1\). One simple pole of \(X(z)\) at \(z=1\) may represent a constant; a repeated pole there grows.

Worked example 4 — reject an illegal final-value calculation

For \(X(z)=z/(z-1)^2\), multiplication by \(1-z^{-1}=(z-1)/z\) leaves \(1/(z-1)\). The remaining pole is on the unit circle, so the theorem is invalid. The underlying sequence is \(x[k]=k\mathbf1[k]\), which has no finite limit.

Exercise-backed method — derive \(\mathcal Z\{k^3\}\)

Apply \(\mathcal Z\{kx[k]\}=-z\,dX/dz\) three times to \(\mathcal Z\{1\}=z/(z-1)\):

\[\mathcal Z\{k^3\}=\frac{z(z^2+4z+1)}{(z-1)^4}=\frac{z^{-1}(1+4z^{-1}+z^{-2})}{(1-z^{-1})^4}.\]

The differentiations, including each product-rule term, were re-derived rather than copied from the supplied solution compilation.

Exercise-backed method — repeated pole and delay

A term \(z^{-1}/(1-az^{-1})^2\) inverts to \(ka^{k-1}\). A separate factor \(z^{-n}\) delays the complete causal sequence by \(n\) samples and inserts \(n\) leading zeros. Keep these two effects separate during partial fractions.

Common mistake: a neat number obtained by substituting \(z=1\) does not prove that the time sequence converges.

Module 3

Sampling, starred transforms, ZOH, and reconstruction

0%
In progress
After this module you can:
  • keep \(x(t)\), \(x[k]\), \(x^*(t)\), \(X^*(s)\), and \(X(z)\) distinct;
  • explain why the ideal sampler uses impulses;
  • derive the ZOH model and predict its intersample waveform.

3.1 Five related objects that are not interchangeable

Continuous signal

\(x(t)\) exists between sampling instants.

Sample sequence

\(x[k]=x(kT)\) is an ordered list of values.

Impulse train

\(x^*(t)=\sum x(kT)\delta(t-kT)\) is a continuous-time distribution used for modelling.

Two transforms

\(X^*(s)=\sum x(kT)e^{-kTs}\), and setting \(z=e^{sT}\) gives \(X(z)\).

An impulse coefficient represents area, not a finite pulse height. This preserves the sample value through continuous convolution.

Worked example 1 — sample a decaying exponential

If \(x(t)=e^{-2t}\mathbf1(t)\), then \(x[k]=e^{-2kT}\) and

\[X(z)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(e^{-2T}z^{-1})^k=\frac{1}{1-e^{-2T}z^{-1}}.\]

3.2 The zero-order hold is a reconstruction rule

The ZOH keeps the most recent sample constant until the next arrives:

\[h(kT+\tau)=x(kT),\quad0\le\tau

One sample creates a width-\(T\) rectangular pulse. Its Laplace transform is

\[G_{h0}(s)=\frac{1-e^{-sT}}{s}.\]
Worked example 2 — why the hold is not the plant

A sequence \([1,2,-1]\) becomes a staircase: \(1\) on \([0,T)\), \(2\) on \([T,2T)\), and \(-1\) on \([2T,3T)\). The plant then responds continuously to that staircase.

Worked example 3 — information lost between samples

The signals \(x_1(t)=0\) and \(x_2(t)=\sin(2\pi t/T)\) have identical samples at \(t=kT\). Sampling alone cannot tell them apart. Reconstructing a unique curve requires an additional assumption such as band limitation or a specified hold.

Lab — sampling and ZOH visualizer

The thin curve is \(x(t)=\sin(2\pi t)\); dots are samples; the staircase is the ZOH output.

Common mistake: \(z=e^{sT}\) links the starred transform to the sequence transform; it does not mean “replace \(s\) by \(z\)” inside an ordinary plant \(G_p(s)\).

Module 4

Pulse-transfer functions, topology, controllers, and PID

0%
In progress
After this module you can:
  • derive a pulse-transfer relation from sampled convolution;
  • decide whether factors may be separated by inspecting sampler placement;
  • convert a ZOH/plant pair and a digital-controller recurrence correctly.

4.1 Pulse-transfer functions describe samples, not every intersample value

With an input impulse sampler, continuous convolution sampled at \(t=kT\) becomes

\[y(kT)=\sum_{h=0}^{k}g((k-h)T)x(hT).\]

This is discrete convolution, so \(Y(z)=G(z)X(z)\) with \(G(z)=\mathcal Z\{g(kT)\}\).

4.2 Sampler placement controls legal factorisation

  • Sampler before \(G(s)\): \(Y(z)=G(z)X(z)\).
  • No input sampler: \(Y(z)=\mathcal Z\{G(s)X(s)\}\), generally not \(G(z)X(z)\).
  • Intermediate sampler between \(G\) and \(H\): use \(G(z)H(z)\).
  • No intermediate sampler: use \(GH(z)=\mathcal Z\{G(s)H(s)\}\).

Lab — sampler-topology decision

Worked example 1 — ZOH plus a first-order plant

For \(G_p(s)=a/(s+a)\),

\[G(z)=(1-z^{-1})\mathcal Z\left\{\frac{G_p(s)}s\right\}=(1-z^{-1})\mathcal Z\{1-e^{-akT}\}=\frac{(1-e^{-aT})z^{-1}}{1-e^{-aT}z^{-1}}.\]

The factor \(z^{-1}\) reflects the causal one-sample delay from input sample to observed output response.

Worked example 2 — controller recurrence to \(G_D(z)\)

For the zero-initial-condition controller

\[m[k]-0.4m[k-1]=0.7e[k]-0.2e[k-1],\]

the transformed relation is

\[(1-0.4z^{-1})M=(0.7-0.2z^{-1})E,\qquad G_D(z)=\frac{M}{E}=\frac{0.7-0.2z^{-1}}{1-0.4z^{-1}}.\]

Only after reducing the ZOH/plant to \(G(z)\) may a correctly placed unity loop use \(C/R=G_DG/(1+G_DG)\).

Worked example 3 — digital PID terms

A common approximation gives

\[G_D(z)=K_P+\frac{K_I}{1-z^{-1}}+K_D(1-z^{-1}).\]

The integral term has a pole at \(z=1\), so it accumulates persistent error. The derivative term uses sample-to-sample change, making it sensitive to noise and the choice of \(T\).

Exercise-backed method — sampled closed-loop sequence

With \(T=1\), a ZOH and plant \(K/s\) give \(G(z)=Kz^{-1}/(1-z^{-1})\). For a unit-step reference in unity feedback,

\[C(z)=\frac{Kz^{-1}}{[1-(1-K)z^{-1}](1-z^{-1})},\qquad c[k]=1-(1-K)^k.\]

The transient decays for \(0<K<2\). This uses the remaining pole \(z=1-K\), not a Chapter 4 Jury test.

Common mistake: \(G(z)H(z)\neq GH(z)\) unless a sampler separates the continuous blocks.

\(s\)-to-\(z\) mapping, Jury stability, and steady-state performance

After this module you can:
  • map continuous poles by radius and angle;
  • apply the complete second-order Jury test;
  • interpret system type and static error constants only under their conditions.

5.1 One equation explains the pole geometry

\[z=e^{sT}=e^{\sigma T}e^{j\omega T},\qquad |z|=e^{\sigma T},\qquad \arg z=\omega T\pmod{2\pi}.\]

Left-half-plane poles have \(\sigma<0\), so they map inside the unit circle. The imaginary axis maps to the unit circle. Frequencies separated by \(2\pi/T\) share the same discrete angle.

Lab — \(s\)-plane to \(z\)-plane mapper

Worked example 1 — map a complex pole

For \(s=-2+j3\), \(T=0.1\): \(z=e^{-0.2}e^{j0.3}\). Thus \(|z|\approx0.8187\) and the angle is \(0.3\) rad. It is stable because the radius is below one.

5.2 Jury tests stability without explicitly solving every root

For \(P(z)=a_0z^2+a_1z+a_2\), \(a_0>0\), the complete second-order conditions are

\[|a_2|0,\qquad P(-1)>0.\]
Worked example 2 — find a stable parameter interval

For \(P(z)=z^2-0.4z+q\):

\[|q|<1,\qquad0.6+q>0,\qquad1.4+q>0.\]

The intersection is \(-0.6

5.3 Stability must be checked before steady-state error

For the lecture's standard configuration, system type is the number of uncancelled open-loop poles at \(z=1\). A Type 1 stable loop has zero step error and finite ramp error. For

\[L(z)=\frac{K}{(z-1)(z-0.5)},\]

the loop is Type 1 and

\[K_v=\lim_{z\to1}\frac{(1-z^{-1})L(z)}T=\frac{2K}{T},\qquad e_{\rm ramp}=\frac{T}{2K}.\]
Worked example 3 — why integral action rejects constants

An uncancelled controller pole at \(z=1\) accumulates constant error. If the closed loop remains stable and the plant has no cancelling zero at \(z=1\), a constant error cannot persist at equilibrium, so constant reference or disturbance error can be eliminated.

Common mistake: system-type tables assume a stable loop and a particular error definition. They are not universal shortcuts for arbitrary diagrams.

Six worked archetypes

Archetype 1 — piecewise sequence from the defining sum

Make a \(k,kT,x(kT)\) table first. If the samples are \(1,1,3,3,3,0,\ldots\), then

\[X(z)=1+z^{-1}+3z^{-2}+3z^{-3}+3z^{-4}.\]

Boundary ownership is decided before algebra.

Archetype 2 — recurrence with initial conditions

For \(y[k+1]+0.25y[k]=\mathbf1[k]\), \(y[0]=0\), write \((z+0.25)Y=z/(z-1)\). Invert \(Y/z\), then verify the first two samples directly from the recurrence.

Archetype 3 — parameter-dependent final value

For \(y[k+1]-\beta y[k]=1\), the remaining pole after removing the step pole is \(z=\beta\). The final-value theorem is valid exactly for \(|\beta|<1\). Treat \(\beta=1\), \(\beta=-1\), and \(|\beta|>1\) separately.

Archetype 4 — sampler, ZOH, plant, and output

Reduce the ZOH/plant by \((1-z^{-1})\mathcal Z\{G_p(s)/s\}\), multiply by the sampled input transform, and inspect the output poles before final value.

Archetype 5 — does a pulse-transfer function exist?

If a continuous external input remains inseparably inside a starred product, a universal input-independent ratio may not exist. Write the starred-domain signal equations instead of forcing a block ratio.

Archetype 6 — controller recurrence and closed loop

Transform the controller recurrence to \(G_D=M/E\), reduce the ZOH/plant to \(G\), confirm the feedback sign and sampler topology, then form \(C/R=G_DG/(1+G_DG)\).
