Q1 · Signals, z-transform, difference equations

Z1. Signals, the z-transform and the final value theorem Refreshevery recalled Q1

Signals. A continuous-time signal is defined for every \(t\), jumps allowed. Sampling it gives a discrete-time signal. If its amplitude is still continuous it is a sampled-data signal; only if the amplitude is also quantised is it digital. Past Q1s open with exactly this classification.

\[X(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x(kT)\,z^{-k},\qquad \lim_{k\to\infty}x(k)=\lim_{z\to1}\,(1-z^{-1})X(z)\] valid only when every pole of \((1-z^{-1})X(z)\) is strictly inside the unit circle.

Example. \(x(kT)=3,1,1,1,\dots\) (a 3 at \(k=0\), then a step of 1): \(X(z)=3+\sum_{k\ge1}z^{-k}=3+\dfrac{1}{z-1}\). Its final value: \((1-z^{-1})X(z)=\dfrac{z-1}{z}\cdot3+\dfrac1z\to 1\) as \(z\to1\). Correct: the sequence settles at 1.

Pitfalls. At a jump, the sample takes the value of the branch that holds at that instant (\(t\ge1\) means the new branch at \(t=1\)). A pole at \(z=-1\) or a double pole at \(z=1\) forbids the FVT: say so, do not compute a number.

Z2. Difference equations by z-transform Refreshevery recalled Q1

Shift theorem: \(\mathcal Z[y(k+1)]=zY(z)-zy(0)\), \(\mathcal Z[y(k+2)]=z^2Y(z)-z^2y(0)-zy(1)\). So you need \(y(0)\) and \(y(1)\). If the question gives \(y(k)=0\) for \(k<0\) instead, compute them by putting \(k=-2\) and \(k=-1\) into the equation (Prof. Wen: do not assume initial values the question does not give).

  1. Initial values from the equation at negative \(k\).
  2. Transform, solve for \(Y(z)\).
  3. Partial fractions on \(Y(z)/z\), then the table. Why \(/z\): each term \(\dfrac{c}{z-a}\), multiplied back by \(z\), is \(c\,\dfrac{z}{z-a}\to c\,a^k\) (Row 18) straight away, and a leftover \(\dfrac{c_0}{z}\) term becomes \(c_0\,\delta(k)\). Expanding \(Y(z)\) itself gives \(\dfrac{c}{z-a}\to c\,a^{k-1}\ (k\ge1)\) (Row 19): the same signal, with a shift to track (Wen notes p. 60, eq. 2-21).
  4. Check one value against the recursion.
Start here: the short way when the system starts at rest (no initial-value terms, cover-up partial fractions)

Picture. A sequence is a row of numbers on a time line. \(y(k+2)\) slides the row two steps to the left: \(y(0)\) and \(y(1)\) land at \(k=-2,-1\), where the one-sided z-transform does not look, so they fall off and the shift theorem has to put them back (\(-z^2y(0)-zy(1)\)). \(y(k-2)\) slides the row to the right: nothing falls off, only the zeros from before \(k=0\) slide in. So a delay costs no initial-value terms: Wen's Table 2-3 gives \(\mathcal Z[x(k-m)]=z^{-m}X(z)\) and nothing else (Wen notes pp. 67–68, eq. 2-27, Table 2-3).

SymbolMeaning
\(x(k)\), \(y(k)\)input (given), output (to find)
at restthe question says \(x(k)=y(k)=0\) for \(k<0\)
\(z^{-1}\)one-step delay: \(\mathcal Z[y(k-1)]=z^{-1}Y\) when \(y\) is zero before \(k=0\)

Derive (Retry R1: \(y(k+2)-0.6y(k+1)+0.08y(k)=3x(k+1)\), at rest). (1) Put \(k-2\) for \(k\), which is the same equation written with delays: \(y(k)-0.6y(k-1)+0.08y(k-2)=3x(k-1)\). (2) Table 2-3, delay rows: \(Y-0.6z^{-1}Y+0.08z^{-2}Y=3z^{-1}X\). (3) Multiply by \(z^2\): \((z^2-0.6z+0.08)\,Y=3z\,X\). In words: the left-side coefficients give the denominator, the right side gives the numerator, and there are no initial-value terms. With \(X=\dfrac{z}{z-1}\): \(Y=\dfrac{3z^2}{(z-1)(z-0.4)(z-0.2)}\).

Why the long way gives the same answer. The terms the advance form drops are the equation itself at \(k=-2\) and \(k=-1\), times \(z^2\) and \(z\). Left side: \(z^2\cdot y(0)+z\cdot[y(1)-0.6y(0)]=0+3z\). Right side: \(z^2\cdot3x(-1)+z\cdot3x(0)=0+3z\). The equation holds at those \(k\), so the dropped terms are equal and cancel. That is all \(y(0)\) and \(y(1)\) do there.

Cover-up for the partial fractions (simple poles only). \(\dfrac Yz=\dfrac{A}{z-1}+\dfrac{B}{z-0.4}+\dfrac{C}{z-0.2}\). Multiply by \((z-1)\) and put \(z=1\): the \(B\) and \(C\) terms are multiplied by 0, so \(A\) is \(\dfrac Yz\) with its \((z-1)\) covered, evaluated at \(z=1\): \(A=\dfrac{3}{0.6\cdot0.8}=6.25\). Likewise \(B=\dfrac{3(0.4)}{(-0.6)(0.2)}=-10\) and \(C=\dfrac{3(0.2)}{(-0.8)(-0.2)}=3.75\). So \(y(k)=6.25-10(0.4)^k+3.75(0.2)^k=0,\,3,\,4.8,\dots\), which matches the recursion.

When it is not allowed. If the question gives initial values that do not come from rest, e.g. Example 2-17, \(x(k+2)+3x(k+1)+2x(k)=0\) with \(x(0)=0,\ x(1)=1\) (Wen notes p. 69), use the advance rows with those values. With zero input, a system at rest would stay at 0, so that 1 was put in by hand. In the exam, find \(y(0)\) and \(y(1)\) whenever they are asked for: they earn marks and they are your check.

Method to remember: at rest → read \(Y=\dfrac{\text{right-side polynomial}}{\text{left-side polynomial}}\,X\), do cover-up on \(Y/z\), multiply by \(z\), use Row 18, check \(y(1)\).

Q2 · Stability, errors, direct design, implementation

J1. Jury range of K and steady-state errors Refreshrecalled 2019–2023

Closed-loop CE \(P(z)=1+C(z)G(z)\) cleared of fractions, written \(a_0z^n+\dots+a_n\) with \(a_0>0\). For \(n=2\) the Jury test is three inequalities:

\[|a_2|<a_0,\qquad P(1)>0,\qquad P(-1)>0\ (n \text{ even}).\] Type = number of poles of \(CG\) at \(z=1\). \(K_p=\lim_{z\to1}CG\), \(K_v=\lim_{z\to1}\dfrac{(z-1)CG}{Tz}\); step error \(1/(1+K_p)\), ramp error \(1/K_v\).

For \(n=3\) you also need the \(b\)-row: \(|b_2|>|b_0|\) with \(b_0=a_3a_1-a_0a_2\)… you lost this in Quiz 3 Mock D, so practise it from the Quiz 3 primer.

Start here: where the three Jury inequalities and the error formulas come from, worked on the check below

Picture. A digital loop is stable when every closed-loop pole is strictly inside the unit circle, so each mode \(p^k\) dies away. The poles are the roots of \(P(z)\). For a 2nd-order \(P\) you could solve the quadratic, but with an unknown \(K\) that is messy. The Jury test only asks three sign questions about \(P\), and those give a range of \(K\) directly (Wen notes pp. 164–166, eq. 4-5 and the criterion).

SymbolMeaning
\(G(z)\)plant pulse transfer function (given)
\(C(z)=K\)controller gain (you choose it)
\(P(z)=a_0z^2+a_1z+a_2\)numerator of \(1+CG\) after clearing fractions; its roots are the closed-loop poles
typehow many factors \((z-1)\) the denominator of \(CG\) has; each is a digital integrator (running sum)
\(e_{ss}\)error \(r-y\) at the sampling instants as \(k\to\infty\)

Why each inequality (\(n=2\), \(a_0>0\)).

  1. \(P(1)>0\). With \(a_0>0\), \(P(z)\) is positive for very large \(z\). If \(P(1)\le0\) it must cross zero somewhere at \(z\ge1\): a real pole on or outside the circle on the right.
  2. \(P(-1)>0\) for \(n\) even (\(<0\) for \(n\) odd). The same argument on the left: for \(n\) even, \(P\) is positive for very negative \(z\), so \(P(-1)\le0\) means a real pole at \(z\le-1\).
  3. \(|a_2|<a_0\). The product of the two roots is \(a_2/a_0\). If both have size below 1, the product does too. This one catches the complex pair that leaves the circle sideways, which the first two cannot see.

For \(n=2\) the three together are also enough (Wen p. 166: the 4th condition is null). For \(n=3\) they are not, which is why the \(b\)-row is added.

Worked on the check. \(G=\dfrac{0.2(z+1)}{(z-1)(z-0.4)}\), \(C=K\). \(1+CG=0\), times \((z-1)(z-0.4)\):

\[P(z)=(z-1)(z-0.4)+0.2K(z+1)=z^2+(0.2K-1.4)z+(0.4+0.2K).\]

(1) \(P(1)\): the first term \((z-1)(z-0.4)\) is 0 at \(z=1\), but the feedback term is not: \(0.2K(1+1)=0.4K\). So \(P(1)=0.4K>0\ \Rightarrow\ K>0\). Why \(P(1)\ne0\) although \(G\) has a pole at 1: \(z=1\) is an open-loop pole. Closing the loop adds \(0.2K(z+1)\) and moves the poles; only with \(K=0\) (no feedback) does a pole stay at \(z=1\), which is why \(K=0\) is excluded. (2) \(P(-1)=(-2)(-1.4)+0=2.8>0\) for every \(K\): the plant zero at \(z=-1\) kills the \(K\) term. (3) \(|0.4+0.2K|<1\ \Rightarrow\ -7<K<3\). Together: \(0<K<3\). At \(K=3\), \(P=z^2-0.8z+1\): complex poles with \(|z|^2=1\), on the circle, so marginal, not stable. The "largest stable \(K\)" is the strict bound 3.

Why the error formulas (Wen notes pp. 184–187, eqs. 4.6–4.9). Around the loop \(E=R-CG\,E\), so \(E=\dfrac{R}{1+CG}\). The final value theorem gives \(e_{ss}=\lim_{z\to1}(1-z^{-1})\dfrac{R}{1+CG}\), valid only if the loop is stable, so do Jury first.

  • Step, \(R=\dfrac{z}{z-1}\): the \((1-z^{-1})=\dfrac{z-1}{z}\) cancels it, leaving \(e_{ss}=\dfrac{1}{1+K_p}\), \(K_p=\lim_{z\to1}CG\).
  • Ramp, \(R=\dfrac{Tz}{(z-1)^2}\): one \((z-1)\) is left in the denominator, so \(e_{ss}=\lim\dfrac{T}{(1-z^{-1})CG}=\dfrac{1}{K_v}\), \(K_v=\lim_{z\to1}\dfrac{(1-z^{-1})CG}{T}=\lim_{z\to1}\dfrac{(z-1)CG}{Tz}\). The \(T\) comes from the ramp itself: \(r(kT)=kT\) climbs \(T\) per sample.

So the type decides which error is finite. Type 0: \(K_p\) finite, \(K_v=0\), ramp error \(\infty\). Type 1: \(K_p=\infty\), step error 0, ramp error finite. In words: an integrator keeps adding the error until the error is gone, so one integrator removes a constant error, and against a ramp it leaves a constant lag.

Worked on the second check. \(K=1\) is inside \(0<K<3\). \(CG\) has one \((z-1)\): type 1, step error 0. \(K_v=\dfrac{1}{T}\cdot\dfrac{0.2(1+1)}{1\cdot(1-0.4)}=\dfrac{1}{0.2}\cdot\dfrac{0.4}{0.6}=3.33\), ramp error \(=1/K_v=0.3\). A 400-step simulation of the loop with \(r(k)=0.2k\) settles at \(e=0.300\) (my own check).

Method to remember: clear fractions to get \(P(z)\) → \(P(1)>0\), \(P(-1)\) sign, \(|a_n|<a_0\) → intersect the ranges, strict → check \(K\) is in the range → count the \((z-1)\) factors → \(K_p\) or \(K_v\) (with \(1/T\)) → error.

Self-check. \(G=\dfrac{0.5}{(z-1)(z-0.5)}\), \(C=K\). Find the stable range of \(K\), then the ramp error for \(K=0.5\), \(T=0.1\) s.

Answer

\(P=z^2-1.5z+(0.5+0.5K)\). \(P(1)=0.5K>0\Rightarrow K>0\); \(P(-1)=3+0.5K>0\Rightarrow K>-6\); \(|0.5+0.5K|<1\Rightarrow-3<K<1\). Stable for \(0<K<1\). \(K=0.5\) is inside; type 1; \(K_v=\dfrac{1}{0.1}\cdot\dfrac{0.5(0.5)}{0.5}=5\), ramp error \(0.2\) (simulation: 0.200).

D1. Direct design: deadbeat and ripple-free Exam focus · open mistakerecalled in 5 of 5 papers 2019–2023

You choose the closed loop \(G_{cl}(z)\) you want, then the controller follows from \(G_{cl}=\dfrac{CG}{1+CG}\):

\[C(z)=\frac{G_{cl}(z)}{G(z)\,[1-G_{cl}(z)]},\qquad U(z)=\frac{G_{cl}(z)}{G(z)}R(z),\qquad Y(z)=G_{cl}(z)R(z).\]
Start here: why a plain deadbeat ripples, and how to fix it

Picture. \(G_{cl}=z^{-1}\) means "output equals the reference one sample later". At the sampling instants that is perfect. But \(C\) must contain \(1/G\), so it cancels every plant zero. A cancelled zero at \(z=-b\) becomes a pole of \(U(z)\): the control keeps alternating as \((-b)^k\). The plant integrates those alternating pushes, so between samples \(y(t)\) wobbles: intersample ripple.

Fix. Do not cancel the zero: put it into \(G_{cl}\). Then \(U=G_{cl}R/G\) has no \((-b)^k\) mode and becomes constant after a few samples.

Condition on \(G_{cl}\)Why
relative degree \(\ge\) that of \(G\)\(C\) must be causal
contains the plant zeros you must not cancel (outside the circle, or all of them for ripple-free)otherwise \(C\) has an unstable or ringing pole
\(1-G_{cl}\) contains the plant poles on or outside the circleotherwise \(C\) cancels an unstable pole
\(G_{cl}(1)=1\)zero steady-state error for a step

Recipe for a ripple-free deadbeat (step input), plant \(G=\dfrac{c(z+b)}{(z-1)(z-p)}\): take \(G_{cl}=K\,z^{-1}(1+bz^{-1})=\dfrac{K(z+b)}{z^2}\) and \(K=\dfrac1{1+b}\) from \(G_{cl}(1)=1\). Settling takes one sample more than the plain deadbeat.

Q4 · Observers and optimal control

O1. Prediction observer and its gain Exam focusobserver gain in 4 of 7 recalled papers

Why. State feedback \(u=-\mathbf K\mathbf x\) needs every state, but usually only \(y\) is measured. An observer runs a copy of the model and corrects it with the output error.

\[\hat{\mathbf x}(k+1)=\mathbf A\hat{\mathbf x}(k)+\mathbf Bu(k)+\mathbf L_O\,[y(k)-\mathbf C\hat{\mathbf x}(k)],\qquad \mathbf e(k+1)=(\mathbf A-\mathbf L_O\mathbf C)\,\mathbf e(k),\ \ \mathbf e=\mathbf x-\hat{\mathbf x}.\] \[\mathbf L_O=\alpha_O(\mathbf A)\,\mathbf W_O^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}0\\ \vdots\\ 1\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{(Ackermann, dual of the controller formula)}.\]

Choose the observer poles (roots of \(\alpha_O\)) 2–4 times faster than the controller poles, or all at 0 for a deadbeat error: then \((\mathbf A-\mathbf L_O\mathbf C)^n=\mathbf 0\) and the estimate is exact after \(n\) samples. "Prediction" because \(\hat{\mathbf x}(k+1)\) uses measurements only up to \(k\). The observer needs \((\mathbf A,\mathbf C)\) observable.

Example (Lee Ex 4.3). Double integrator, \(\mathbf C=[1\ 0]\), deadbeat error: \(\mathbf L_O=\left[2\ \ \tfrac1T\right]^T\). With \(T=0.5\): \(\mathbf A-\mathbf L_O\mathbf C=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0.5\\-2&1\end{bmatrix}\), whose square is \(\mathbf 0\).

Pitfall. The observer equation has two inputs, \(u(k)\) and \(y(k)\): write it as \(\hat{\mathbf x}(k+1)=(\mathbf A-\mathbf L_O\mathbf C)\hat{\mathbf x}(k)+\mathbf Bu(k)+\mathbf L_Oy(k)\).

O2. Reduced-order and current observers Exam focusreduced-order: recalled 2019

Reduced-order. If \(y=x_a\) is measured directly, estimate only the rest, \(x_b\). Partition \(\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{aa}&A_{ab}\\A_{ba}&A_{bb}\end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf B=\begin{bmatrix}B_a\\B_b\end{bmatrix}\):

\[e_b(k+1)=(A_{bb}-L_rA_{ab})\,e_b(k),\qquad \hat x_b(k+1)=(A_{bb}-L_rA_{ab})\hat x_b(k)+L_r\,y(k+1)+(A_{ba}-L_rA_{aa})\,y(k)+(B_b-L_rB_a)\,u(k).\]

For a scalar \(x_b\), \(L_r\) comes from matching \(z-A_{bb}+L_rA_{ab}\) to the desired \(z-p\).

Current observer. Predict, then correct with the measurement taken at the same instant: \(\bar{\mathbf x}(k+1)=\mathbf A\hat{\mathbf x}(k)+\mathbf Bu(k)\), \(\hat{\mathbf x}(k+1)=\bar{\mathbf x}(k+1)+\mathbf L_C[y(k+1)-\mathbf C\bar{\mathbf x}(k+1)]\). Its error matrix is \(\mathbf A-\mathbf L_C\mathbf{CA}\) and

\[\mathbf L_C=\alpha_O(\mathbf A)\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{CA}\\ \vdots\\ \mathbf{CA}^n\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}0\\ \vdots\\ 1\end{bmatrix}.\]

Example (Lee Ex 4.4). \(T=0.1\) double integrator, pole at 0.5: \(z-1+0.1L_r=z-0.5\Rightarrow L_r=5\).

Separation. Feeding back \(\hat{\mathbf x}\) instead of \(\mathbf x\) gives closed-loop poles = controller poles \(\cup\) observer poles: the characteristic polynomial is \(\alpha_c(z)\,\alpha_O(z)\). Design the two separately.

Reference input. \(u(k)=-\mathbf K\mathbf x(k)+k_r\,r(k)\) gives \(\dfrac{Y(z)}{R(z)}=k_r\,\mathbf C(z\mathbf I-\mathbf A+\mathbf{BK})^{-1}\mathbf B\). \(k_r\) does not move the poles; choose it so the DC gain (\(z=1\)) is 1.

L2. Infinite horizon: the algebraic Riccati equation Exam focusrecalled 2017–2023, 5 times

As \(N\to\infty\), \(\mathbf S(k)\) settles to a constant \(\hat{\mathbf S}\), and the gain becomes constant:

\[\hat{\mathbf S}=\mathbf A^T\hat{\mathbf S}\mathbf A-\mathbf A^T\hat{\mathbf S}\mathbf B\left(\mathbf B^T\hat{\mathbf S}\mathbf B+\mathbf R\right)^{-1}\mathbf B^T\hat{\mathbf S}\mathbf A+\mathbf Q,\qquad \mathbf K=\left(\mathbf B^T\hat{\mathbf S}\mathbf B+\mathbf R\right)^{-1}\mathbf B^T\hat{\mathbf S}\mathbf A.\]
