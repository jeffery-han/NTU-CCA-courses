1 · Ch 4: z-plane stability and steady-state error

Idea. Sampling maps \(s\) to \(z=e^{sT}\). The left half s-plane lands inside the unit circle, so a sampled loop is stable iff every closed-loop pole has \(|z|\lt 1\). The Jury test checks this from the coefficients, without finding the roots pp. 146–147.

Jury, for \(P(z)=a_0z^n+\dots+a_n\) with \(a_0\gt0\) pp. 164–166: (1) \(|a_n|\lt a_0\); (2) \(P(1)\gt0\); (3) \(P(-1)\gt0\) if \(n\) is even, \(\lt0\) if \(n\) is odd; (4) the table rows \(|b_{n-1}|\gt|b_0|,\dots\). For \(n=2\) you only need (1)–(3).

Error constants (unity feedback, \(GH(z)\) includes the ZOH) pp. 186–187: \[K_p=\lim_{z\to1}GH(z),\quad K_v=\lim_{z\to1}\frac{(1-z^{-1})GH(z)}{T},\quad K_a=\lim_{z\to1}\frac{(1-z^{-1})^2GH(z)}{T^2},\] which give \(e_{ss}=\frac1{1+K_p},\ \frac1{K_v},\ \frac1{K_a}\) for a step, ramp and acceleration respectively.

Pitfall: \(K_v\) has a \(1/T\). Forget it and you are wrong by a factor of 10 at \(T=0.1\).

Quick checks

\(P(z)=z^2+(K-1.3)z+0.4\). What is the upper limit of \(K\) for stability?

\(|0.4|\lt1\) always holds. \(P(1)=K+0.1\gt0\) gives \(K\gt-0.1\). \(P(-1)=1-(K-1.3)+0.4=2.7-K\gt0\) gives \(K\lt2.7\).

\(GH(z)=\dfrac{0.1(z+0.9)}{(z-1)(z-0.6)}\), \(T=0.1\). Find \(K_v\).

\((1-z^{-1})GH=\frac{z-1}{z}GH\to\frac{0.1\cdot1.9}{1\cdot0.4}=0.475\). Divide by \(T\): \(K_v=4.75\), so the ramp error is \(1/4.75\approx0.21\).

2 · §5.2: Turning an analog controller into a digital one

Idea. Design \(C(s)\) as usual, map it to \(C(z)\), then check the sampled response. This works well when \(T\) is small. The design steps are on pp. 7–8.

Bilinear (Tustin). Trapezoidal integration gives \(\displaystyle s=\frac{2}{T}\frac{z-1}{z+1}\). Substitute it and simplify. It preserves DC gain: \(z=1\) maps to \(s=0\). Example: \(\frac{a}{s+a}\to\frac{a}{\frac{2}{T}\frac{z-1}{z+1}+a}\).

Pole-zero matching pp. 14–15. Map every pole and zero by \(z=e^{sT}\). With \(n\) poles and \(m\) zeros, add \(n-m\) zeros at \(-1\) (proper) or \(n-m-1\) (strictly proper, as the slide formula uses). Then pick \(\alpha\) to match the gain at a critical frequency, usually DC: \(G(1)=G_a(0)\). For complex poles \(-\zeta\omega_n\pm j\omega_d\), the denominator is \(z^2-2e^{-\zeta\omega_nT}\cos(\omega_dT)z+e^{-2\zeta\omega_nT}\) (Example 5.2 pp. 16–17).

Quick checks

Map \(C(s)=\frac{2}{s+2}\) with the bilinear rule at \(T=0.5\). Where is the pole of \(C(z)\)?

\(s=4\frac{z-1}{z+1}\), so \(C=\frac{2(z+1)}{4(z-1)+2(z+1)}=\frac{z+1}{3z-1}\). The pole is at \(z=1/3\). DC check: \(C(1)=2/2=1\).

Same \(C(s)\), now pole-zero matched and strictly proper at \(T=0.5\): \(C(z)=\frac{\alpha}{z-e^{-1}}\). Find \(\alpha\) for equal DC gain.

\(n-m-1=0\), so no \((z+1)\) is added. \(C(1)=\alpha/(1-0.3679)=1\) gives \(\alpha=0.6321\).

3 · §5.3: Frequency-response design in the w-plane

Idea. \(G(e^{j\omega T})\) is not rational in \(\omega\), so Bode rules do not apply directly. The w-transform \(z=\frac{1+wT/2}{1-wT/2}\), or equivalently \(w=\frac2T\frac{z-1}{z+1}\), maps the unit-circle interior onto the left half w-plane. Then you design with ordinary Bode tools in the fictitious frequency \(v\) pp. 23–24.

Procedure: \(G_{ZAS}(z)\to G(w)\); choose \(K\) from the error constant (e.g. \(K_v=\lim_{w\to0}wG_D(w)G(w)\)); read the PM of \(KG\); add the phase lead \(\phi=\text{PM}_\text{req}-\text{PM}+(5^\circ\text{ to }10^\circ)\); set \[\alpha=\frac{1-\sin\phi}{1+\sin\phi};\] place the new crossover where \(|KG(jv)|=10\log\alpha\) dB, i.e. \(-10\log(1/\alpha)\); set \(\tau=1/(\sqrt\alpha\,v_g')\); \(G_D(w)=\frac{\tau w+1}{\alpha\tau w+1}\); map back with \(w=\frac2T\frac{z-1}{z+1}\) (Example 5.5 pp. 44–46).

Lead vs lag p. 29. Lead raises the PM and bandwidth, so the response is faster but more sensitive to noise. Lag raises low-frequency gain and lowers bandwidth, so steady-state accuracy improves, the response is slower and noise is attenuated. Lag-lead combines both.

Quick checks

You need \(\phi=30^\circ\) of lead. Find \(\alpha\).

\((1-0.5)/(1+0.5)=1/3\). The new crossover is then where \(|KG|=10\log(1/3)=-4.77\) dB.

Which compensator do you add to cut the steady-state error without much change to the transient?

Lag raises the low-frequency gain, which improves accuracy. Lead targets the phase margin and speed of response.

4 · §5.4: Direct design from a desired \(G_{cl}(z)\); deadbeat

Idea. Choose the closed loop you want, then solve for the controller. From \(G_{cl}=\frac{CG}{1+CG}\) pp. 49–50: \[C(z)=\frac{1}{G_{ZAS}(z)}\,\frac{G_{cl}(z)}{1-G_{cl}(z)}\qquad(5.11)\]

Four conditions on \(G_{cl}\) pp. 51–56: (i) relative degree of \(G_{cl}\) ≥ that of \(G_{ZAS}\) (causality); (ii) \(G_{cl}\) contains every zero of \(G_{ZAS}\) outside the unit circle; (iii) \(1-G_{cl}\) contains every unstable pole of \(G_{ZAS}\); (iv) \(G_{cl}(1)=1\) (zero step error). Conditions (ii) and (iii) exist because \(C\) would otherwise cancel an unstable zero or pole. The loop would then look input-output stable but would not be asymptotically stable.

Choosing \(G_{cl}\) p. 57: pick \(T_s\) and the overshoot, choose a unit-gain 1st/2nd-order \(G_{cl}(s)\), map its poles by \(z=e^{sT}\), then check (i)–(iv). A plant delay \(z^{-5}\) forces \(z^{-5}\) into \(G_{cl}\) (Example 5.7).

Deadbeat pp. 64–65: if all poles and zeros of \(G_{ZAS}\) are inside the circle, choose \(G_{cl}=z^{-m}\) with \(m\) = relative degree. Then \(C=\frac{1}{G_{ZAS}}\frac{z^{-m}}{1-z^{-m}}\) (5.12), and the only design knob left is \(T\).

Count the relative degree in powers of \(z\), after clearing every \(z^{-1}\): (degree of denominator) − (degree of numerator).

Quick checks

\(G_{ZAS}=\dfrac{0.05z^{-2}(1+0.7z^{-1})}{(1-0.9z^{-1})(1-0.5z^{-1})}\). Deadbeat \(G_{cl}=z^{-m}\): what is \(m\)?

Numerator \(=\frac{z+0.7}{z^3}\); denominator \(=\frac{(z-0.9)(z-0.5)}{z^2}\). So \(G=\frac{0.05(z+0.7)}{z(z-0.9)(z-0.5)}\), with relative degree 3 − 1 = 2.

If \(G_{ZAS}\) had a zero at \(z=-1.5\), could you still use \(G_{cl}=z^{-m}\)?

No. Condition (ii) makes \(G_{cl}\) contain \((z+1.5)\); otherwise \(C\) has a pole at −1.5 and the control signal blows up.

5 · §5.4.3: Ripple-free deadbeat

Idea. Plain deadbeat makes \(C\) cancel all plant zeros. A zero near \(-1\) then becomes a controller pole near \(-1\), so \(u(k)\) alternates sign and the output rings between samples (Examples 5.8–5.9 pp. 66–67). The fix is to keep \(u\) constant after \(n\) samples, \(n\) being the plant order pp. 73–74: \[U(z)=G_{cl}(z)\frac{R(z)}{G_{ZAS}(z)}\qquad(5.13)\] Choose \(G_{cl}\) so that \(U(z)\) is a finite polynomial in \(z^{-1}\). In practice: \(G_{cl}=Kz^{-d}\times\)(all zeros of \(G_{ZAS}\)), then \(G_{cl}(1)=1\) fixes \(K\).

Type 1 (has \(1-z^{-1}\))\(u(k)=0\); \(R\)'s \(1-z^{-1}\) cancels the plant integratorEx 5.10 pp. 75–77
Type 0\(u(k)\) = non-zero constant; \(e(k)=0\), so \(E=e_0+e_1z^{-1}\), and \(C\) must contain an integratorEx 5.12 pp. 78–82

Quick checks

A type-1 plant has one zero, at \(z=-0.6\), and relative degree 1. The ripple-free choice is \(G_{cl}=Kz^{-1}(1+0.6z^{-1})\). Find \(K\).

\(G_{cl}(1)=K(1+0.6)=1\) gives \(K=0.625\). (Wrong path: \(1/0.6\).)

For a type-0 plant, what does \(u(k)\) do after \(n\) samples in a ripple-free design?

Without an integrator in the plant, holding \(y=1\) needs a constant input. The integrator must then sit in \(C(z)\) (p. 79).

6 · §5.5–5.7: One vs two degrees of freedom; pole placement

1-DOF p. 84: \(y=Tr+Sv\) with \(T=\frac{GG_c}{1+GG_c}\) and \(S=\frac{1}{1+GG_c}\). Because \(S+T=1\), fixing one fixes the other.

2-DOF pp. 85–87: \(u=\gamma\frac{T_c}{R_c}r-\frac{S_c}{R_c}y\) and plant \(G=z^{-k}B/A\) give \[Y=\frac{z^{-k}\gamma BT_c}{R_cA+z^{-k}BS_c}R+\frac{AR_c}{R_cA+z^{-k}BS_c}V.\] The denominator sets stability, the second term should be small (disturbance rejection), and the first should be close to 1 (tracking).

Pole placement pp. 89–93: split \(B=B^gB^b\), \(A=A^gA^b\) into good and bad factors (bad = outside the circle or poorly damped). Set \(R_c=B^gR_1\), \(S_c=A^gS_1\), \(T_c=A^gT_1\). Then solve \[A^bR_1+z^{-k}B^bS_1=\phi_{cl}\qquad(5.24)\] for \(R_1,S_1\) by matching coefficients. With \(T_1=1\): \(\gamma=\phi_{cl}(1)/B^b(1)\) and \(G_Y=\gamma z^{-k}B^b/\phi_{cl}\). Bad zeros cannot be moved by feedback.

Quick checks

\(\phi_{cl}=1-1.2z^{-1}+0.45z^{-2}\) and \(B^b=1+1.5z^{-1}\). With \(T_1=1\), find \(\gamma\).

\(\phi_{cl}(1)=0.25\) and \(B^b(1)=2.5\), so \(\gamma=0.1\).

Which choice of \(T_1\) collapses the 2-DOF controller back to 1-DOF?

With \(T_1=S_1\), \(T_c=S_c\) and \(u=\frac{S_c}{R_c}(\gamma r-y)\): one block acting on the error (p. 92).

7 · Ch 6: Implementing \(C(z)\) in software

Write \(C(z)=\frac{b_0+b_1z^{-1}+\dots+b_mz^{-m}}{1+a_1z^{-1}+\dots+a_nz^{-n}}\) (6.1, \(n\ge m\)) pp. 104–105.

Direct programming (Fig 6.1): \(y(k)=-a_1y(k-1)-\dots-a_ny(k-n)+b_0x(k)+\dots+b_mx(k-m)\). It uses \(n+m\) delays pp. 106–107.

Standard programming (Fig 6.2) pp. 108–110: split through an internal signal \(H\): \[h(k)=x(k)-a_1h(k-1)-\dots-a_nh(k-n),\] \[y(k)=b_0h(k)+b_1h(k-1)+\dots+b_mh(k-m).\] It needs only \(n\) delays, which saves memory. The \(a_i\) are feedback gains and the \(b_i\) feed-forward gains.

Coefficient quantization pp. 116–119: \(|\Delta p_i|=\left|\sum_j\frac{p_i^{\,n-j}}{\prod_{k\ne i}(p_i-p_k)}\Delta a_j\right|\). Tightly clustered poles make the denominator small and the error large. The fix is to break \(C\) into 1st/2nd-order sections: series \(C=\prod G_i\), grouping real pairs or conjugate pairs; or parallel \(C=A+\sum G_i\) by partial fractions pp. 120–125.

Sign trap: (6.1) has \(+a_i\) in the denominator, but the recursion uses \(-a_i\). For \(1-0.5z^{-1}\) the recursion term is \(+0.5\,y(k-1)\).

Quick checks

\(C=\dfrac{2-1.2z^{-1}+0.3z^{-2}}{1-0.5z^{-1}+0.06z^{-2}}\). How many delay elements does standard programming need?

\(n=2\), so 2 delays. Direct programming would need \(n+m=4\).

Same \(C\), direct form \(u(k)=c\,u(k-1)-0.06\,u(k-2)+\dots\). What is \(c\)?

\(a_1=-0.5\), and the recursion uses \(-a_1=+0.5\).
