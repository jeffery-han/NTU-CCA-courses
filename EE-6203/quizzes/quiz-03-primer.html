1 · Ch 4: z-plane stability and steady-state error

Start here: what "stable" and "steady-state error" mean, and where every rule below comes from (about 8 minutes)

1.0.1 The picture: a pole is a multiplier applied once per sample. In a sampled system, a pole at \(z=p\) adds a term \(p^k\) to the output at sample \(k\). The value does not repeat. The rule does: every sample, that part of the output is multiplied by the same \(p\).

Why. \(z\) means "one sample later". So \(\dfrac{Y}{U}=\dfrac{1}{z-p}\) says \(zY=pY+U\), i.e. \(y(k+1)=p\,y(k)+u(k)\). Kick it once (\(u(0)=1\), then 0) and step it: \(y=0,\ 1,\ p,\ p^2,\ p^3,\dots\) Each new value is \(p\) times the old one. In reverse, \(\mathcal Z\{p^k\}=\sum_k p^kz^{-k}=\dfrac{1}{1-pz^{-1}}=\dfrac{z}{z-p}\) (a geometric series), so "\(p^k\)" and "pole at \(p\)" are the same thing written two ways. A bigger \(Y(z)\) splits by partial fractions into \(\sum\frac{A_i}{z-p_i}\): one such recursion per pole, running side by side, so \(y(k)=\sum A_ip_i^{\,k}\) (shifted by one sample, which does not change growth or decay). The link to continuous time: a mode \(e^{st}\) sampled at \(t=kT\) is \(e^{skT}=(e^{sT})^k\). So \(p=e^{sT}\) is exactly "how much that mode changes in one sample period". A complex \(p=re^{j\theta}\) scales by \(r\) and rotates by \(\theta\) each sample. With its conjugate, that gives the real decaying oscillation \(r^k\cos(k\theta+\varphi)\).

Four sample values of \(p\) show everything:

Pole \(p\)\(p^k\) for \(k=0,1,2,3,4\)Behaviour
\(0.5\)\(1,\ 0.5,\ 0.25,\ 0.125,\ 0.06\)dies out → stable
\(-0.5\)\(1,\ -0.5,\ 0.25,\ -0.125,\ 0.06\)flips sign each sample, still dies out → stable
\(1\)\(1,\ 1,\ 1,\ 1,\ 1\)never dies → on the edge
\(1.2\)\(1,\ 1.2,\ 1.44,\ 1.73,\ 2.07\)grows forever → unstable

Only the size \(|p|\) decides whether \(p^k\) dies out, so the boundary is the circle \(|z|=1\). It is the same rule as "poles in the left half plane" in continuous time. A continuous pole \(s=\sigma+j\omega\) is sampled as \(z=e^{sT}\), whose size is \(|z|=e^{\sigma T}\). That is \(\lt1\) exactly when \(\sigma\lt0\) pp. 146–147.

Which poles? Those of the closed loop. With unity feedback (\(E=R-Y\), \(Y=GH\,E\)), solving for \(Y\) gives \(\dfrac{Y}{R}=\dfrac{GH}{1+GH}\). The closed-loop poles are the roots of \(1+GH(z)=0\). Write \(GH=N/D\) and multiply by \(D\): the characteristic equation is \(P(z)=D(z)+N(z)=0\). In Example 4.8 p. 174, \(D=(z-0.3679)(z-1)=z^2-1.3679z+0.3679\) and \(N=K(0.3679z+0.2642)\), so \(P(z)=z^2+(0.3679K-1.3679)z+(0.3679+0.2642K)\).

1.0.3 Why each Jury condition holds (\(n=2\)). Write the roots as \(z_1,z_2\), so \(P(z)=a_0(z-z_1)(z-z_2)\). Suppose both are inside the circle and see what that forces.

  • (1) \(|a_2|\lt a_0\). Expanding, \(a_2/a_0=z_1z_2\). Two numbers smaller than 1 in size have a product smaller than 1 in size.
  • (2) \(P(1)\gt0\). \(P(1)=a_0(1-z_1)(1-z_2)\). A real root inside the circle is \(\lt1\), so \(1-z_i\gt0\). A complex pair gives \((1-z_1)(1-\bar z_1)=|1-z_1|^2\gt0\). Either way the product is positive.
  • (3) \(P(-1)\) has sign \((-1)^n\). \(P(-1)=a_0(-1-z_1)(-1-z_2)=(-1)^2a_0(1+z_1)(1+z_2)\), and \((1+z_1)(1+z_2)\gt0\) by the same argument with \(-1\) in place of \(1\). For degree \(n\) you pull out \(n\) minus signs, hence "\(\gt0\) for even \(n\), \(\lt0\) for odd" p. 166.

Why these three are enough for \(n=2\). As \(K\) changes, the roots move continuously. To leave the circle they must cross it, and a 2nd-order polynomial with real coefficients can cross in only three ways. Each way switches one condition off:

From degree 3 on, a complex pair can cross while the product of all roots is still below 1 in size, so (1) no longer catches it. That is the job of the table rows in condition (4) pp. 164–165.

1.0.4 Worked: Example 4.8 pp. 174–175. \(P(z)=z^2+(0.3679K-1.3679)z+(0.3679+0.2642K)\), \(a_0=1\).

  • (1) \(|0.3679+0.2642K|\lt1\) → \(-5.1755\lt K\lt2.3925\).
  • (2) \(P(1)=1+0.3679K-1.3679+0.3679+0.2642K=0.6321K\gt0\) → \(K\gt0\).
  • (3) \(P(-1)=1-0.3679K+1.3679+0.3679+0.2642K=2.7358-0.1037K\gt0\) → \(K\lt26.38\).

Intersect them: \(0\lt K\lt2.3925\). Check with the picture. The upper limit came from (1), so at \(K=2.3925\) a complex pair should sit on the circle. Indeed \(P=z^2-0.4877z+1\) has roots \(0.2439\pm j0.9698\), of size \(\sqrt{0.2439^2+0.9698^2}=1.000\). At \(K=3\) the size is \(1.077\): unstable. The lower limit came from (2), so at \(K=0\) a root sits at \(z=+1\), which is the plant's own integrator pole with no feedback acting on it.

1.0.5 Steady-state error, derived. Once the loop is stable, ask how far \(y\) ends up from \(r\). The error is \(E=R-Y=R-GH\,E\), so \(E(z)=\dfrac{R(z)}{1+GH(z)}\). The final value theorem gives the end value of a sequence: \(e_{ss}=\lim_{z\to1}(1-z^{-1})E(z)\). It is \(z\to1\) because \(z=1\) is \(s=0\): zero frequency, "after everything has settled". Now insert each test input pp. 186–188:

  • Step \(R=\dfrac{1}{1-z^{-1}}\). The \((1-z^{-1})\) cancels: \(e_{ss}=\dfrac{1}{1+GH(1)}\). Call \(K_p=\lim_{z\to1}GH(z)\).
  • Ramp \(r(kT)=kT\), so \(R=\dfrac{Tz^{-1}}{(1-z^{-1})^2}\). One \((1-z^{-1})\) cancels and one is left: \(e_{ss}=\lim\dfrac{Tz^{-1}}{(1-z^{-1})+(1-z^{-1})GH}\). At \(z=1\) the first term in the denominator is 0, leaving \(\dfrac{T}{\lim(1-z^{-1})GH}\). Call \(K_v=\lim\dfrac{(1-z^{-1})GH}{T}\), so \(e_{ss}=1/K_v\).
  • Acceleration works the same way with two leftover factors and \(T^2\).

Where the \(1/T\) comes from. The sampled ramp is \(kT\), not \(k\). The \(T\) sits in \(R(z)\), and dividing by it makes \(K_v\) a "per second" quantity that matches the continuous-time \(K_v\). Why the \((1-z^{-1})\) factors matter. Each pole of \(GH\) at \(z=1\) is a discrete integrator. It contributes a factor \(\frac{1}{z-1}\) that cancels one \((1-z^{-1})=\frac{z-1}{z}\). The number of such poles is the system type:

1.0.6 Worked: Example 4.8 at \(K=1\), \(T=1\). It is stable, since \(0\lt1\lt2.3925\). \(G\) has one pole at \(z=1\), so it is type 1 and the step error is 0. For the ramp, \((1-z^{-1})G=\frac{z-1}{z}\cdot\frac{0.3679z+0.2642}{(z-0.3679)(z-1)}=\frac{0.3679z+0.2642}{z(z-0.3679)}\). The \((z-1)\) cancelled, so now set \(z=1\): \(\frac{0.6321}{0.6321}=1\). Divide by \(T=1\) to get \(K_v=1\), so the ramp error is \(e_{ss}=1\).

Verify by running the loop. Step the difference equation with \(r(k)=k\): \(e=0,\ 1,\ 1.63,\ 1.63,\ \dots,\ 0.99\) (\(k=20\)), \(1.000\) (\(k=39\)) ✓. The step error goes \(1,\ 0.63,\ 0,\ -0.15,\ \dots\to0\) ✓. Cross-check: this \(G\) is the ZOH version of \(\frac{K}{s(s+1)}\) at \(T=1\), whose continuous \(K_v=K=1\). Sampling with a ZOH keeps the DC behaviour, so the two agree.

Pitfall: the final value theorem only applies to a stable loop. At \(K=3\) the formula would give \(K_v=3\) and \(e_{ss}=0.33\), yet the loop is unstable and the error grows without bound. Always run Jury first.

Method to remember: \(P(z)=\) numerator of \(1+GH\) → Jury (1)–(3) for \(n=2\), plus the table for \(n\ge3\) → only if stable: count the poles of \(GH\) at \(z=1\) (the type) → cancel \((1-z^{-1})\) before setting \(z=1\) → divide by \(T\) or \(T^2\).

Check: a closed-loop pole pair at \(z=0.6\pm0.6j\). Each sample, that mode rotates by a fixed angle. How many samples does one full oscillation take?

\(p=0.6+0.6j\) has angle \(\theta=\arctan(0.6/0.6)=45^\circ\), so \(360^\circ/45^\circ=8\) samples per turn. Its size is \(\sqrt{0.6^2+0.6^2}=0.849\lt1\), so each turn also shrinks it (stable). That is the decaying-oscillation case \(r^k\cos(k\theta+\varphi)\).

Check: Example 4.8 with \(K=2\) (\(T=1\)). Find the steady-state error for a unit ramp.

First, stability: \(0\lt2\lt2.3925\) ✓. Next, \((1-z^{-1})G\to\frac{2(0.6321)}{0.6321}=2\), and \(T=1\), so \(K_v=2\) and \(e_{ss}=1/K_v=0.5\). The poles have size \(\sqrt{0.3679+0.5284}=0.947\), close to the circle, so the error approaches 0.5 slowly.

Idea. Sampling maps \(s\) to \(z=e^{sT}\). The left half s-plane lands inside the unit circle, so a sampled loop is stable iff every closed-loop pole has \(|z|\lt 1\). The Jury test checks this from the coefficients, without finding the roots pp. 146–147.

Jury, for \(P(z)=a_0z^n+\dots+a_n\) with \(a_0\gt0\) pp. 164–166: (1) \(|a_n|\lt a_0\); (2) \(P(1)\gt0\); (3) \(P(-1)\gt0\) if \(n\) is even, \(\lt0\) if \(n\) is odd; (4) the table rows \(|b_{n-1}|\gt|b_0|,\dots\). For \(n=2\) you only need (1)–(3).

Error constants (unity feedback, \(GH(z)\) includes the ZOH) pp. 186–187: \[K_p=\lim_{z\to1}GH(z),\quad K_v=\lim_{z\to1}\frac{(1-z^{-1})GH(z)}{T},\quad K_a=\lim_{z\to1}\frac{(1-z^{-1})^2GH(z)}{T^2},\] which give \(e_{ss}=\frac1{1+K_p},\ \frac1{K_v},\ \frac1{K_a}\) for a step, ramp and acceleration respectively.

Pitfall: \(K_v\) has a \(1/T\). Forget it and you are wrong by a factor of 10 at \(T=0.1\).

Quick checks

\(P(z)=z^2+(K-1.3)z+0.4\). What is the upper limit of \(K\) for stability?

\(|0.4|\lt1\) always holds. \(P(1)=K+0.1\gt0\) gives \(K\gt-0.1\). \(P(-1)=1-(K-1.3)+0.4=2.7-K\gt0\) gives \(K\lt2.7\).

\(GH(z)=\dfrac{0.1(z+0.9)}{(z-1)(z-0.6)}\), \(T=0.1\). Find \(K_v\).

\((1-z^{-1})GH=\frac{z-1}{z}GH\to\frac{0.1\cdot1.9}{1\cdot0.4}=0.475\). Divide by \(T\): \(K_v=4.75\), so the ramp error is \(1/4.75\approx0.21\).

2 · §5.2: Turning an analog controller into a digital one

Start here: why "replace \(s\)" works, where both mapping rules come from, and both lecture examples worked (about 10 minutes)

2.0.1 The picture. Think of cruise control. The car is a continuous (analog) system, but the controller is a few lines of code that run once every \(T\) seconds. Each time, the code reads the speed error \(e(k)\), computes a number \(u(k)\), and the ZOH holds that number until the next run. This is the loop on p. 13: digital controller → ZOH → plant, with the output sampled and fed back.

You already know how to design an analog controller \(C(s)\). Example 5.1 does exactly that for the car and gets the PI controller \(C(s)=1050+\frac{670}{s}\) pp. 9–11. The question in §5.2 is: what code makes the computer behave almost like that \(C(s)\)? The answer is the three-step procedure pp. 4–6: (1) design \(C(s)\) as usual; (2) map it to a digital \(C(z)\) (the slides also write \(G_D(z)\)); (3) simulate the sampled loop and repeat if the specs are missed. "Map" means approximate, and the approximation is good only when \(T\) is small compared with how fast the signals change. That is the warning on p. 6.

Why you cannot just keep \(s\). \(s\) means "differentiate" (and \(\frac1s\) means "integrate"). The computer never sees the whole curve \(e(t)\), only the samples \(e(0),e(T),e(2T),\dots\). So it cannot integrate exactly. It can only estimate the area under the curve from the samples. Each way of estimating that area gives a rule "replace \(s\) by some function of \(z\)". Bilinear is one such rule. Pole-zero matching takes a different route: it copies the poles and zeros exactly instead.

2.0.3 Bilinear (Tustin), derived pp. 7–8. Take the simplest controller, \(\dot y=-ay+ax\), i.e. \(C(s)=\frac{a}{s+a}\).

  1. Integrate over one sample period, from \((k-1)T\) to \(kT\). The left side integrates exactly: \(y(k)-y(k-1)=-a\!\int\!y\,dt+a\!\int\!x\,dt\). The integrals on the right are the part the computer cannot do exactly.
  2. Estimate each integral as a trapezoid. The area under \(y\) between two samples is approximately the width \(T\) times the average height: \(\int y\,dt\approx\frac T2\,[y(k)+y(k-1)]\). The same holds for \(x\).
  3. Substitute: \(y(k)-y(k-1)=\frac{aT}{2}\big(-[y(k)+y(k-1)]+[x(k)+x(k-1)]\big)\). This is already a difference equation, i.e. code.
  4. Take z-transforms, using \(y(k-1)\to z^{-1}Y\): \((1-z^{-1})Y=\frac{aT}{2}(1+z^{-1})(X-Y)\). Divide through by \(\frac T2(1+z^{-1})\) and solve for \(Y/X\): \[\frac{Y}{X}=\frac{a}{\dfrac{2}{T}\dfrac{1-z^{-1}}{1+z^{-1}}+a}.\]
  5. Compare with \(\frac{a}{s+a}\). It is the same expression with \(s\) replaced by \(\frac2T\frac{1-z^{-1}}{1+z^{-1}}=\frac2T\frac{z-1}{z+1}\). That is (5.4).

Why the rule works for every \(C(s)\), not just this one. Any rational \(C(s)\) can be built from integrators \(\frac1s\). Steps 2–4 say that the trapezoid rule turns one integrator into \(\frac1s\approx\frac T2\frac{z+1}{z-1}\). Replace every integrator this way and you have replaced every \(s\) by \(\frac2T\frac{z-1}{z+1}\).

Two points of the map are worth knowing. At \(z=1\), \(s=\frac2T\cdot\frac{0}{2}=0\), so the DC gain is kept: \(C(z{=}1)=C(s{=}0)\). At \(z=-1\), the denominator \(z+1\) is 0, so \(s=\infty\). That is why a bilinear result often has factors \((z+1)\) on top. Each one is an analog "zero at infinity" that has landed at \(z=-1\).

Worked: Example 5.1 pp. 12–13. \(C(s)=\frac{1050s+670}{s}\), \(T=0.1\), so \(\frac2T=20\) and \(s\to20\frac{z-1}{z+1}\). Multiply top and bottom by \((z+1)\) to clear the inner fractions: \[C(z)=\frac{1050\cdot20(z-1)+670(z+1)}{20(z-1)}=\frac{21670z-20330}{20(z-1)}=\frac{1083.5z-1016.5}{z-1}.\] The analog integrator (pole at \(s=0\)) became a pole at \(z=1\): it is still an integrator, now a discrete one. As code: write \(C(z)\) in powers of \(z^{-1}\), \(U(1-z^{-1})=(1083.5-1016.5z^{-1})E\), so \[u(k)=u(k-1)+1083.5\,e(k)-1016.5\,e(k-1).\] Check by running the loop (this \(u\), a ZOH, the plant \(\frac{1}{1000s+100}\), a step of 100). The speed peaks at about 118.6 km/h at \(t\approx2.8\) s and settles at 100. That matches the plot on p. 13, and the overshoot of 18.6 % meets the "under 20 %" spec. Step 3 of the procedure is passed.

2.0.4 Pole-zero matching, built from the picture pp. 14–15. Bilinear approximates integration and gets the poles roughly right. Pole-zero matching instead puts them exactly where sampling puts them. From §1: a mode \(e^{s_it}\) sampled at \(t=kT\) is \((e^{s_iT})^k\), which is the signature of a z-pole at \(e^{s_iT}\). So there are three steps:

  1. Map every finite pole and zero by \(z=e^{sT}\): an analog pole at \(b_i\) becomes the factor \((z-e^{b_iT})\) in the denominator, and a zero at \(a_i\) becomes \((z-e^{a_iT})\) in the numerator.
  2. Deal with the zeros at infinity. When \(n\gt m\), the analog gain falls to 0 as \(\omega\to\infty\). We say it has \(n-m\) zeros "at infinity". A sampled signal has no frequency above the folding frequency \(\omega_s/2\), i.e. \(\omega T=\pi\), which is \(z=e^{j\pi}=-1\). So \(z=-1\) is the digital "infinity", and the missing zeros go there as factors \((z+1)\). Add all \(n-m\) of them for a proper \(C(z)\) (numerator degree = denominator degree). Add \(n-m-1\) for a strictly proper one (numerator one degree lower). The slide formula on p. 15 uses the strictly proper version.
    Why you might want strictly proper: numerator degree \(\lt\) denominator degree means \(u(k)\) depends only on \(e(k-1)\) and older. The computer then has a full sample period to do the calculation before it must output \(u\).
  3. Fix the overall height with \(\alpha\). Steps 1–2 fix the shape of the response but not its size. Choose \(\alpha\) so that the gains agree at the frequency you care about most. For a low-pass filter or controller that is DC, \(s=0\leftrightarrow z=1\): set \(G(1)=G_a(0)\).

Worked: Example 5.2 pp. 16–18. \(G_a(s)=\frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}\) with \(\zeta=0.5\), \(\omega_n=5\) rad/s, \(T=0.1\) s.

  • Poles are the roots of the denominator \(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2=0\). The quadratic formula gives \(s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\). With \(\zeta\lt1\) the root is of a negative number, so write it as \(j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\): \(s_{1,2}=-\zeta\omega_n\pm j\omega_d\) (the slide's "\(\varsigma\)" is \(\zeta\)), with \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}=5\sqrt{0.75}=4.33\) rad/s. So \(s_{1,2}=-2.5\pm j4.33\). There are no finite zeros, so \(n=2\), \(m=0\).
  • Zeros at \(-1\): strictly proper, so \(n-m-1=1\) factor \((z+1)\).
  • Denominator (p. 18): expand \((z-e^{s_1T})(z-e^{s_2T})=z^2-(e^{s_1T}+e^{s_2T})z+e^{s_1T}e^{s_2T}\). The sum is \(e^{-\zeta\omega_nT}(e^{j\omega_dT}+e^{-j\omega_dT})=2e^{-\zeta\omega_nT}\cos(\omega_dT)\) (Euler: \(e^{j\theta}+e^{-j\theta}=2\cos\theta\)). The product is \(e^{(s_1+s_2)T}=e^{-2\zeta\omega_nT}\), because the \(\pm j\omega_d\) parts cancel. The result is real, as it must be. In numbers: \(\zeta\omega_nT=0.25\) and \(\omega_dT=0.433\) rad, so \(2e^{-0.25}\cos(0.433)=2(0.7788)(0.9077)=1.4138\) and \(e^{-0.5}=0.6065\).
  • \(\alpha\): \(G_a(0)=\omega_n^2/\omega_n^2=1\). \(G(1)=\frac{\alpha(1+1)}{1-1.4138+0.6065}=\frac{2\alpha}{0.1927}=1\), so \(\alpha=0.0963\). The slide's 0.09625 comes from the rounded coefficients 1.414 and 0.6065.
\[G(z)=\frac{0.0963(z+1)}{z^2-1.414z+0.6065}.\]

Both rules keep the DC gain, both keep a stable pole stable, and they agree as \(T\to0\). They part ways as \(T\) grows, which is the reason for "works when \(T\) is sufficiently small" (p. 6). The other visible difference is the zeros: bilinear produces the \((z+1)\) factors automatically (here one, a proper result), while matching adds only as many as you choose (none here if strictly proper).

Pitfalls. (a) Bilinear: keep the \(\frac2T\) and multiply top and bottom by \((z+1)^{\text{power}}\) before collecting terms; a dropped \((z+1)\) is the usual algebra slip. (b) Matching: \(\alpha\) is set at \(z=1\) (digital DC), against \(s=0\) (analog DC), never \(z=0\). (c) Count zeros at \(-1\) with \(n-m-1\) for strictly proper: \(\frac{a}{s+a}\) gets none. (d) Complex poles: the cosine takes \(\omega_dT\) (damped frequency), not \(\omega_nT\), and the constant term is \(e^{-2\zeta\omega_nT}\), the squared radius.

4 · §5.4: Direct design from a desired \(G_{cl}(z)\); deadbeat

Start here if "closed loop" means nothing to you yet (about 5 minutes)

4.0.1 The loop. Think of cruise control. You set a target speed. A computer measures the real speed, compares, adjusts the throttle, measures again, and so on forever. The measured output is fed back to the decision, so the signal path forms a circle: a closed loop (feedback control). This is the loop of p. 49:

Signal / boxMeaning
\(R\)reference: what you want the output to be
\(Y\)output: what you actually get
\(E=R-Y\)error: how far off you are right now
\(C(z)\)controller: the program on the computer. You design this.
\(U\)control signal the computer sends out
\(G_{ZAS}(z)\)the plant as the computer sees it: zero-order hold (DAC) → analog plant \(G(s)\) → sampler (ADC), i.e. \(G_{ZAS}(z)=(1-z^{-1})\,\mathcal Z\{G(s)/s\}\). Given; you cannot change it.

Read \(z^{-1}\) as "one sample ago": a block equal to \(z^{-1}\) outputs its input delayed by one sample period \(T\).

4.0.2 Why \(G_{cl}=\frac{CG}{1+CG}\). \(G_{cl}\) is the whole circle seen as one box, from what you ask for to what you get: \(G_{cl}=Y/R\). Write \(G\) for \(G_{ZAS}\) and walk around the loop:

\[Y=CG\,E,\qquad E=R-Y\quad\Rightarrow\quad Y=CG(R-Y)\] \[\Rightarrow\quad Y(1+CG)=CG\,R\quad\Rightarrow\quad G_{cl}=\frac{Y}{R}=\frac{CG}{1+CG}.\]

If \(G_{cl}\) were exactly 1, the output would copy the reference perfectly.

4.0.3 Direct design runs that backwards. Usually you guess \(C\) and see what \(G_{cl}\) you get. Here you pick the \(G_{cl}\) you want and solve the same equation for \(C\):

\[G_{cl}(1+CG)=CG\;\Rightarrow\;G_{cl}=CG\,(1-G_{cl})\;\Rightarrow\;C=\frac{1}{G}\,\frac{G_{cl}}{1-G_{cl}}.\]

That is all (5.11) is. Notice the factor \(1/G\): the controller tries to undo the plant. Each of the four conditions below marks a case where undoing the plant is impossible or dangerous:

  • (i) Causality. If the plant needs \(d\) samples before its output reacts at all, no controller can make the loop react sooner; that would need tomorrow's measurement. So \(G_{cl}\) must keep at least that much delay.
  • (ii) Zeros outside the unit circle. \(1/G\) turns every plant zero into a pole of \(C\). A zero at, say, \(z=-1.5\) would give \(C\) an unstable pole, so \(U\) grows without bound even while \(Y\) looks fine on paper. The cure is to keep that zero inside \(G_{cl}\) so \(C\) never has to cancel it.
  • (iii) Unstable plant poles. Mirror image: \(C\) would need a zero exactly on top of the unstable pole. Any small mismatch leaves something exploding inside the loop. The cure is to put that pole into \(1-G_{cl}\).
  • (iv) \(G_{cl}(1)=1\). Setting \(z=1\) gives the steady-state gain. Gain 1 means that after a step, \(Y\) ends up exactly equal to \(R\).

"Input-output stable but not asymptotically stable" means exactly the failure in (ii)/(iii). The \(R\to Y\) formula looks stable, but a signal hidden inside the loop (for example \(U\)) diverges.

4.0.4 Deadbeat, worked. Deadbeat picks the most aggressive target, \(G_{cl}=z^{-m}\): "the output equals the reference, \(m\) samples late", with no overshoot and no slow tail. Take \(G_{ZAS}=\dfrac{0.5}{z-0.5}\). The pole \(0.5\) is inside the circle and there are no zeros, so deadbeat is allowed. The relative degree is \(1-0=1\), so the fastest choice is \(G_{cl}=z^{-1}=1/z\). Then

\[C=\frac{z-0.5}{0.5}\cdot\frac{1/z}{1-1/z}=\frac{z-0.5}{0.5}\cdot\frac{1}{z-1}=\frac{2(z-0.5)}{z-1}.\]

Verify. \(CG=\frac{2(z-0.5)}{z-1}\cdot\frac{0.5}{z-0.5}=\frac{1}{z-1}\), so \(G_{cl}=\frac{1/(z-1)}{1+1/(z-1)}=\frac1z\) ✓. For a unit step \(r=1,1,1,\dots\) the output is \(y=0,1,1,1,\dots\): exactly on target from the first sample on. \(C\) has a pole at \(z=1\). That is a discrete integrator, and it is what delivers condition (iv).

Method to remember: identify \(G_{ZAS}\) → pick \(G_{cl}\) (deadbeat: \(z^{-m}\)) → check (i)–(iv) → \(C=\frac{1}{G_{ZAS}}\frac{G_{cl}}{1-G_{cl}}\) → verify by recomputing \(\frac{CG}{1+CG}\).

Check: \(G_{ZAS}=\dfrac{0.2}{z^2-0.3z}\). What is the smallest \(m\) allowed for a deadbeat \(G_{cl}=z^{-m}\)?

Denominator degree 2, numerator degree 0, so the relative degree is 2: the plant needs two samples to respond. The fastest deadbeat is \(G_{cl}=z^{-2}\). The poles (0 and 0.3) are inside the circle and there are no zeros, so deadbeat is allowed.

Idea. Choose the closed loop you want, then solve for the controller. From \(G_{cl}=\frac{CG}{1+CG}\) pp. 49–50: \[C(z)=\frac{1}{G_{ZAS}(z)}\,\frac{G_{cl}(z)}{1-G_{cl}(z)}\qquad(5.11)\]

Four conditions on \(G_{cl}\) pp. 51–56: (i) relative degree of \(G_{cl}\) ≥ that of \(G_{ZAS}\) (causality); (ii) \(G_{cl}\) contains every zero of \(G_{ZAS}\) outside the unit circle; (iii) \(1-G_{cl}\) contains every unstable pole of \(G_{ZAS}\); (iv) \(G_{cl}(1)=1\) (zero step error). Conditions (ii) and (iii) exist because \(C\) would otherwise cancel an unstable zero or pole. The loop would then look input-output stable but would not be asymptotically stable.

Choosing \(G_{cl}\) p. 57: pick \(T_s\) and the overshoot, choose a unit-gain 1st/2nd-order \(G_{cl}(s)\), map its poles by \(z=e^{sT}\), then check (i)–(iv). A plant delay \(z^{-5}\) forces \(z^{-5}\) into \(G_{cl}\) (Example 5.7).

Count the relative degree in powers of \(z\), after clearing every \(z^{-1}\): (degree of denominator) − (degree of numerator).

Got a negative or odd \(m\)? Why you cannot count the \(z^{-1}\) powers

\(m\) is a delay. The relative degree is the number of samples before anything you put into the plant appears at its output. A negative \(m\) would mean the output reacts before the input arrives, so it is always a counting mistake.

The trap. The same \(G\) can be written in \(z\)-form (positive powers) or \(z^{-1}\)-form (delay powers). "(degree of denominator) − (degree of numerator)" is defined only in the \(z\)-form. Taking the highest \(z^{-1}\) powers above (3 on top, 2 below) and subtracting gives \(2-3=-1\). Those powers describe how far back the system remembers, not how soon it reacts.

Method 1: convert. Multiply the top and bottom by \(z^3\), the highest \(z^{-1}\) power present:

\[\text{top: }0.05z^{-2}(1+0.7z^{-1})\cdot z^3=0.05(z+0.7),\] \[\text{bottom: }(1-0.9z^{-1})(1-0.5z^{-1})\cdot z^3=z(z-0.9)(z-0.5),\]

so the degrees are 1 and 3 and \(m=3-1=2\).

Method 2: read the front. Every factor \((1+a z^{-1})\) starts with 1, so it adds no delay. Only a bare \(z^{-k}\) out front adds delay; here it is \(z^{-2}\), so \(m=2\). (Subtract the \(k\) of a bare \(z^{-k}\) in the denominator, if there is one.) Why this works: for large \(z\), \(z^{-1}\to0\), every \((1+az^{-1})\to1\), and \(G\approx0.05z^{-2}\).

Verify with signals. The denominator expands to \(1-1.4z^{-1}+0.45z^{-2}\), so \(y(k)=1.4y(k-1)-0.45y(k-2)+0.05u(k-2)+0.035u(k-3)\). A single pulse \(u(0)=1\) gives \(y(0)=0,\ y(1)=0,\ y(2)=0.05\). The first reaction comes 2 samples late, which confirms \(m=2\).

Check: \(G=\dfrac{0.2z^{-2}+0.1z^{-4}}{1-0.5z^{-1}}\). What is the smallest allowed deadbeat \(m\)?

The bare \(z^{-2}\) out front means 2 samples of delay. In \(z\)-form, multiplying by \(z^4\) gives \(\frac{0.2z^2+0.1}{z^3(z-0.5)}\), so the degrees are 4 and 2 and \(m=2\). The trap gives \(1-4=-3\).

If \(G_{ZAS}\) had a zero at \(z=-1.5\), could you still use \(G_{cl}=z^{-m}\)?

No. Condition (ii) makes \(G_{cl}\) contain \((z+1.5)\); otherwise \(C\) has a pole at −1.5 and the control signal blows up.

5 · §5.4.3: Ripple-free deadbeat

Start here: what "ripple" is and why the fix works (about 6 minutes)

5.0.1 The problem: the computer only looks at sample instants. It measures \(y\) at \(t=0,T,2T,\dots\) and nowhere in between. The real plant is continuous, so it keeps moving between those instants. A design can hit the target at every sample and still swing wildly in between. That swinging is ripple (intersample oscillation).

Sketch, not to scale. Dots are what the computer sees; the curve is the real continuous output.

5.0.2 Where the ripple comes from (Example 5.8, pp. 66–67). The plant is \(G_{ZAS}=1.8604\times10^{-4}\frac{z+0.9293}{(z-0.8187)(z-0.9802)}\). Everything is inside the circle, so §4's plain deadbeat \(G_{cl}=z^{-1}\) is allowed. Look at the controller it produces:

\[C(z)=\frac{5375.05\,(z-0.8187)(z-0.9802)}{(z-1)(z+0.9293)}.\]

The factor \(1/G_{ZAS}\) turned the plant zero at \(-0.9293\) into a controller pole at \(-0.9293\). A pole at \(p\) contributes a term \(p^k\) to the signal, and \((-0.93)^k=1,-0.93,+0.86,-0.80,\dots\) flips sign every sample and dies away very slowly. So the control signal is

\[u(k)=5375,\ -9289,\ +8652,\ -8021,\ +7473,\ \dots\]

That is a huge input slammed up and down every period. Why do the samples of \(y\) still look perfect? In \(CG\) the controller pole at \(-0.9293\) is cancelled by the plant zero at \(-0.9293\). The cancelled mode is invisible at the sample instants, but the continuous plant is still being shaken between them. This is the same "hidden mode" story as §4's conditions (ii)/(iii). Here the hidden mode is stable, so nothing blows up, but it decays so slowly and alternates so hard that the output ripples. A zero near \(-1\) is the warning sign.

5.0.3 The cure in one sentence. If \(u(k)\) becomes constant after a few samples, the plant sees a constant input from then on, settles, and has nothing left to ripple about pp. 73–74. "A few" is \(n\) = degree of the plant's denominator. Which constant?

  • Type 1 plant (has a \(1-z^{-1}\), i.e. an integrator, as in position control): \(u\) must go to 0. A non-zero constant into an integrator makes the output ramp away.
  • Type 0 plant (no integrator, as in speed control): \(u\) must settle at a non-zero constant, namely the input needed to hold \(y=1\), which is \(1/\text{DC gain}\).

5.0.4 Turning that into a rule for \(G_{cl}\). The plant links \(U\) to \(Y\) by \(Y=G_{ZAS}U\), so \(U=Y/G_{ZAS}\). With \(Y=G_{cl}R\):

\[U(z)=G_{cl}(z)\,\frac{R(z)}{G_{ZAS}(z)}\qquad(5.13)\]

"\(u\) settles in finitely many steps" means \(U(z)\) has to come out as a finite polynomial in \(z^{-1}\), plus at most a constant tail. Now look at what \(1/G_{ZAS}\) puts in the denominator of \(U\): every plant zero. Each leftover factor \((1+bz^{-1})\) in a denominator creates an endless \((-b)^k\) tail in \(u\), which is exactly the ripple source. The only way to remove them is to let \(G_{cl}\) contain all the zeros of \(G_{ZAS}\), stable ones included. Plain deadbeat only had to keep the unstable zeros; ripple-free keeps them all. Hence the recipe:

\[G_{cl}=K\,z^{-d}\times(\text{every zero factor of }G_{ZAS}),\qquad G_{cl}(1)=1\ \text{fixes }K,\]

with \(d\) = the plant's delay (its relative degree, as in §4).

6 · §5.5–5.7: One vs two degrees of freedom; pole placement

Start here: what a "degree of freedom" is, and pole placement built from zero (about 8 minutes)

6.0.1 The picture: one knob vs two knobs. Back to cruise control. The controller has two separate jobs. Job 1, tracking: when you raise the set speed from 80 to 100, the car should reach 100 smoothly, without a jolt. Job 2, disturbance rejection: when the car hits a hill, which you did not ask for, it should fight back hard and fast. A good design wants a gentle response to the first and an aggressive response to the second.

In the §4 loop the controller \(C\) only ever sees the error \(e=r-y\). A setpoint step and a hill both arrive as "the error just changed", so \(C\) cannot treat them differently. One block means one knob: a one degree of freedom (1-DOF) controller. The fix is to let the reference and the measurement enter through different blocks. That gives the two degrees of freedom (2-DOF) structure of Figure 5.17 p. 85:

6.0.2 Why 1-DOF has only one knob, in algebra p. 84. With one controller \(G_c\) on the error and \(v\) added at the output: \(y=GG_c(r-y)+v\). Collect the \(y\) terms: \(y(1+GG_c)=GG_c\,r+v\), so

\[y=\underbrace{\frac{GG_c}{1+GG_c}}_{T}\,r+\underbrace{\frac{1}{1+GG_c}}_{S}\,v,\qquad S+T=\frac{1+GG_c}{1+GG_c}=1.\]

You want \(T\approx1\) (copy \(r\)) and \(S\approx0\) (ignore \(v\)). These two wishes agree. The trouble is that you cannot shape them separately: choosing how \(y\) reacts to \(r\) (that is, \(T\)) also fixes \(S=1-T\), so it fixes how \(y\) reacts to \(v\). You get one free function, not two.

6.0.3 Deriving the 2-DOF output (5.16) pp. 86–87. The controller is \(U=\gamma\frac{T_c}{R_c}R-\frac{S_c}{R_c}Y\). The plant says \(Y=z^{-k}\frac{B}{A}U+V\). Multiply the plant equation by \(A\) and by \(R_c\) to clear both fractions, then substitute \(R_cU=\gamma T_cR-S_cY\):

\[R_cA\,Y=z^{-k}B\,(R_cU)+R_cA\,V=z^{-k}B(\gamma T_cR-S_cY)+R_cA\,V.\]

Move the \(Y\) term to the left:

\[\big(R_cA+z^{-k}BS_c\big)\,Y=z^{-k}\gamma BT_c\,R+AR_c\,V\] \[\Rightarrow\quad Y=\frac{z^{-k}\gamma BT_c}{R_cA+z^{-k}BS_c}\,R+\frac{AR_c}{R_cA+z^{-k}BS_c}\,V.\qquad(5.16)\]

Read this formula carefully. The shared denominator \(R_cA+z^{-k}BS_c\) does not contain \(T_c\) or \(\gamma\). So:

  • Knob 1, \(R_c\) and \(S_c\) (the feedback block): sets the poles, so it sets stability and how hard \(v\) is rejected.
  • Knob 2, \(T_c\) and \(\gamma\) (the feedforward block): appear only in the \(R\) numerator. You can reshape the tracking response freely without touching stability or disturbance rejection.

Those are the two degrees of freedom.

6.0.4 Pole placement: decide the answer first pp. 89–90. As in §4, pick the closed loop you want, then solve for the controller. The target is \(G_{cl}=\gamma z^{-k}\frac{B_r}{\phi_{cl}}\) (5.18). Here you choose the poles, so \(\phi_{cl}\) is yours. \(\gamma=\phi_{cl}(1)/B_r(1)\) (5.19) makes \(G_{cl}(1)=1\), so after a step \(y(\infty)=r(\infty)\). That is the same condition (iv) as in §4. Setting \(V=0\) in (5.16) and matching it to the target gives

\[\frac{BT_c}{AR_c+z^{-k}BS_c}=\frac{B_r}{\phi_{cl}}.\qquad(5.21)\]

6.0.5 Why split into "good" and "bad" factors pp. 91–92. A factor is good if its root is well inside the unit circle and well damped. It is bad if the root is outside the circle, or inside but close to it or poorly damped. Write \(B=B^gB^b\) and \(A=A^gA^b\).

  • Good factors may be cancelled. Put \(B^g\) into the controller's denominator and \(A^g\) into its numerators: \(R_c=B^gR_1\), \(S_c=A^gS_1\), \(T_c=A^gT_1\) (5.22). Cancelling a pole with a zero leaves that mode hidden inside the loop (§4's "not asymptotically stable" story). That is harmless only when the hidden mode dies out quickly by itself, so only good factors may be cancelled.
  • Bad factors may not be cancelled. Cancelling the bad zero \(B^b\) would need a controller pole on top of it, which is an unstable or ringing hidden mode (exactly the §5 ripple story). Cancelling the bad pole \(A^b\) would need an exact controller zero on it, and any mismatch leaves something exploding. So \(A^b\) and \(B^b\) are left in, and feedback has to deal with them.

Substitute (5.22) into (5.21). Every term then contains \(A^gB^g\), so it cancels:

\[\frac{B^gB^bA^gT_1}{A^gB^g\big(A^bR_1+z^{-k}B^bS_1\big)}=\frac{B^bT_1}{A^bR_1+z^{-k}B^bS_1}=\frac{B_r}{\phi_{cl}}.\]

Match the numerators and the denominators separately:

\[B_r=B^bT_1\quad(5.23),\qquad A^bR_1+z^{-k}B^bS_1=\phi_{cl}\quad(5.24).\]

Equation (5.24) is the heart of the method. The bad plant poles \(A^b\) are on the left, the poles you want are on the right, and \(R_1,S_1\) are the unknowns that turn one into the other. This is how feedback moves bad poles: it never cancels them. It mixes them with \(z^{-k}B^bS_1\) until the sum has the roots you chose.

6.0.6 Why "bad zeros cannot be moved" p. 93. With the simple choice \(T_1=1\) (5.25), (5.23) gives \(B_r=B^b\), so

\[G_Y=\gamma z^{-k}\frac{B^b}{\phi_{cl}},\qquad\gamma=\frac{\phi_{cl}(1)}{B^b(1)}\quad(5.26).\]

The poles are now whatever you put in \(\phi_{cl}\), but the bad zeros \(B^b\) are still sitting in the numerator. You could only remove a zero by cancelling it, and 6.0.5 just ruled that out. Feedback can move poles; it cannot move bad zeros. The other option on p. 92, \(T_1=S_1\), makes \(T_c=S_c\). Then \(u=\frac{S_c}{R_c}(\gamma r-y)\) acts on one error signal again, and you are back to 1-DOF.

6.0.7 Worked example (numbers are this page's own). Take \(k=1\) and

\[G=z^{-1}\frac{1+2z^{-1}}{(1-0.5z^{-1})(1-2z^{-1})}.\]
  1. Classify. Pole \(0.5\): good, so \(A^g=1-0.5z^{-1}\). Pole \(2\): outside the circle, bad, so \(A^b=1-2z^{-1}\). Zero \(-2\): outside, bad, so \(B^b=1+2z^{-1}\) and \(B^g=1\).
  2. Choose the poles. Put them at \(0.4\) and \(0.6\): \(\phi_{cl}=(1-0.4z^{-1})(1-0.6z^{-1})=1-z^{-1}+0.24z^{-2}\).
  3. Pick the sizes of \(R_1,S_1\) (supplementary rule, not on the slides): make the number of unknowns equal the number of equations. The left of (5.24) has degree 2 when \(R_1=1+r_1z^{-1}\) and \(S_1=s_0\). That gives two unknowns \((r_1,s_0)\) for the two coefficients of \(z^{-1}\) and \(z^{-2}\). The constant term is \(1=1\) automatically.
  4. Expand (5.24) and match coefficients: \[(1-2z^{-1})(1+r_1z^{-1})+z^{-1}(1+2z^{-1})s_0=1+(r_1-2+s_0)z^{-1}+(-2r_1+2s_0)z^{-2}.\] Match \(z^{-1}\): \(r_1+s_0-2=-1\), so \(r_1+s_0=1\). Match \(z^{-2}\): \(2s_0-2r_1=0.24\), so \(s_0-r_1=0.12\). Together they give \(s_0=0.56\) and \(r_1=0.44\).
  5. \(\gamma\) from (5.26): \(\phi_{cl}(1)=1-1+0.24=0.24\) and \(B^b(1)=3\), so \(\gamma=0.08\).
  6. Controller: \(G_b=\dfrac{S_c}{R_c}=\dfrac{0.56(1-0.5z^{-1})}{1+0.44z^{-1}}\) and \(G_f=\gamma\dfrac{T_c}{R_c}=\dfrac{0.08(1-0.5z^{-1})}{1+0.44z^{-1}}\). The controller's own pole, \(-0.44\), is safely inside the circle.

Verify. The full loop denominator is \(R_cA+z^{-1}BS_c=(1-0.5z^{-1})\,\phi_{cl}\). That is the poles you chose, plus the hidden good pole \(0.5\), which dies out on its own. The unstable pole at 2 is gone. \(G_Y=\dfrac{0.08z^{-1}(1+2z^{-1})}{1-z^{-1}+0.24z^{-2}}\) has \(G_Y(1)=0.08\cdot3/0.24=1\) ✓. The step response is \(y=0,\ 0.08,\ 0.32,\ 0.54,\ 0.70,\ 0.81,\dots\to1\). The input settles at \(u(\infty)=-1/6\). Why negative? The plant's DC gain is \(G(1)=\frac{3}{(0.5)(-1)}=-6\), and holding \(y=1\) needs \(u=1/(-6)\). The bad zero \((1+2z^{-1})\) is still in \(G_Y\), as 6.0.6 said it must be.

Method to remember: factor \(A,B\) into good and bad → choose \(\phi_{cl}\) from the poles you want → solve \(A^bR_1+z^{-k}B^bS_1=\phi_{cl}\) by matching coefficients (unknowns = equations) → \(\gamma=\phi_{cl}(1)/B^b(1)\) → \(R_c=B^gR_1,\ S_c=A^gS_1,\ T_c=A^g\) → check \(G_Y(1)=1\).

Check: same plant, but now you want both poles at \(0.5\): \(\phi_{cl}=(1-0.5z^{-1})^2=1-z^{-1}+0.25z^{-2}\). With \(R_1=1+r_1z^{-1}\) and \(S_1=s_0\), find \(s_0\).

The matched coefficients are \(r_1+s_0=1\) and \(2s_0-2r_1=0.25\), so \(s_0-r_1=0.125\). That gives \(s_0=0.5625\) and \(r_1=0.4375\).

Same design with \(T_1=1\). Find \(\gamma\).

\(\gamma=\phi_{cl}(1)/B^b(1)=0.25/3=1/12\approx0.0833\). Use \(B^b\), not the whole \(B\). Here they are equal because \(B^g=1\).

1-DOF p. 84: \(y=Tr+Sv\) with \(T=\frac{GG_c}{1+GG_c}\) and \(S=\frac{1}{1+GG_c}\). Because \(S+T=1\), fixing one fixes the other.

2-DOF pp. 85–87: \(u=\gamma\frac{T_c}{R_c}r-\frac{S_c}{R_c}y\) and plant \(G=z^{-k}B/A\) give \[Y=\frac{z^{-k}\gamma BT_c}{R_cA+z^{-k}BS_c}R+\frac{AR_c}{R_cA+z^{-k}BS_c}V.\] The denominator sets stability, the second term should be small (disturbance rejection), and the first should be close to 1 (tracking).

Pole placement pp. 89–93: split \(B=B^gB^b\), \(A=A^gA^b\) into good and bad factors (bad = outside the circle or poorly damped). Set \(R_c=B^gR_1\), \(S_c=A^gS_1\), \(T_c=A^gT_1\). Then solve \[A^bR_1+z^{-k}B^bS_1=\phi_{cl}\qquad(5.24)\] for \(R_1,S_1\) by matching coefficients. With \(T_1=1\): \(\gamma=\phi_{cl}(1)/B^b(1)\) and \(G_Y=\gamma z^{-k}B^b/\phi_{cl}\). Bad zeros cannot be moved by feedback.

Quick checks

\(\phi_{cl}=1-1.2z^{-1}+0.45z^{-2}\) and \(B^b=1+1.5z^{-1}\). With \(T_1=1\), find \(\gamma\).

\(\phi_{cl}(1)=0.25\) and \(B^b(1)=2.5\), so \(\gamma=0.1\).

Which choice of \(T_1\) collapses the 2-DOF controller back to 1-DOF?

With \(T_1=S_1\), \(T_c=S_c\) and \(u=\frac{S_c}{R_c}(\gamma r-y)\): one block acting on the error (p. 92).

7 · Ch 6: Implementing \(C(z)\) in software

Write \(C(z)=\frac{b_0+b_1z^{-1}+\dots+b_mz^{-m}}{1+a_1z^{-1}+\dots+a_nz^{-n}}\) (6.1, \(n\ge m\)) pp. 104–105.

Direct programming (Fig 6.1): \(y(k)=-a_1y(k-1)-\dots-a_ny(k-n)+b_0x(k)+\dots+b_mx(k-m)\). It uses \(n+m\) delays pp. 106–107.

Standard programming (Fig 6.2) pp. 108–110: split through an internal signal \(H\): \[h(k)=x(k)-a_1h(k-1)-\dots-a_nh(k-n),\] \[y(k)=b_0h(k)+b_1h(k-1)+\dots+b_mh(k-m).\] It needs only \(n\) delays, which saves memory. The \(a_i\) are feedback gains and the \(b_i\) feed-forward gains.

Coefficient quantization pp. 116–119: \(|\Delta p_i|=\left|\sum_j\frac{p_i^{\,n-j}}{\prod_{k\ne i}(p_i-p_k)}\Delta a_j\right|\). Tightly clustered poles make the denominator small and the error large. The fix is to break \(C\) into 1st/2nd-order sections: series \(C=\prod G_i\), grouping real pairs or conjugate pairs; or parallel \(C=A+\sum G_i\) by partial fractions pp. 120–125.

Sign trap: (6.1) has \(+a_i\) in the denominator, but the recursion uses \(-a_i\). For \(1-0.5z^{-1}\) the recursion term is \(+0.5\,y(k-1)\).
