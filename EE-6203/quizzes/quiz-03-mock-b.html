Question 1 10 marks

A unity-feedback digital loop (sampler, ZOH, plant) has open-loop pulse transfer function \[G(z)=\frac{K(0.4261z+0.3608)}{(z-1)(z-0.6065)},\qquad T=1\ \text{s}.\] 7(a) Find the closed-loop characteristic equation and the range of \(K\) for stability.
3(b) Find \(K_v\) as a function of \(K\), and the steady-state error to a unit ramp when \(K=0.5\).

This is the same kind of problem as Example 4.8 pp. 174–175.

Part (a): characteristic equation and K-range

Translate the question

Think first: which equation do the closed-loop poles satisfy, and what does "stable" mean for them in the z-plane?

Here \(G(z)\) already includes the ZOH, so nothing needs to be sampled. With unity feedback the closed loop is \(\dfrac{G}{1+G}\). Its poles are the roots of

\[1+G(z)=0.\]

A sampled system is stable when every closed-loop pole lies strictly inside the unit circle, \(|z|\lt1\). So the task is: for which K are both roots of \(1+G=0\) inside the unit circle?

\(z=e^{sT}\) sends the left half s-plane to the inside of the unit circle, so "\(|z|\lt1\)" plays the role that "Re \(s\lt0\)" plays for continuous systems.

Build the characteristic polynomial P(z)

Think first: \(1+G=0\) still contains a fraction. What do you multiply by?

Multiply \(1+G=0\) by the denominator \((z-1)(z-0.6065)\):

\[(z-1)(z-0.6065)+K(0.4261z+0.3608)=0.\]

Expand the first product: \((z-1)(z-0.6065)=z^2-1.6065z+0.6065\). Then collect powers of \(z\):

\[P(z)=z^2+(0.4261K-1.6065)\,z+(0.6065+0.3608K)=0.\]

Label the coefficients as in the Jury test: \(a_0=1\), \(a_1=0.4261K-1.6065\), \(a_2=0.6065+0.3608K\). The degree is \(n=2\).

The roots of P are exactly the closed-loop poles. Each coefficient is linear in K, so every Jury condition will come out as a simple inequality in K.

Common slip: writing \((z-1)(z-0.6065)+K=0\). The K multiplies the whole numerator \(0.4261z+0.3608\), so K appears in both \(a_1\) and \(a_2\).
Choose the test: Jury for n = 2

Think first: how many Jury conditions does a 2nd-order polynomial need, and what are they?

For \(P(z)=a_0z^n+\dots+a_n\) with \(a_0\gt0\) pp. 164–166:

(1) \(|a_n|\lt a_0\);   (2) \(P(1)\gt0\);   (3) \(P(-1)\gt0\) for even \(n\) (\(\lt0\) for odd \(n\));   (4) the table rows.

For \(n=2\) the table adds nothing, so conditions (1)–(3) are the whole test.

Finding the roots for every K would be slow. Jury reads stability straight from the coefficients, which is what an exam with a free parameter K needs.

Condition (1): |a₂| < a₀

Think first: write the double inequality and solve both sides for K.

\[-1\lt 0.6065+0.3608K\lt 1\]

Right side: \(0.3608K\lt 0.3935\Rightarrow K\lt 1.0905\). Left side: \(0.3608K\gt-1.6065\Rightarrow K\gt-4.452\).

\[\Rightarrow\quad -4.452\lt K\lt 1.0905.\]

For a quadratic with \(a_0=1\), \(a_2\) is the product of the two roots. If the roots are a complex pair, \(a_2=|z|^2\), so \(|a_2|\lt1\) is exactly "the pair is inside the circle".

Condition (2): P(1) > 0

Think first: substitute \(z=1\) and collect the K terms.

\[P(1)=1+(0.4261K-1.6065)+(0.6065+0.3608K)=0.7869K\gt0\ \Rightarrow\ K\gt0.\]

The constants cancel: \(1-1.6065+0.6065=0\). That always happens here, because the open loop has a pole at \(z=1\).

P(z) is positive for large real z. If \(P(1)\le0\), P has to cross zero somewhere at \(z\ge1\), which means a real root on or outside the circle.

Condition (3): P(−1) > 0 (n is even)

Think first: which sign do you need for n = 2? Then substitute \(z=-1\).

\[P(-1)=1-(0.4261K-1.6065)+(0.6065+0.3608K)=3.2130-0.0653K\gt0\ \Rightarrow\ K\lt 49.2.\]

This is the same idea at the other end of the real axis: it rules out a real root at \(z\le-1\). For even n, \((-1)^n=+1\), so the requirement is \(P(-1)\gt0\).

Intersect the three ranges, then sanity-check

Think first: which of the three conditions is the tightest at each end?

The conditions give \((-4.452,\,1.0905)\), \((0,\infty)\) and \((-\infty,\,49.2)\). Their overlap is

\[\boxed{0\lt K\lt 1.0905}\]

The lower end comes from \(P(1)\) and the upper end from \(|a_2|\lt1\).

Check by sampling: take \(K=0.5\), inside the range. Then \(P=z^2-1.3935z+0.7869\). The discriminant \(1.3935^2-4(0.7869)\lt0\), so the roots are a complex pair with \(|z|=\sqrt{0.7869}=0.887\lt1\). Stable ✓. At \(K=1.0905\), \(a_2=1\), so the pair sits exactly on \(|z|=1\): the loop is on the edge of oscillation.

A single test value inside the range catches sign slips. The boundary check confirms that the binding condition is the one you think it is.

Common slip: giving \(K\lt49.2\) as the answer. Every condition must hold at once, so the tightest bound wins.

Check (a)

True or false: for this \(P(z)\), the stability limit comes from \(P(-1)\gt0\), so the upper limit is \(K=49.2\).

False. \(P(-1)\gt0\) only gives \(K\lt49.2\). The condition \(|a_2|\lt1\) is tighter and gives \(K\lt1.0905\). The answer is the intersection of all three conditions.

The open loop is changed to \(\dfrac{K(0.4261z+0.3608)}{(z-1)(z-0.5)}\). Only the pole moved. Use condition (1) to find the new upper limit of K (3 s.f.).

Now \(a_2=0.5+0.3608K\lt1\) gives \(K\lt0.5/0.3608=1.386\).

Part (b): Kv and the ramp error

Pick the right error constant

Think first: a unit ramp input goes with which constant, and what is its formula?

A ramp goes with the velocity constant pp. 186–187:

\[K_v=\lim_{z\to1}\frac{(1-z^{-1})\,G(z)}{T},\qquad e_{ss}=\frac{1}{K_v}.\]

The open loop has one pole at \(z=1\) (type 1). A type-1 loop follows a ramp with a constant lag, and \(K_v\) measures that lag.

Cancel the z = 1 pole, then take the limit

Think first: rewrite \(1-z^{-1}\) so that it cancels a factor of the denominator.

\(1-z^{-1}=\dfrac{z-1}{z}\), so

\[(1-z^{-1})G(z)=\frac{K(0.4261z+0.3608)}{z\,(z-0.6065)}.\]

At \(z=1\): \(\dfrac{K(0.7869)}{1\times0.3935}=2.000K\). Divide by \(T=1\):

\[\boxed{K_v=2K}\]

Without the cancellation the limit would be "0/0". The \((z-1)\) factor is the integrator, and removing it leaves a finite number.

Plug in K = 0.5 and check

Think first: is K = 0.5 inside the stable range from (a)? It must be, or \(e_{ss}\) means nothing.

\(0.5\) lies in \(0\lt K\lt1.0905\) ✓. Then \(K_v=1\), so \(\boxed{e_{ss}=1}\) (one unit of lag behind the unit ramp).

Cross-check: this \(G(z)\) is the ZOH sampling of \(K/(s(s+0.5))\). The continuous \(K_v=K/0.5=2K\) is the same. A ZOH does not change \(K_v\).

A steady-state error is only meaningful for a stable loop. Always confirm that the K you were given is in the range first.

Common slip: leaving out \(1/T\). Here \(T=1\), so nothing changes, but with \(T=0.1\) the answer would be off by a factor of 10.

Check (b)

Same loop with \(K=0.25\). What is the steady-state error to a unit ramp?

\(K_v=2(0.25)=0.5\), so \(e_{ss}=1/0.5=2\). K = 0.25 is inside the stable range, so this is valid.

Question 2 10 marks

A continuous system \(G(s)=\dfrac{4}{s^2+2s+4}\) is to be approximated digitally with \(T=0.5\) s.
4(a) Find \(G(z)\) by the bilinear transformation. Check the DC gain and whether \(G(z)\) is stable.
3(b) Find the strictly proper pole-zero matched \(G(z)\), with the DC gain matched.
3(c) Write the difference equation that implements your answer to (a) by direct programming. How many delay elements does it use, and how many would standard programming use?

Part (a): bilinear transformation

Write down the substitution

Think first: what replaces s, and what is the number in front at T = 0.5?

The bilinear (Tustin) rule pp. 7–8 is

\[s=\frac{2}{T}\,\frac{z-1}{z+1}=4\,\frac{z-1}{z+1}\qquad(T=0.5).\]

It comes from approximating an integral by trapezoids. It maps the whole left half s-plane into the unit circle, so a stable \(G(s)\) always gives a stable \(G(z)\).

Substitute and clear the fractions

Think first: \(s^2\) brings a \((z+1)^2\) in the denominator. What should you multiply the top and bottom by?

\(s^2=\dfrac{16(z-1)^2}{(z+1)^2}\). Multiply the numerator and the denominator of \(G\) by \((z+1)^2\):

\[G(z)=\frac{4(z+1)^2}{16(z-1)^2+2\cdot4(z-1)(z+1)+4(z+1)^2}.\]

Expand each term: \(16z^2-32z+16\), \(\ 8z^2-8\), \(\ 4z^2+8z+4\). Sum: \(28z^2-24z+12\).

Multiplying by the highest power of \((z+1)\) turns every term into a polynomial in one move. The numerator 4 must be multiplied too, which is where the \((z+1)^2\) on top comes from.

Common slip: clearing the denominator and leaving the numerator as plain 4. The result then has the wrong DC gain, and the next step catches it.
Simplify

Think first: is there a common factor?

Divide the top and bottom by 4:

\[\boxed{G(z)=\frac{(z+1)^2}{7z^2-6z+3}}\]

A clean form makes the checks and part (c) easier.

Check the DC gain

Think first: DC means \(s=0\). Which z does that correspond to?

\(s=0\) means \(z=1\) under the bilinear rule. \(G(1)=\dfrac{4}{7-6+3}=1\), and \(G(0)=\dfrac44=1\) ✓.

The bilinear map sends \(s=0\) to \(z=1\) exactly, so the DC gain must match. If it doesn't, there is an algebra slip.

Check stability

Think first: you need \(|z|\) of the roots of \(7z^2-6z+3\). Is there a shortcut that avoids solving?

The discriminant is \(36-84\lt0\), so the poles are a complex pair. For a complex pair, \(|z|^2\) equals the product of the roots, \(c/a=3/7\):

\[|z|=\sqrt{3/7}=0.655\lt1\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{stable}.\]

This agrees with what the bilinear map guarantees: \(G(s)\) has poles \(-1\pm j\sqrt3\), which are stable, so \(G(z)\) has to be stable too.

Check (a)

Map \(G(s)=\dfrac{3}{s+3}\) by the bilinear rule with \(T=0.5\). Where is the pole of \(G(z)\)? (A fraction is fine.)

\(s=4\frac{z-1}{z+1}\) gives \(G=\frac{3(z+1)}{4(z-1)+3(z+1)}=\frac{3(z+1)}{7z-1}\). The pole is at \(z=1/7\). DC check: \(G(1)=6/6=1=G(0)\) ✓.

Part (b): pole-zero matching

List the poles and zeros of G(s)

Think first: solve \(s^2+2s+4=0\). How many finite zeros are there?

\(s=-1\pm j\sqrt3\), so \(\zeta\omega_n=1\) and \(\omega_d=\sqrt3=1.732\). There are no finite zeros, so \(n=2\) and \(m=0\).

Pole-zero matching pp. 14–15 maps each pole and zero on its own, so you need them listed first.

Map the poles with z = e^{sT}

Think first: a conjugate pair maps to a quadratic. What are its two coefficients?

\(z=e^{(-1\pm j\sqrt3)(0.5)}=e^{-0.5}e^{\pm j0.866}\): radius \(r=e^{-0.5}=0.6065\), angle \(\theta=0.866\) rad. The pair gives \(z^2-2r\cos\theta\,z+r^2\) (Example 5.2 pp. 16–17):

\[z^2-2e^{-0.5}\cos(0.866)\,z+e^{-1}=z^2-0.7859z+0.3679.\]

\((z-re^{j\theta})(z-re^{-j\theta})=z^2-2r\cos\theta\,z+r^2\). Using it avoids complex arithmetic.

Common slip: leaving the calculator in degrees. \(\cos(0.866^\circ)\approx1\) gives \(0.6065\times2=1.213\), which is wrong. The angle is \(\omega_dT\) in radians.
Place the zeros at z = −1

Think first: \(G(s)\) has \(n-m=2\) zeros at infinity. How many go to \(-1\) in the strictly proper version?

Zeros at \(s=\infty\) (the highest frequency) map to \(z=-1\) (the highest discrete frequency). The proper version would add \(n-m=2\). The strictly proper version adds \(n-m-1=\mathbf1\):

\[G(z)=\alpha\,\frac{z+1}{z^2-0.7859z+0.3679}.\]

Strictly proper means numerator degree < denominator degree. This leaves a one-sample delay, so the computer has one full period to compute \(u(k)\) before it is needed.

Match the DC gain to find α

Think first: what must \(G(z)\) equal at \(z=1\)?

\(G(s=0)=1\), so set \(G(z=1)=1\):

\[\alpha\cdot\frac{2}{1-0.7859+0.3679}=\alpha\cdot\frac{2}{0.5820}=1\ \Rightarrow\ \alpha=0.2910.\] \[\boxed{G(z)=\frac{0.2910\,(z+1)}{z^2-0.7859z+0.3679}}\]

Mapping the poles and zeros fixes the shape but not the gain. One extra condition is needed, and the usual choice is DC.

Check (b)

True or false: the bilinear and the pole-zero matched \(G(z)\) have the same poles, because both come from the same \(G(s)\).

False. Pole-zero matching uses the exact map \(e^{sT}\), giving \(|z|=e^{-0.5}=0.6065\). Bilinear is an approximation and gives \(|z|=\sqrt{3/7}=0.655\). Both are stable and both have DC gain 1, but the poles differ.

Pole-zero match \(G(s)=\dfrac{2}{s+2}\) at \(T=1\), strictly proper, with DC gain matched. \(G(z)=\dfrac{\alpha}{z-e^{-2}}\). Find \(\alpha\) (4 d.p.).

\(n-m-1=0\), so no zero is added. \(G(s=0)=1\), so \(G(1)=\alpha/(1-e^{-2})=\alpha/0.8647=1\Rightarrow\alpha=0.8647\).

Part (c): direct programming

Rewrite G in powers of z⁻¹ with a leading 1

Think first: divide by what, so that the denominator starts with exactly 1?

Divide the top and bottom of \(\dfrac{(z+1)^2}{7z^2-6z+3}\) by \(7z^2\):

\[G=\frac{\tfrac17\,(1+2z^{-1}+z^{-2})}{1-\tfrac67z^{-1}+\tfrac37z^{-2}}.\]

\(z^{-1}\) means "one sample ago", and that is what a computer can store. A leading 1 in the denominator lets you solve for \(y(k)\) directly.

Cross-multiply and read off the recursion

Think first: move every \(y\) term except \(y(k)\) to the right. Watch the signs.

\(Y(1-\tfrac67z^{-1}+\tfrac37z^{-2})=\tfrac17X(1+2z^{-1}+z^{-2})\). Turn each \(z^{-i}\) into a delay of \(i\) samples, then solve for \(y(k)\) pp. 104–107:

\[\boxed{y(k)=\tfrac67\,y(k-1)-\tfrac37\,y(k-2)+\tfrac17\big[x(k)+2x(k-1)+x(k-2)\big]}\]

Direct programming is just this recursion, \(y(k)=-a_1y(k-1)-a_2y(k-2)+b_0x(k)+\dots\). Here \(a_1=-\tfrac67\), so \(-a_1=+\tfrac67\).

Common slip: copying \(-\tfrac67\) onto the right-hand side. Moving a term across the equals sign flips its sign.
Count the delays, then check the recursion

Think first: list every past value the program has to remember.

Direct programming stores \(x(k-1),x(k-2),y(k-1),y(k-2)\): \(\mathbf{n+m=4}\) delays. Standard programming pp. 108–110 runs both sides through one internal signal and needs only \(\mathbf{n=2}\).

DC check: with \(x\equiv1\) and \(y\equiv y_\infty\): \(y_\infty(1-\tfrac67+\tfrac37)=\tfrac47\), so \(y_\infty\cdot\tfrac47=\tfrac47\) and \(y_\infty=1\) ✓, matching \(G(1)=1\).

Putting a constant through the recursion is a 10-second test that catches a sign error in the coefficients.

Check (c)

\(C(z)=\dfrac{2-1.2z^{-1}}{1-0.5z^{-1}}\). In the direct-programming equation, what is the coefficient of \(u(k-1)\) in \(u(k)=\ ?\,u(k-1)+\dots\)

\(a_1=-0.5\), so the coefficient is \(-a_1=+0.5\): \(u(k)=0.5u(k-1)+2e(k)-1.2e(k-1)\).

For the same \(C(z)\), how many delays does direct programming use?

\(n+m=1+1=2\) (\(e(k-1)\) and \(u(k-1)\)). Standard programming would need \(n=1\).

Question 3 10 marks

Unity feedback with a digital controller \(C(z)\) in front of \(G_{ZAS}(z)\). The desired closed loop is \(G_{cl}(z)=z^{-1}\).
5(a) \(G_{ZAS}(z)=\dfrac{0.2(z+0.6)}{(z-0.8)(z-0.4)}\). Can \(G_{cl}=z^{-1}\) be used, and will the output track a step? If so, find \(C(z)\).
5(b) Repeat for \(G_{ZAS}(z)=\dfrac{0.1(z+1.5)}{(z-1)(z-0.5)}\). If \(z^{-1}\) is unacceptable, explain why, choose a valid \(G_{cl}\), and find \(C(z)\) and the output \(y(k)\), \(k=0..3\).

Part (a): a plant where z⁻¹ works

Get the controller formula

Think first: start from \(G_{cl}=\dfrac{CG}{1+CG}\) and solve for C.

Cross-multiply: \(G_{cl}(1+CG)=CG\), so \(G_{cl}=CG(1-G_{cl})\), which gives pp. 49–50

\[C(z)=\frac{1}{G_{ZAS}(z)}\,\frac{G_{cl}(z)}{1-G_{cl}(z)}\qquad(5.11)\]

Direct design works backwards: pick the closed loop you want, then solve for the controller. The formula always produces a C, so the real question is whether that C is acceptable. The four conditions answer that.

Condition (i): causality (relative degree)

Think first: relative degree = denominator degree − numerator degree. Compare the plant with \(z^{-1}=1/z\).

The four conditions are on pp. 51–56. The plant has degree 2 − 1 = 1. \(G_{cl}=1/z\) has degree 1 − 0 = 1. Since \(1\ge1\), condition (i) holds ✓.

The plant needs one sample before any input shows up at its output. A closed loop cannot respond faster than its plant, so \(G_{cl}\) needs at least that much delay. Otherwise C would have to see the future.

Conditions (ii) and (iii): unstable zeros and poles

Think first: are any plant zeros or poles on or outside the unit circle?

Zero at \(-0.6\): \(|{-0.6}|\lt1\), so (ii) asks nothing ✓. Poles at \(0.8\) and \(0.4\), both inside, so (iii) asks nothing ✓.

C contains \(1/G_{ZAS}\), so it cancels every plant zero and pole. Cancelling something stable is harmless. Cancelling something unstable hides a growing mode inside the loop. Part (b) shows this happening.

Condition (iv): does the output track a step?

Think first: evaluate \(G_{cl}\) at \(z=1\). What does \(Y=z^{-1}R\) mean in time?

\(G_{cl}(1)=1\) ✓, so the steady-state step error is zero. In fact \(y(k)=r(k-1)\): the output is the input delayed by one sample, so for a step \(y=0,1,1,1,\dots\). This is deadbeat pp. 64–65.

Answer: yes, \(z^{-1}\) can be used and the output tracks the step.

\(z=1\) is DC, so \(G_{cl}(1)\) is the closed-loop DC gain. A gain of 1 means \(y(\infty)=r(\infty)\).

Compute C(z)

Think first: simplify \(\dfrac{z^{-1}}{1-z^{-1}}\) before multiplying by \(1/G_{ZAS}\).

\(\dfrac{z^{-1}}{1-z^{-1}}=\dfrac{1}{z-1}\). Then

\[C=\frac{(z-0.8)(z-0.4)}{0.2(z+0.6)}\cdot\frac{1}{z-1}=\boxed{\frac{5(z-0.8)(z-0.4)}{(z-1)(z+0.6)}}\]

The plant is type 0 (no pole at \(z=1\)), so C supplies the integrator \(1/(z-1)\) that removes the step error. C's other pole, \(-0.6\), is the cancelled plant zero. Being on the negative real axis, it makes \(u(k)\) alternate, so expect some ripple between samples. Ripple-free deadbeat removes that.

Check (a)

A plant is \(G_{ZAS}=\dfrac{0.3}{z(z-0.5)}\), with everything inside the circle. What is the smallest m for which \(G_{cl}=z^{-m}\) is causal?

Relative degree = 2 − 0 = 2, so \(m\ge2\). With \(z^{-1}\), (5.11) gives \(C=\frac{z(z-0.5)}{0.3(z-1)}\): numerator degree 2 above denominator degree 1, so C would need a future error value.

True or false: in part (a) the pole of C at \(z=1\) is needed because the plant is type 0.

True. For a constant output with zero error, something in the loop must keep producing a constant from a zero input, and that is an integrator. The plant has none, so C supplies it. Formula (5.11) adds it automatically through \(1/(1-G_{cl})\).

Part (b): a plant where z⁻¹ fails

Run the four conditions on the new plant

Think first: where is the zero this time? Where are the poles?

(i) Relative degree 2 − 1 = 1, and \(z^{-1}\) has 1 ✓.
(ii) Zero at \(-1.5\): \(|{-1.5}|\gt1\), outside the circle. So \(G_{cl}\) must contain \((z+1.5)\), and \(z^{-1}\) does not ✗.
(iii) Pole at \(1\), on the circle: \(1-G_{cl}\) must vanish at \(z=1\). Pole \(0.5\) is fine.
(iv) \(G_{cl}(1)=1\) ✓.

So \(z^{-1}\) fails condition (ii).

Checking all four conditions in order is what earns the marks, and it shows at once which one breaks.

Show what goes wrong if you use z⁻¹ anyway

Think first: compute C from (5.11) and look at its poles.

\[C_{bad}=\frac{(z-1)(z-0.5)}{0.1(z+1.5)}\cdot\frac{1}{z-1}=\frac{10(z-0.5)}{z+1.5}.\]

C has a pole at \(-1.5\), outside the circle. On paper \(Y=z^{-1}R\) looks perfect, but the control signal is \(U=Y/G_{ZAS}\), and that contains \(\dfrac{1}{z+1.5}\). So \(u(k)\) grows like \((-1.5)^k\) and saturates the actuator. The loop is input-output stable but not asymptotically stable p. 52.

This is why "\(G_{cl}(1)=1\), so it tracks" is not enough. The examiner wants you to name the hidden unstable mode.

Choose a valid Gcl: keep the bad zero

Think first: what is the simplest \(G_{cl}\) that contains \((1+1.5z^{-1})\) and still has relative degree ≥ 1?

\[G_{cl}=K\,z^{-1}(1+1.5z^{-1})=K\,\frac{z+1.5}{z^2}.\]

Relative degree 2 − 1 = 1 ✓. The bad zero now sits in \(G_{cl}\), so C no longer has to cancel it.

You cannot remove a zero outside the circle. The only option is to accept it in the closed loop. The price is one extra sample of settling.

Fix K from condition (iv)

Think first: \(G_{cl}(1)=1\). Is it \(1.5K\) or something else?

\[G_{cl}(1)=K(1+1.5)=2.5K=1\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{K=0.4}.\]
Common slip: \(1.5K=1\). You have to evaluate the whole factor \((1+1.5z^{-1})\) at \(z=1\), not only the zero.

K is the only free number. Condition (iv) sets it so that the DC gain is 1.

Check condition (iii) for the pole at z = 1

Think first: does \(1-G_{cl}\) vanish at \(z=1\)? Factor it.

\[1-G_{cl}=\frac{z^2-0.4z-0.6}{z^2}=\frac{(z-1)(z+0.6)}{z^2}.\]

It contains \((z-1)\) ✓. That is automatic: \(G_{cl}(1)=1\) forces \(1-G_{cl}\) to vanish at \(z=1\).

If (iii) held trivially, nothing extra is needed for the marginal plant pole. Factoring \(1-G_{cl}\) now also prepares the next step.

Compute C(z)

Think first: substitute into (5.11) and cancel. Which factors cancel?

\[C=\frac{G_{cl}}{G_{ZAS}(1-G_{cl})}=\frac{0.4(z+1.5)}{z^2}\cdot\frac{(z-1)(z-0.5)}{0.1(z+1.5)}\cdot\frac{z^2}{(z-1)(z+0.6)}=\boxed{\frac{4(z-0.5)}{z+0.6}}\]

\((z+1.5)\) cancels between \(G_{cl}\) and \(1/G_{ZAS}\), so it never becomes a pole of C. \((z-1)\) and \(z^2\) cancel too. C's only pole is \(-0.6\), which is stable ✓.

Compare with \(C_{bad}\): its pole at \(-1.5\) is gone. That was the whole purpose of putting the zero into \(G_{cl}\). C needs no integrator because the plant already has one at \(z=1\).

Find y(k) for k = 0..3 (and confirm u is bounded)

Think first: \(Y=G_{cl}R\) with \(G_{cl}=0.4z^{-1}+0.6z^{-2}\). Write \(y(k)\) in terms of \(r\).

\(y(k)=0.4\,r(k-1)+0.6\,r(k-2)\). For a unit step:

\[y(0)=0,\quad y(1)=0.4,\quad y(2)=0.4+0.6=1,\quad y(3)=1.\]

Control signal: \(U=\dfrac{G_{cl}}{G_{ZAS}}R=\dfrac{4(z-0.5)}{z}=4-2z^{-1}\), so \(u=4,-2,0,0,\dots\) Bounded ✓. It settles at 0, as it should for a type-1 plant holding a constant output.

Reading the coefficients of \(G_{cl}\) as a moving sum of past inputs gives \(y(k)\) without long division. Checking \(u\) proves the hidden instability is gone.

Check (b)

Another plant has a zero at \(z=-2\) (and is otherwise like (b)). You choose \(G_{cl}=Kz^{-1}(1+2z^{-1})\). Find K.

\(G_{cl}(1)=K(1+2)=3K=1\Rightarrow K=1/3\).

Back to (b) as given. What is \(y(1)\)?

\(y(1)=0.4\,r(0)=0.4\). The output reaches 1 only at \(k=2\), one sample later than plain deadbeat.

True or false: in (b), \(G_{cl}=z^{-1}\) is acceptable because \(G_{cl}(1)=1\) gives zero steady-state error.

False. The output would track, but C would cancel the zero at \(-1.5\) with an unstable pole, and \(u(k)\) would grow like \((-1.5)^k\). Condition (ii) is violated.

Method to remember

  • Jury K-range: \(1+G=0\) → clear fractions → \(P(z)\). For \(n=2\): \(|a_2|\lt a_0\), \(P(1)\gt0\), \(P(-1)\gt0\). Intersect the ranges, then test one K inside.
  • \(K_v\): write \(1-z^{-1}=(z-1)/z\), cancel the integrator, set \(z=1\), divide by \(T\). Confirm K is stable before quoting \(e_{ss}\).
  • Bilinear: \(s=\frac2T\frac{z-1}{z+1}\). Multiply top and bottom by \((z+1)^n\). Check \(G(1)=G(0)\).
  • Pole-zero matching: map poles by \(e^{sT}\) (radians!), add \(n-m-1\) zeros at \(-1\) for strictly proper, and fix \(\alpha\) by DC gain.
  • Direct programming: divide so the denominator starts with 1, then flip the signs of \(a_i\). Delays: direct \(n+m\), standard \(n\).
  • Direct design: \(C=\frac1{G}\frac{G_{cl}}{1-G_{cl}}\). Check (i) relative degree, (ii) bad zeros kept in \(G_{cl}\), (iii) bad poles in \(1-G_{cl}\), (iv) \(G_{cl}(1)=1\). A zero outside the circle → \(G_{cl}=Kz^{-1}(1-\beta z^{-1})\) with \(K\) from \(G_{cl}(1)=1\).
