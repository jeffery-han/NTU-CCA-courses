EE6203 Quiz 1 — prediction papers

Four complete papers built to the recalled Quiz 1 shape: one question, two parts, 30 minutes, closed book. Part (a) is always a direct \(z\)-transform of a piecewise sampled input from the definition only; part (b) is always the open-loop sampler / ZOH / plant output plus a final-value ruling.

Equations are not rendering. Keep this file in the quizzes folder next to its vendor/mathjax/ renderer, and reload.

How to use this

  • Sit one paper at a time, closed book, on paper. Start the timer, write full working, and do not open a model answer until the clock stops.
  • The formula sheet below is the only thing you may look at during a paper — it matches what the quiz supplies (necessary transform pairs; calculator allowed).
  • Part (a) has a hard restriction: no transform table, no shift theorem. You must build the answer out of the defining sum and sum the geometric series yourself.
  • Mark yourself against the scheme afterwards by ticking each earned checkpoint. Method marks only count if you actually wrote the reason.
  • Target: 16/20 or better with no conceptual error. Below that, the diagnosis at the bottom tells you what to reread.

Paper 1 — two-level input, first-order plant

20 marks30 minclosest to the recall
30:00

Given. Sampling period \(T=0.5\ \mathrm{s}\); plant \(H(s)=\dfrac{2}{s+2}\); the input is the piecewise signal

\[ x(t)=\begin{cases} 0, & t<0,\\ 2, & 0\le t<2T,\\ 1, & t\ge 2T. \end{cases} \]
Part (a) — 8 marks

Obtain \(X(z)\) in closed rational form and state its region of convergence. Work only from the defining sum: you may not quote a transform-table pair and you may not invoke the shift (delay) property.

Step 1 — turn the piecewise rule into samples

Everything hinges on which branch each sampling instant falls in. \(2T=1.0\ \mathrm{s}\), so:

\(k\)0123\(\ge 2\)
\(kT\)00.51.01.5\(\ge 1.0\)
\(x(kT)\)22111

The interval \(0\le t<2T\) is closed on the left and open on the right, so \(t=1.0\) belongs to the second branch: \(x[2]=1\), not 2.

Step 2 — substitute into the definition

\[ X(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x(kT)z^{-k}=2+2z^{-1}+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}z^{-k}. \]

Step 3 — sum the tail from first principles

Relabel the summation index with \(m=k-2\). This is renaming a dummy index inside the sum, not the shift theorem:

\[ \sum_{k=2}^{\infty}z^{-k}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{-(m+2)}=z^{-2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(z^{-1}\right)^{m}. \]

Build the geometric sum rather than quoting it. With \(S_N=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}r^m\), the difference \(S_N-rS_N=1-r^N\) gives \(S_N=\dfrac{1-r^N}{1-r}\), and \(r^N\to0\) iff \(|r|<1\), so \(S_\infty=\dfrac{1}{1-r}\). Here \(r=z^{-1}\), which needs \(|z^{-1}|<1\), i.e. \(|z|>1\).

Step 4 — assemble

\[ X(z)=2+2z^{-1}+\frac{z^{-2}}{1-z^{-1}} =\frac{(2+2z^{-1})(1-z^{-1})+z^{-2}}{1-z^{-1}} =\frac{2-z^{-2}}{1-z^{-1}}=\frac{2z^{2}-1}{z(z-1)},\quad |z|>1. \]
Check by re-expanding: \((2-z^{-2})(1+z^{-1}+z^{-2}+\cdots)=2+2z^{-1}+z^{-2}+z^{-3}+\cdots\) — the samples come back out. Always spend 20 seconds on this.

  • Defining sum written out and split into the finite head plus the infinite tail 2
  • Index relabelled and the geometric series derived, not quoted 2
  • Correct single rational closed form 1
  • ROC \(|z|>1\) stated 1
Trap: writing \(x[2]=2\) because "2 appears up to \(2T\)". One misread boundary costs the whole of part (a) and then poisons part (b).
Part (b) — 12 marks

Obtain \(Y(z)\) for the sampled output. Then decide whether the final-value theorem may be used to find \(\lim_{k\to\infty}y(kT)\), justifying the decision; if it may, evaluate it.

Step 1 — say why \(Y(z)=G(z)X(z)\) is legal

The sampler is upstream of the hold, so the driving signal of the continuous part is already starred. Hence \([G X^{*}]^{*}=G^{*}X^{*}\), the hold-plus-plant has a genuine pulse transfer function, and \(Y(z)=G(z)X(z)\). State this in one line; it is a method mark.

Step 2 — reduce ZOH + plant

\[ \frac{H(s)}{s}=\frac{2}{s(s+2)}=\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{s+2}\ \Longrightarrow\ 1-e^{-2t}. \]

With \(a=e^{-2T}=e^{-1}=0.3679\),

\[ G(z)=(1-z^{-1})\left[\frac{1}{1-z^{-1}}-\frac{1}{1-az^{-1}}\right] =\frac{(1-a)z^{-1}}{1-az^{-1}}=\frac{0.6321\,z^{-1}}{1-0.3679\,z^{-1}}. \]

Step 3 — output

\[ Y(z)=G(z)X(z)=\frac{0.6321\,z^{-1}\left(2-z^{-2}\right)}{\left(1-0.3679\,z^{-1}\right)\left(1-z^{-1}\right)}. \]

Step 4 — rule on the final-value theorem

\[ (1-z^{-1})Y(z)=\frac{0.6321\,z^{-1}\left(2-z^{-2}\right)}{1-0.3679\,z^{-1}}. \]

Its poles are \(z=0.3679\) and \(z=0\), both strictly inside the unit circle. The theorem therefore applies, and

\[ \lim_{k\to\infty}y(kT)=\lim_{z\to1}(1-z^{-1})Y(z)=\frac{0.6321(2-1)}{1-0.3679}=\frac{0.6321}{0.6321}=1. \]
Independent check: the input settles at 1 and the plant's DC gain is \(H(0)=2/2=1\), so the output must settle at \(1\times1=1\). Two routes, same number.

  • Used \(G(z)=(1-z^{-1})\mathcal{Z}\{H(s)/s\}\), not \(s\mapsto z\) 2
  • Partial fraction of \(H(s)/s\) and correct inverse Laplace 2
  • \(G(z)=(1-a)z^{-1}/(1-az^{-1})\) with \(a=e^{-1}\) evaluated 2
  • \(Y(z)\) assembled correctly 1
  • Formed \((1-z^{-1})Y(z)\) and tested its poles before concluding 2
  • \(y(\infty)=1\) 1
Trap: substituting \(z=1\) first and calling the number "the final value". The number only means something after the pole test passes.

Paper 2 — plant with an integrator

Given. \(T=1\ \mathrm{s}\); plant \(H(s)=\dfrac{1}{s(s+1)}\); input

\[ x(t)=\begin{cases} 0, & t<0,\\ 3, & 0\le t<T,\\ 1, & t\ge T. \end{cases} \]
Part (a) — 8 marks

From the definition alone (no table, no shift property), find \(X(z)\) as a single rational function of \(z^{-1}\), and give the region of convergence.

Samples: \(x[0]=3\) (the instant \(t=0\) is in the first branch), and \(x[k]=1\) for all \(k\ge1\) since \(kT\ge T\).

\[ X(z)=3+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z^{-k}=3+z^{-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(z^{-1}\right)^{m} =3+\frac{z^{-1}}{1-z^{-1}},\qquad |z|>1, \]

with the geometric sum obtained as in Paper 1 from \(S_N-rS_N=1-r^N\). Combining,

\[ X(z)=\frac{3(1-z^{-1})+z^{-1}}{1-z^{-1}}=\frac{3-2z^{-1}}{1-z^{-1}}=\frac{3z-2}{z-1},\qquad |z|>1. \]
Check: \((3-2z^{-1})(1+z^{-1}+z^{-2}+\cdots)=3+z^{-1}+z^{-2}+\cdots\). Correct: one sample of height 3, then all ones.

  • Geometric series derived, \(z^{-1}\) factored out of the tail correctly 2

Part (b) — 12 marks

Find \(G(z)\) for the hold-plus-plant and hence \(Y(z)\). State, with justification, whether the final-value theorem may be used on \(y(kT)\), and if it may, evaluate the limit.

Step 1 — reduce the hold and plant

\[ \frac{H(s)}{s}=\frac{1}{s^{2}(s+1)}=-\frac{1}{s}+\frac{1}{s^{2}}+\frac{1}{s+1}\ \Longrightarrow\ -1+t+e^{-t}. \]

With \(T=1\) and \(b=e^{-1}=0.3679\),

\[ G(z)=(1-z^{-1})\left[\frac{-1}{1-z^{-1}}+\frac{z^{-1}}{(1-z^{-1})^{2}}+\frac{1}{1-bz^{-1}}\right] =\frac{b\,z^{-1}+(1-2b)z^{-2}}{(1-z^{-1})(1-b z^{-1})} \] \[ =\frac{0.3679\,z^{-1}+0.2642\,z^{-2}}{(1-z^{-1})(1-0.3679\,z^{-1})}. \]
Check the first samples: the ZOH step response of \(H\) is \(t-1+e^{-t}\); at \(t=T\) that is \(0.3679\), which is exactly the coefficient the series expansion of \(G(z)/(1-z^{-1})\) gives at \(k=1\). At \(t=2\) it gives \(1.1353\), matching too.

Step 2 — output

\[ Y(z)=\frac{\left(0.3679\,z^{-1}+0.2642\,z^{-2}\right)\left(3-2z^{-1}\right)}{(1-z^{-1})^{2}\left(1-0.3679\,z^{-1}\right)}. \]

Step 3 — the ruling

\[ (1-z^{-1})Y(z)=\frac{\left(0.3679\,z^{-1}+0.2642\,z^{-2}\right)\left(3-2z^{-1}\right)}{(1-z^{-1})\left(1-0.3679\,z^{-1}\right)}. \]

One factor \((1-z^{-1})\) survives, so \((1-z^{-1})Y(z)\) still has a pole at \(z=1\), which is on the unit circle, not strictly inside. The final-value theorem may not be used, and \(y(kT)\) has no finite limit.

Physically: the plant contains an integrator, and after the first sample the held input is a constant 1, so the output ramps away without bound.

Trap: "there is a \((1-z^{-1})\) in front, so the pole at \(z=1\) is cancelled." Count them. \(Y(z)\) has a double pole at \(z=1\) — one from the input's constant tail, one from the plant's integrator — and the theorem cancels only one.

  • Used the ramp pair \(Tz^{-1}/(1-z^{-1})^{2}\) with \(T\) in the numerator 2
  • \(G(z)\) simplified, integrator pole at \(z=1\) still visible 2
  • \(Y(z)=G(z)X(z)\) formed, with the double pole at \(z=1\) exposed 2
  • Ruled the theorem invalid because a pole remains at \(z=1\) after multiplying by \((1-z^{-1})\) 3
  • Physical reason given: integrator driven by a non-zero steady input 1

Paper 3 — decaying input, unstable plant

20 marks30 minfinal value fails
30:00

Given. \(T=1\ \mathrm{s}\); plant \(H(s)=\dfrac{1}{s-1}\); input

\[ x(t)=\begin{cases} 0, & t<0,\\ 1, & 0\le t<2T,\\ e^{-(t-2T)}, & t\ge 2T. \end{cases} \]
Part (a) — 8 marks

Working only from \(X(z)=\sum_{k\ge0}x(kT)z^{-k}\) — no table, no shift property — obtain \(X(z)\) in closed form and state the ROC.

Samples: \(x[0]=1\), \(x[1]=1\), and for \(k\ge2\), \(x[k]=e^{-(kT-2T)}=e^{-(k-2)}\). Note \(x[2]=e^{0}=1\): the signal is continuous at \(t=2T\).

\[ X(z)=1+z^{-1}+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}e^{-(k-2)}z^{-k}. \]

Relabel \(m=k-2\) inside the sum:

\[ \sum_{k=2}^{\infty}e^{-(k-2)}z^{-k}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}e^{-m}z^{-(m+2)} =z^{-2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(e^{-1}z^{-1}\right)^{m}=\frac{z^{-2}}{1-e^{-1}z^{-1}}, \]

valid when \(|e^{-1}z^{-1}|<1\), i.e. \(|z|>e^{-1}=0.3679\). This is the ROC of the whole transform, since the first two terms converge everywhere except \(z=0\). Hence

\[ X(z)=1+z^{-1}+\frac{z^{-2}}{1-e^{-1}z^{-1}} =\frac{1+(1-e^{-1})z^{-1}+(1-e^{-1})z^{-2}}{1-e^{-1}z^{-1}} =\frac{1+0.6321z^{-1}+0.6321z^{-2}}{1-0.3679z^{-1}}. \]
Check: expanding gives \(1,\ 1,\ 1,\ 0.3679,\ 0.1353,\dots\) — two ones, then \(e^{0},e^{-1},e^{-2}\). Exactly the sampled signal.

  • Tail relabelled and written as a geometric series in \(e^{-1}z^{-1}\) 2
  • Series summed from first principles with the convergence condition 2
  • Closed rational form 1
  • ROC \(|z|>e^{-1}\) 1
Trap: writing the tail as \(e^{-kT}\) instead of \(e^{-(k-2)T}\). The exponential restarts at the junction, so the exponent is measured from \(2T\).
Part (b) — 12 marks

Find \(Y(z)\). The input decays to zero; does it follow that \(y(kT)\to 0\)? Decide whether the final-value theorem may be applied and justify the answer.

Step 1 — hold plus plant

\[ \frac{H(s)}{s}=\frac{1}{s(s-1)}=-\frac{1}{s}+\frac{1}{s-1}\ \Longrightarrow\ -1+e^{t}. \]

With \(c=e^{T}=e=2.7183\),

\[ G(z)=(1-z^{-1})\left[\frac{-1}{1-z^{-1}}+\frac{1}{1-cz^{-1}}\right] =\frac{(c-1)z^{-1}}{1-cz^{-1}}=\frac{1.7183\,z^{-1}}{1-2.7183\,z^{-1}}. \]

Same algebraic shape as the stable case, but the pole is now at \(z=e^{+T}\), outside the unit circle.

Step 2 — output

\[ Y(z)=\frac{1.7183\,z^{-1}\left(1+0.6321z^{-1}+0.6321z^{-2}\right)}{\left(1-2.7183\,z^{-1}\right)\left(1-0.3679\,z^{-1}\right)}. \]

Step 3 — the ruling

\((1-z^{-1})Y(z)\) has poles at \(z=2.7183\) and \(z=0.3679\). The first lies outside the unit circle, so the final-value theorem may not be used. A decaying input does not rescue an unstable plant: \(y(kT)\) diverges.

Trap, and the reason this paper exists: here \(Y(z)\) has no pole at \(z=1\), so \(\lim_{z\to1}(1-z^{-1})Y(z)=0\) computes cleanly and would "prove" \(y(\infty)=0\). It is wrong. The theorem's precondition is about all poles of \((1-z^{-1})Y(z)\), not about whether the limit happens to exist.
Independent check by recurrence: \(G(z)\) gives \(y[k]=c\,y[k-1]+(c-1)x[k-1]\) with \(y[0]=0\). Then \(y[1]=1.72\), \(y[2]=6.39\), \(y[3]=19.1\) — growing by roughly a factor \(e\) per step.

  • Recognised the pair maps to a pole at \(z=e^{+aT}\), not \(e^{-aT}\) 2
  • \(G(z)=(c-1)z^{-1}/(1-cz^{-1})\) with \(c=e\) 2
  • \(Y(z)=G(z)X(z)\) assembled 2
  • Ruled the theorem invalid on the pole at \(z=e>1\), not on the value of the limit 3

Paper 4 — finite-duration input, second-order plant

20 marks30 minfinal value valid, limit 0
30:00

Given. \(T=1\ \mathrm{s}\); plant \(H(s)=\dfrac{2}{(s+1)(s+2)}\); input

\[ x(t)=\begin{cases} 0, & t<0,\\ 4, & 0\le t<T,\\ 2, & T\le t<3T,\\ 0, & t\ge 3T. \end{cases} \]
Part (a) — 8 marks

From the definition only, find \(X(z)\). State where the series converges and explain why no convergence condition on \(|z|\) beyond \(z\neq0\) is needed here.

Sample instants \(t=0,1,2,3,\dots\) against the branches: \(t=0\to4\); \(t=T\) belongs to the third branch (closed on the left) \(\to2\); \(t=2T\to2\); \(t=3T\to0\), and zero thereafter.

\[ X(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x(kT)z^{-k}=4+2z^{-1}+2z^{-2}. \]

The sum terminates after three terms, so it is a polynomial in \(z^{-1}\): there is nothing to converge. It is finite for every \(z\neq0\), and no geometric-series condition arises. That is the whole point of the second sentence in the question.

  • All four branch boundaries read correctly (\(x[1]=2\), \(x[3]=0\)) 3
  • Defining sum written and evaluated term by term 2
  • Correct polynomial \(4+2z^{-1}+2z^{-2}\) 1
  • Explained that a finite sequence gives a polynomial, convergent for all \(z\neq0\) 2
Part (b) — 12 marks

Obtain \(G(z)\) and \(Y(z)\). Determine whether the final-value theorem may be used for \(y(kT)\); if it may, evaluate the limit and verify it by a physical argument.

Step 1 — hold plus plant

\[ \frac{H(s)}{s}=\frac{2}{s(s+1)(s+2)}=\frac{1}{s}-\frac{2}{s+1}+\frac{1}{s+2}\ \Longrightarrow\ 1-2e^{-t}+e^{-2t}. \]

With \(p=e^{-T}=0.3679\) and \(q=e^{-2T}=0.1353\),

\[ G(z)=(1-z^{-1})\left[\frac{1}{1-z^{-1}}-\frac{2}{1-pz^{-1}}+\frac{1}{1-qz^{-1}}\right] =\frac{(1-2p+q)z^{-1}+(p-2q+pq)z^{-2}}{1-(p+q)z^{-1}+pq\,z^{-2}} \] \[ =\frac{0.3996\,z^{-1}+0.1470\,z^{-2}}{1-0.5032\,z^{-1}+0.0498\,z^{-2}}. \]
Two checks worth doing: (i) \(G(1)=\dfrac{0.3996+0.1470}{1-0.5032+0.0498}=1.000=H(0)=2/2\), the DC gains agree; (ii) the coefficient \(0.3996\) is the ZOH step response \(1-2e^{-t}+e^{-2t}\) evaluated at \(t=T\).

Step 2 — output

\[ Y(z)=\frac{\left(0.3996\,z^{-1}+0.1470\,z^{-2}\right)\left(4+2z^{-1}+2z^{-2}\right)}{1-0.5032\,z^{-1}+0.0498\,z^{-2}}. \]

Step 3 — the ruling

\((1-z^{-1})Y(z)\) has poles only at \(z=p=0.3679\) and \(z=q=0.1353\) (the input contributes none, being a polynomial), both strictly inside the unit circle. The theorem applies:

\[ \lim_{k\to\infty}y(kT)=\lim_{z\to1}(1-z^{-1})Y(z)=0\cdot G(1)X(1)=0. \]
Physical check: the input is switched off after \(3T\) and the plant is stable with poles at \(s=-1,-2\), so the output must decay to zero. The theorem agrees.
Trap: claiming the theorem is inapplicable "because the answer is zero" or "because the input stops". Zero is a perfectly good final value; validity is decided by the poles, and nothing else.

Twist bank — 60 seconds each, out loud

If a paper felt comfortable, these are where the same question gets marked down. Answer before opening.

1. The ZOH is deleted, so the sampler feeds \(H(s)\) directly. What changes in part (b)?

The hold conversion disappears: \(G(z)=\mathcal{Z}\{H(s)\}\), not \((1-z^{-1})\mathcal{Z}\{H(s)/s\}\). For Paper 1 that gives \(G(z)=\dfrac{2}{1-e^{-1}z^{-1}}\). Note there is no longer a guaranteed \(z^{-1}\) factor, so the output can respond in the same sample. The plant is now driven by impulses rather than by a held staircase, so the numerical output differs — do not carry the old answer across.

2. The input sampler is removed; \(x(t)\) enters \(H(s)\) continuously and only the output is sampled. Can you still write \(Y(z)=G(z)X(z)\)?

No. The sampled output is \(\left[H(s)X(s)\right]^{*}\), which in general does not factor into \(H^{*}(s)X^{*}(s)\). There is no input-independent pulse transfer function \(Y(z)/X(z)\) for that topology. You can still compute \(Y(z)=\mathcal{Z}\{H(s)X(s)\}\) for the specific given \(x\). Output sampling never repairs a missing input sampler.

3. In Paper 1, \(T\) changes from 0.5 s to 1 s. Does \(y(\infty)\) change?

No. \(a=e^{-2T}\) changes to \(e^{-2}=0.1353\), so \(G(z)\) and the transient change, but \(G(1)=\dfrac{1-a}{1-a}=1\) for every \(T>0\). The final value stays 1, fixed by the plant DC gain and the input's settling level. Sampling faster or slower does not change where a stable loop ends up.

4. In Paper 2, the input is instead a single pulse: \(x(t)=3\) on \(0\le t<T\) and 0 afterwards. Is the final-value theorem valid now?

Yes, and this is the instructive case. \(X(z)=3\), a polynomial with no pole at \(z=1\), so \((1-z^{-1})Y(z)=\dfrac{3\left(bz^{-1}+(1-2b)z^{-2}\right)}{1-bz^{-1}}\) — the plant's integrator pole is exactly cancelled by the theorem's own factor, leaving only \(z=b=0.3679\) inside the circle. The limit is \(\dfrac{3(1-b)}{1-b}=3\). Physically: the hold delivers a rectangle of area \(3T=3\) into an integrator whose downstream DC gain is 1, so the output parks at 3 forever. Same plant as Paper 2, opposite ruling — the input decides.

5. In Paper 3, the plant is \(1/(s+1)\) instead of \(1/(s-1)\). What is the ruling and the limit?

The pole moves from \(z=e^{+T}=2.718\) to \(z=e^{-T}=0.368\), inside the unit circle. \(X(z)\)'s poles are also inside. The theorem is valid and, since the input decays to zero and the plant is stable, \(y(\infty)=0\).

6. Suppose the sampled input tail is \(x[k]=(-1)^{k}\). What does the final-value theorem say?

\(X(z)\) has a pole at \(z=-1\), on the unit circle. Multiplying by \((1-z^{-1})\) cancels nothing there, so the theorem is invalid: the samples alternate forever and have no limit. Only a simple pole at \(z=+1\) is permitted, and only because the theorem's own factor removes it. Every other unit-circle pole is fatal.

7. You are asked for \(y(2T)\) rather than the final value. Fastest route?

Do not do partial fractions. Long-divide \(Y(z)\) into a power series in \(z^{-1}\) and read the coefficient of \(z^{-2}\); or convert \(Y(z)=G(z)X(z)\) into the recurrence \(y[k]=\dots\) and iterate two steps from rest. Both are two-minute jobs and both double as checks on your closed form.
