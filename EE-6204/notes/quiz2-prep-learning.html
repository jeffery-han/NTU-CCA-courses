1 · Geometric & exponential: memoryless clocks

Prerequisite — conditional probability. \(P(A\mid B) = P(A\cap B)/P(B)\): restrict the world to the cases where \(B\) happened, then ask how often \(A\) also happened. Source: main-points-lecture-1.pdf p1.

The geometric random variable (discrete time)

Run independent trials, each a "success" (breakdown, completion…) with probability \(p\). Let \(X\) be the number of the trial on which the first success happens. For \(X = n\) we need \(n-1\) failures then a success:

\[ P(X = n) = (1-p)^{\,n-1}p,\qquad n = 1,2,\ldots \qquad P(X > m) = (1-p)^m,\qquad E(X) = \frac1p. \]
Why \(P(X>m)=(1-p)^m\): "first success after trial \(m\)" means the first \(m\) trials all failed. No sum is needed.

Memoryless property. Divide the two survival probabilities:

\[ P(X > m+n \mid X > m) = \frac{P(X>m+n)}{P(X>m)} = \frac{(1-p)^{m+n}}{(1-p)^m} = (1-p)^n = P(X>n). \]

Having survived \(m\) trials tells you nothing: the "clock" restarts. The geometric is the only discrete distribution with this property. Source: lecture-01-random-processes.pdf p3–6, p12.

Worked (lecture Eg 1.1). A tool breaks on each operation with \(p = 0.01\). Mean life \(=1/0.01 = 100\) operations. Already lasted 10: \(P(X > 40 \mid X > 10) = P(X>30) = 0.99^{30} = 0.7397\).

The exponential random variable (continuous time)

The continuous analogue: a lifetime \(X\ge 0\) with rate \(\lambda\) (events per unit time):

\[ f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x},\quad P(X > x) = e^{-\lambda x},\quad P(X\le x) = 1-e^{-\lambda x},\quad E(X) = \frac1\lambda. \]

Memoryless in the same way: \(P(X > x+y \mid X > x) = e^{-\lambda(x+y)}/e^{-\lambda x} = e^{-\lambda y}\). Source: lecture-01 p9–11.

Two facts that power every CTMC (lecture Problem 2.4). For independent \(X_1 = \mathrm{EXP}(\lambda_1)\), \(X_2 = \mathrm{EXP}(\lambda_2)\):

\[ \min(X_1, X_2) = \mathrm{EXP}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2), \qquad P(X_1 \lt X_2) = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}. \]
Why the minimum adds rates: \(P(\min > x) = P(X_1>x)P(X_2>x) = e^{-\lambda_1x}e^{-\lambda_2x} = e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)x}\). Intuitively, two independent alarm clocks ringing at rates \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) together ring at rate \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2\), and the share that clock 1 wins is its share of the total rate.
Pitfall — rate vs mean. "Mean repair time 30 min" means \(\lambda = 2\) per hour, not 30. Convert to one time unit before anything else.

2 · Stochastic processes & the Poisson process

A stochastic process \(\{X(t): t\in T\}\) is a family of random variables indexed by time. Two independent choices classify it: is time \(T\) countable (discrete time) or an interval (continuous time)? Is the state space \(S\) countable (a chain) or a continuum? Source: lecture-01 p12–15; main-points-lecture-1 p2.

The lecturer's classification convention (summary notes random-process-additional-notes-p1-19.pdf p5–8, taught Week 5). Decide the two axes separately: time is discrete if \(X\) is only defined at countable instants (hourly prices, "customers arriving between \(t\) and \(t+1\)" for \(t = 9, 10, \ldots\)); state is discrete if the values are countable. A stock price quoted in cents (\(15.80, 15.81, \ldots\)) is a discrete state; an average price or average spending over an interval (any value in \([15.80, 16.10]\)) is a continuous state.

The Poisson random variable (lecture-00 §7, flagged "very important" in Week 5)

\(X \sim P(\lambda)\) counts random, independent events in a fixed period or region with mean \(\lambda\):

\[ P(X=k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!},\quad k = 0,1,2,\ldots \qquad E(X) = \operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda. \]
  • Rescale \(\lambda\) to the period asked. Lecture Example 6.2: power failures at 4.2 per year → a 4-month period has \(\lambda = 1.4\), so \(P(\text{none}) = e^{-1.4} = 0.247\); a 6-month period has \(\lambda = 2.1\), so \(P(X\le 2) = e^{-2.1}(1 + 2.1 + 2.1^2/2) = 0.650\).
  • "More than" → complement. Lecture Example 7.1 (\(\lambda = 2\) per hour): \(P(X>3) = 1 - e^{-2}(1 + 2 + 4/2 + 8/6) = 0.143\).

The Poisson process below is exactly this random variable with \(\lambda\) replaced by \(\lambda t\). Source: lecture-00-probability-preliminaries.pdf p103–115 (printed p98–110).

The Poisson process

Let \(X(t)\) count arrivals in \([0,t]\). It is a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\) when

\[ P\big(X(t) = k\big) = \frac{e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^k}{k!},\quad k = 0,1,2,\ldots \qquad E[X(t)] = \lambda t. \]

Set \(k=0\): \(P(\text{no arrival in }[0,t]) = e^{-\lambda t}\). But "no arrival by \(t\)" is exactly "inter-arrival time \(T > t\)", so \(P(T>t) = e^{-\lambda t}\). Inter-arrival times are EXP(\(\lambda\)). The counting view and the clock view are the same process. Source: lecture-01 p15–17.

Superposition and decomposition (main-points-lecture-2):

  • Merging independent Poisson streams with rates \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n\) gives a Poisson stream with rate \(\sum\lambda_i\).
  • Splitting a rate-\(\lambda\) stream, sending each arrival to branch \(i\) with probability \(p_i\), gives Poisson streams with rates \(p_i\lambda\).

Worked (own numbers). Parts arrive from conveyor 1 at 4/h and conveyor 2 at 2/h. Merged rate 6/h.

  • P(no part in 20 min) \(= e^{-6\cdot(1/3)} = e^{-2} = 0.1353\).
  • P(exactly 3 parts in 30 min): \(\lambda t = 3\), \(e^{-3}3^3/3! = 0.2240\).
  • P(next part is from conveyor 1) \(= 4/6 = 2/3\).
Pitfall — the time unit inside \(\lambda t\). Rate per hour × time in hours. 30 minutes is \(t = 0.5\).

3 · DTMC: states, TPM, n steps, sojourn

A discrete-time Markov chain \(\{X_n\}\) on a countable \(S\) satisfies the Markov property: given the present state, the future is independent of the past,

\[ P(X_n = j \mid X_{n-1} = i, X_{n-2},\ldots,X_0) = P(X_n = j\mid X_{n-1}=i) = p_{ij}. \]

"Homogeneous" means \(p_{ij}\) does not depend on \(n\). Collect them in the transition probability matrix \(P = [p_{ij}]\): row = from, column = to, every entry in \([0,1]\), every row sums to 1 (from \(i\) you must go somewhere). The state transition diagram draws an arc \(i\to j\) labelled \(p_{ij}\) for every non-zero entry. Source: lecture-01 p20–22.

n-step transitions (Chapman–Kolmogorov)

To go \(i \to j\) in \(m+n\) steps you must be somewhere (\(k\)) after \(m\) steps. Total probability plus the Markov property give

\[ p_{ij}(m+n) = \sum_{k\in S} p_{ik}(m)\,p_{kj}(n) \;\Longrightarrow\; P(n) = P^n,\qquad \pi(n) = \pi(0)P^n, \]

where \(\pi(n) = [P(X_n=0),\,P(X_n=1),\ldots]\) is a row vector. Source: lecture-01 p34–36.

Reading \(p_{20}(2)\) by hand: row 2 of \(P\) dotted with column 0 of \(P\): \(\sum_k p_{2k}p_{k0}\). You never need the whole \(P^2\).

Sojourn time

Each step in state \(i\), you stay with probability \(p_{ii}\) and leave with probability \(1-p_{ii}\). So the number of steps spent in \(i\) per visit is geometric with success probability \(1-p_{ii}\), and

\[ E(T_i) = \frac{1}{1-p_{ii}}\ \text{steps}\quad(p_{ii} = 0 \Rightarrow 1;\ p_{ii}=1 \Rightarrow \infty,\ \text{absorbing}). \] Source: lecture-01 p23–24.

Worked (lecture Eg 5.1 / 6.1). A machine is working (0) or under repair (1), checked hourly; it fails in an hour w.p. \(a\), is repaired w.p. \(b\):

\[ P = \begin{pmatrix} 1-a & a\\ b & 1-b\end{pmatrix}. \]

With \(a = 0.2,\ b = 0.3\): \(p_{00}(2) = (0.8)(0.8) + (0.2)(0.3) = 0.70\); mean up-time \(=1/a = 5\) hours. Special cases from the lecture: \(a=b=0\) gives \(P=I\) (never moves); \(a=b=1\) gives \(P^n = I\) for even \(n\), \(P\) for odd \(n\) (it alternates; exercise Problem 6.2 has the 3-state version).

A second lecture model — two machines, no repair (lecture p30–31)

State = number of failed machines, each fails in an hour w.p. \(a\), independently. From 0: none fail \((1-a)^2\), exactly one \(2a(1-a)\), both \(a^2\). From 2 nothing can change: \(p_{22}=1\) (absorbing). Counting "exactly one" as \(a(1-a)+(1-a)a\) is the binomial step to remember.

4 · DTMC steady state

For a well-behaved chain, \(\pi(n)\) settles to a limit \(Y = [y_0, y_1, \ldots]\) that does not depend on where you started. Take the limit of \(\pi(n) = \pi(n-1)P\) on both sides:

\[ Y = YP,\qquad \sum_j y_j = 1,\qquad y_j\ge 0. \]

\(y_j\) is the long-run fraction of steps spent in state \(j\). Source: lecture-01 p39–44.

Solving recipe. \(Y=YP\) is one equation per column (\(y_j = \sum_i y_i p_{ij}\)). One of them is always redundant, so drop one (pick the messiest) and replace it by \(\sum y_j = 1\). Express everything in terms of one unknown, then normalise.

Worked (two-state). Column 0: \(y_0 = (1-a)y_0 + b y_1 \Rightarrow a y_0 = b y_1\). With \(y_0+y_1 = 1\): \(y_0 = b/(a+b),\ y_1 = a/(a+b)\). Interpretation: flow \(0\to1\) balances flow \(1\to0\).

Worked (exercise Problem 6.3). \(P = \begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.2&0.2\\0.1&0.8&0.1\\0.6&0&0.4\end{pmatrix}\). Column 1: \(y_1 = 0.2y_0 + 0.8y_1 \Rightarrow y_1 = y_0\). Column 2: \(y_2 = 0.2y_0 + 0.1y_1 + 0.4y_2 \Rightarrow 0.6y_2 = 0.3y_0 \Rightarrow y_2 = y_0/2\). Normalise: \(y_0(1+1+\tfrac12) = 1 \Rightarrow Y = (0.4, 0.4, 0.2)\), matching the lecturer's answer sheet. Source: exercises-solution.pdf.

Pitfall — rows vs columns. \(Y = YP\) multiplies a row vector on the left, so each equation uses a column of \(P\). Using rows gives \(\sum_j p_{ij} = 1\), which is useless.
Tool — steady-state solver (check your hand work; enter a DTMC TPM or a CTMC generator Q)

5 · Building an inventory DTMC (the lecturer's favourite)

This is Question 1 of two-questions-random-process-queue.pdf, and a DTMC state-space + TPM question appears in all 7 archived final exams (2015–2023), several of them inventory models with Poisson demand or usage (spare bulbs, cans…). Learn the procedure.

Problem (lecturer's sheet). A shop stocks PCs. Weekly demands \(D_i\) are i.i.d. Poisson(\(\lambda = 1\)). \(X_t\) = PCs on hand at the end of week \(t\). On Saturday night: if \(X_t = 0\), order 3 (arrive Monday); otherwise order nothing.

Step 1 — state space and the update rule

End-of-week stock can be 0, 1, 2 or 3, so \(S = \{0,1,2,3\}\). Write the update in two cases, because the policy changes the stock before demand hits:

\[ X_{t+1} = \begin{cases} \max(3 - D_{t+1},\,0), & X_t = 0\ \ (\text{restocked to }3)\\ \max(X_t - D_{t+1},\,0), & X_t \ge 1. \end{cases} \]
Why \(\max(\cdot,0)\): unmet demand is lost, and stock cannot be negative. So every demand ≥ current stock lands in state 0. That is a tail probability \(P(D\ge k)\), not a point probability.

Step 2 — the demand probabilities you need

\(P(D=0)=e^{-1}=0.3679\), \(P(D=1)=0.3679\), \(P(D=2)=0.1839\), \(P(D\ge 1)=0.6321\), \(P(D\ge2)=0.2642\), \(P(D\ge3)=0.0803\).

Step 3 — the TPM row by row

            to 0     to 1     to 2     to 3
from 0   [ 0.0803   0.1839   0.3679   0.3679 ]   start with 3: P(D≥3), P(D=2), P(D=1), P(D=0)
from 1   [ 0.6321   0.3679   0        0      ]   start with 1
from 2   [ 0.2642   0.3679   0.3679   0      ]   start with 2
from 3   [ 0.0803   0.1839   0.3679   0.3679 ]   start with 3 — same as row 0

Check: every row sums to 1. Rows 0 and 3 are equal because both weeks start with 3 PCs.

Step 4 — steady state

Solve \(Y = YP\), \(\sum y = 1\) (use the solver above to confirm): \(Y \approx (0.2858,\ 0.2847,\ 0.2631,\ 0.1663)\). Interpretation: an order is placed in about 28.6 % of weeks; mean sojourn in state 1 is \(1/(1-0.3679) = 1.582\) weeks.

Pitfall — forgetting the restock. Writing row 0 as \([1,0,0,0]\) makes 0 absorbing and the whole steady state collapses to "always empty". Read the policy first.
Check 5.1
Same shop. Why is \(p_{10} = 0.6321\) and not \(P(D=1) = 0.3679\)? Type the value of \(p_{10}\). 4 d.p.
Check 5.2
The policy changes to "if stock is 0 or 1, top up to 3". Which rows of the TPM become identical?

6 · CTMC: rates, generator, rate balance

A continuous-time Markov chain obeys the same Markov property, but at every instant. Because "how long you've been here" cannot matter, the time spent in state \(i\) must be memoryless, so it is exponential: \(T_i = \mathrm{EXP}(\lambda_i)\) (\(\lambda_i = 0\): absorbing). Source: lecture-01 p46–49.

The generator (transition rate matrix) Q

For \(i\ne j\), \(q_{ij}\) is the rate of jumping \(i\to j\). The diagonal makes each row sum to zero:

\[ q_{ii} = -\sum_{j\ne i} q_{ij}\quad(\text{minus the total out-rate}),\qquad \sum_j q_{ij} = 0. \]

Sojourn in \(i\) is \(\mathrm{EXP}(-q_{ii})\) (the min of all competing exits, Module 1). The chain evolves by \(d\pi(t)/dt = \pi(t)Q\) (forward Kolmogorov, solution \(\pi(t) = \pi(0)e^{Qt}\)). Source: lecture-01 p53–57.

Steady state: \(\pi Q = 0\) = rate balance

In steady state \(d\pi/dt = 0\), so \(\pi Q = 0\) with \(\sum\pi_j = 1\). Column \(j\) of that equation rearranges to

\[ \pi_j \sum_{k\ne j} q_{jk} \;=\; \sum_{k\ne j}\pi_k q_{kj}\qquad \text{(rate out of } j = \text{rate into } j\text{)}. \]
Read it off the diagram: for each state, (probability of being there) × (sum of arrows out) = Σ (probability of each neighbour) × (its arrow in). No matrix algebra is needed. Source: lecture-01 p58–59, eq. (65)–(67).

Worked (lecture Eg 7.1 / 9.1, resume policy)

States: 0 setup (rate \(s\)), 1 processing (rate \(p\), may fail at rate \(f\)), 2 repair (rate \(r\)); after repair, resume the same part (2→1).

\[ Q = \begin{pmatrix} -s & s & 0\\ p & -(p+f) & f\\ 0 & r & -r\end{pmatrix},\qquad \begin{aligned} \pi_0 s &= \pi_1 p\\ \pi_2 r &= \pi_1 f\\ \pi_0+\pi_1+\pi_2 &= 1\end{aligned} \;\Rightarrow\; \pi_0 = \frac{pr}{pr+rs+fs},\ \pi_1 = \frac{rs}{pr+rs+fs},\ \pi_2 = \frac{fs}{pr+rs+fs}. \]

With \(s=20,\ p=4,\ f=0.05,\ r=1\) per hour: \(\pi = (0.16,\ 0.80,\ 0.04)\). Production rate \(R = p\,\pi_1 = 3.2\) parts/h: parts finish only while processing, at rate \(p\). Under the discard policy (2→0, fresh setup) \(R\) drops to 3.08. Source: lecture-01 p61–67.

Pitfall — \(R = s\pi_0\)? That counts setups completed, and some of those parts are later lost to failures (in discard models). Output is counted where the part finishes: \(R = p\pi_1\).
Pitfall — sign of \(q_{ii}\). Diagonal entries are negative (or 0). A positive diagonal is always wrong.

Exercise variants the lecturer set (lecture p68–69; answer to 9.4 in exercises-solution.pdf): batches of \(n\) parts per setup (Problem 9.3); after a failure, rework w.p. \(q\) (back to 1) or discard w.p. \(1-q\) (back to 0), so the repair state's exits are \(qr\) and \((1-q)r\) (Problem 9.4).

7 · Birth–death processes

A CTMC on \(\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\) that only moves one step at a time: up \(k\to k+1\) at rate \(\lambda_k\) (birth), down \(k\to k-1\) at rate \(\mu_k\) (death). Balancing the flow across the "cut" between \(k-1\) and \(k\) gives \(\lambda_{k-1}\pi_{k-1} = \mu_k\pi_k\). Chain these together:

\[ \pi_k = \pi_0\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\lambda_i}{\mu_{i+1}},\qquad \pi_0 = \Big[1 + \sum_{k\ge1}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\lambda_i}{\mu_{i+1}}\Big]^{-1}. \]

Source: lecture-01 p70–72, eq. (76)–(79). Every queue in Module 8 is this formula with particular \(\lambda_k,\mu_k\).

Why the cut argument works: in the long run, the process crosses the boundary between \(k-1\) and \(k\) upward as often as downward (it cannot cross twice in a row the same way), so the two flows are equal.
Check 7.1
A birth–death chain on \(\{0,1,2\}\) has \(\lambda_0 = 2,\ \lambda_1 = 1,\ \mu_1 = 2,\ \mu_2 = 2\). Find \(\pi_0\) as a fraction in simplest form.

8 · Queues

Notation \(A/S/m/K/L\): arrival process / service distribution / servers / capacity / population; "M" = Markovian (Poisson arrivals or exponential service). Symbols (all steady state): \(L\) mean number in system, \(Q\) mean number in queue, \(W\) mean time in system, \(D\) mean time in queue, \(\rho\) utilisation. Source: lecture-02-queuing-models.pdf p4–5; queue-summary.pdf.

Little's result — the one law you must know (not on the appendix)

\[ L = \lambda W,\qquad Q = \lambda D,\qquad W = D + \frac1\mu. \]
Intuition: if 9 jobs arrive per hour and each stays 1/3 hour, then on average \(9\times\tfrac13 = 3\) are inside. So you only ever need one of \(L, Q, W, D\); Little gives the rest. Also: utilisation \(U = 1-\pi_0\), production rate \(=(1-\pi_0)\mu\) for one server.

M/M/1 (birth–death with \(\lambda_k=\lambda,\ \mu_k=\mu\))

The product formula gives \(\pi_k = \rho^k\pi_0\) with \(\rho = \lambda/\mu\). The geometric series converges only if \(\rho\lt 1\) (stability), then \(\pi_0 = 1-\rho\) and

\[ L = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho},\quad W = \frac{L}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{\mu-\lambda},\quad Q = L - \rho,\quad D = W - \frac1\mu. \]

Eg 3.1: \(\lambda=8,\ \mu=10\) → \(\rho=0.8,\ L=4,\ Q=3.2,\ W=0.5\) h, \(D=0.4\) h. Idle fraction \(=\pi_0 = 0.2\). The number in system \(N\) is a modified geometric r.v. (\(N+1\) is geometric with parameter \(1-\rho\)), which is where \(\pi_k = \rho^k(1-\rho)\) comes from. Source: lecture-02 p5–12; queue-handwritten-notes p7.1.

M/M/1 waiting-time distributions — taught in Week 8, not on the appendix

Under FIFO, a customer who finds \(k\) others waits for \(k\) exponential services (an Erlang-\(k\) sum). Summing over \(\pi_k\) gives (lecture-02 p13–16):

\[ P(d = 0) = 1-\rho,\qquad F_d(t) = P(d\le t) = 1 - \rho\,e^{-\mu(1-\rho)t}\ (t>0),\qquad F_w(t) = P(w\le t) = 1 - e^{-(\mu-\lambda)t}. \]

So the time in system \(w\) is exactly EXP(\(\mu-\lambda\)) (mean \(W\)), while the time in queue has an atom \(1-\rho\) at zero (arrive to an empty system, no wait). Tail probabilities: \(P(w>t) = e^{-(\mu-\lambda)t}\), \(P(d>t) = \rho e^{-\mu(1-\rho)t}\). The means \(W, D\) do not depend on the queue discipline; these distributions assume FIFO.

Pitfall. \(P(d>t)\) has the factor \(\rho\) in front; dropping it treats every customer as waiting. And \(\mu(1-\rho) = \mu-\lambda\): both exponents use the same rate.

M/M/1/N (room for N in total)

Arrivals are blocked when \(N\) are present (\(\lambda_k = 0\) for \(k\ge N\)). Finite chain, so stable for any \(\rho\): \(\pi_k = \rho^k(1-\rho)/(1-\rho^{N+1})\). The blocking probability is \(\pi_N\) (an arrival sees the time-average state). Eg 4.1 (\(N=4,\ \rho=0.8\)): \(\pi_0 = 0.297\). Source: lecture-02 p16–20.

Convention trap. The appendix's M/M/1/N \(W\) and \(D\) equal \(L/\lambda\) and \(Q/\lambda\) with the offered \(\lambda\), which is what lecture Eg 4.1 does (\(W = 1.566/8\)). Physically, Little's law uses the accepted rate \(\lambda(1-\pi_N)\). If a quiz box asks for W of M/M/1/N, the appendix formula is the safe choice.

M/M/m (m servers, one shared queue)

Deaths at rate \(k\mu\) while \(k\lt m\) servers are busy, \(m\mu\) once all are busy. With \(\rho = \lambda/(m\mu)\) (note the \(m\)!):

\[ \pi_0 = \Big[\frac{(m\rho)^m}{m!(1-\rho)} + \sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{(m\rho)^k}{k!}\Big]^{-1},\qquad Q = \frac{\rho(m\rho)^m\pi_0}{m!(1-\rho)^2},\qquad L = Q + \frac\lambda\mu. \]

Average busy servers \(=\lambda/\mu\); each server's utilisation \(=\rho\), so each is idle a fraction \(1-\rho\). Source: lecture-02 p22–27.

Mᵇ/M/1 (batches of b)

Batches arrive at rate \(\lambda\), each with \(b\) customers: \(\rho = b\lambda/\mu\), \(L = \rho(1+b)/(2(1-\rho))\), and Little uses the customer rate: \(W = L/(\lambda b)\). Eg 6.1 (\(b=2,\ \lambda=4,\ \mu=10\)): same \(\rho = 0.8\) as Eg 3.1 but \(L = 6\) vs 4, because each part waits for its batch-mates. Source: lecture-02 p28–33.

Worked — the lecturer's Question 2 (two-questions sheet)

Two queues, arrivals 6/h and 4/h, service mean 5 min (\(\mu=12\)/h).

  • (a) Separate M/M/1s. Queue 1: \(\rho=1/2\), \(L=1\), idle \(1/2\). Queue 2: \(\rho=1/3\), \(L=1/2\), idle \(2/3\). Total in system 1.5.
  • (b) Pooled M/M/2, \(\lambda = 10\): \(\rho = 10/24 = 5/12\), \(m\rho = 5/6\), \(\pi_0 = 7/17\), \(Q = 125/714\), \(L = 120/119 \approx 1.008\); each server idle \(7/12\).
  • (c) Pooling cuts the number in system from 1.5 to 1.008 with the same servers. Assumptions: Poisson arrivals, exponential service, FIFO, steady state, customers go to any free server.

Lecture p27 adds the other side: one server twice as fast (M/M/1 with \(2\mu\)) beats M/M/2 on \(W\), and M/M/2 beats two separate M/M/1 queues.
