    EE6221 · Kalman filter sprint (Quiz 2 scope)

    About 30 minutes. This covers only what Lecture 4 can test on a closed-book, no-calculator quiz: the idea, the model and its assumptions, the gain, the variance update, two-sensor fusion, and the True/False conditions. Every part ends with a check, so do it before you move on. Click a slide reference to see the page.

    The one idea to keep. A Kalman update fuses two things: your current estimate (uncertainty $P$) and a new measurement (uncertainty $R$). The more certain one gets more weight, and the result is more certain than either. If you already know $\dfrac1p = \dfrac1{\sigma_1^2} + \dfrac1{\sigma_2^2}$, you already know the core of the Kalman filter.

    Part 1 · Why a Kalman filter?

    Several sensors (or one sensor over time) measure the same thing. The simplest way to fuse them is a weighted average, with bigger weights for better sensors. It is fast and works in real time.

    The lecture's point is that the Kalman filter is usually preferred. It needs nearly the same processing as a weighted average, and unlike the weighted average its estimate is optimal in a statistical sense. “Statistically optimal” just means the estimate has the smallest possible error variance on average over the noise. Lec 4 pp. 2–3

    It is also recursive. Each step uses only the previous estimate, its variance and the new measurement, never the whole history. That is why it suits “systems without large data storage capabilities”. Lec 4 p. 17

    Check 1a · The weighted average is preferred over the Kalman filter because it is statistically optimal.

    Answer

    False. The Kalman filter is the statistically optimal one (minimum error variance), at nearly the same processing cost, so it is the one usually preferred. Lec 4 p. 3

    Check 1b · The Kalman filter must store all past measurements to compute the current estimate.

    Answer

    False. It is recursive: it needs only the last estimate, its variance and the new measurement, so little storage is needed. Lec 4 p. 17

    Part 2 · The model and its assumptions

    The quiz uses the scalar (single-number) version. There are two equations: how the state moves, and what the sensor sees. Lec 4 p. 25

    $$x_{k+1} = A\,x_k + B\,u_k + w_k \qquad\text{(state: where the thing really is)}$$ $$z_k = H\,x_k + v_k \qquad\text{(measurement: what the sensor reports)}$$

    • $x_k$ is the true state (e.g. position, or an object's shape). It is never seen directly.
    • $A$ says how the state evolves. $A = 1$ means “it stays the same”.
    • $B\,u_k$ is a known input. Ignore it on the quiz; it is usually 0.
    • $H$ says how the sensor sees the state. $H = 1$ means “the sensor measures $x$ directly”.
    • $w_k$ is process noise with variance $Q$ (the world is not perfectly predictable).
    • $v_k$ is measurement noise with variance $R$ (the sensor is not perfect).
    • $P_k$ is the variance of the estimation error $\tilde x_k = x_k - \hat x_k$, i.e. how unsure the current estimate is.

    Assumption (worth marks): $w$ and $v$ are uncorrelated, zero-mean, white Gaussian noise sequences. “Uncorrelated” is what makes the cross terms vanish in the derivation (Part 6). Lec 4 p. 21

    The optimal filtering equations also assume all the model parameters ($\Phi$/$A$, $B$, $G$, $H$, $Q$, $R$) are known. Lec 4 p. 22

    Check 2a · The process noise $w$ and the measurement noise $v$ are assumed to be correlated.

    Answer

    False. They are assumed uncorrelated zero-mean white Gaussian sequences, which is why the cross terms drop out of the variance. Lec 4 p. 21

    Check 2b · For a stationary object measured directly by a sensor, $A = 1$ and $H = 1$.

    Answer

    True. The state does not change ($x_{k+1} = x_k$, so $A = 1$) and the sensor reads the state plus noise ($z = x + v$, so $H = 1$). This is the lecture's shape-recognition example. Lec 4 pp. 33–34

    Part 3 · The update and the gain K

    The new estimate is the prediction plus a correction: $$\hat x_{k+1} = A\hat x_k + B u_k + K_k\,\big(z_k - H\hat x_k\big).$$ The bracket is the innovation, “what I measured minus what I expected to measure”. The Kalman gain $K$ decides how much of that surprise to believe. Lec 4 p. 26

    The design rule is to choose $K$ to minimise the variance of the new estimate error. That gives (derivation in Part 6) Lec 4 p. 29 p. 31 $$K_k = \frac{A H P_k}{H^2 P_k + R}, \qquad P_{k+1} = A^2P_k + Q - \frac{(AHP_k)^2}{H^2P_k + R}.$$

    Read K by intuition (with $A = H = 1$, $K = \dfrac{P}{P+R}$):

    • $R \to 0$ (perfect sensor): $K \to 1$, so the filter trusts the measurement fully.
    • $R \to \infty$ (useless sensor): $K \to 0$, so it ignores the measurement.
    • $P$ large (unsure estimate): $K \to 1$, so it leans on the measurement.

    Saying “a larger R gives a larger K”. R sits in the denominator: a noisier sensor is trusted less.

    Check 3a · $A = H = 1$, $P = 6$, $R = 3$. Find $K$ (a fraction is fine).

    Hint

    $K = P/(P+R)$.

    Answer

    $K = 6/(6+3) = 2/3$. The estimate is unsure ($P = 6$) compared with the sensor ($R = 3$), so the filter leans toward the measurement.

    Check 3b · Increasing the measurement noise variance $R$ increases the Kalman gain.

    Answer

    False. $K = AHP/(H^2P + R)$ falls as $R$ grows. A noisier sensor gets less weight, and as $R \to \infty$, $K \to 0$. Lec 4 p. 29

    Part 4 · The shortcut: A = H = 1, Q = 0 is just fusion

    This is the case the quiz is most likely to use, a constant state measured directly. Substitute $A = H = 1$ and $Q = 0$ into Part 3:

    1. $K = \dfrac{P}{P+R}$
    2. $P_{\text{new}} = P - \dfrac{P^2}{P+R} = \dfrac{P(P+R) - P^2}{P+R} = \dfrac{PR}{P+R}$ (put everything over one denominator)
    3. Take reciprocals: $\dfrac{1}{P_{\text{new}}} = \dfrac{P+R}{PR} = \dfrac1R + \dfrac1P$

    So $\boxed{\dfrac{1}{P_{\text{new}}} = \dfrac1P + \dfrac1R}$. This is exactly the two-sensor fusion formula, with the old estimate playing “sensor 1” and the measurement playing “sensor 2”. Equivalently, $P_{\text{new}} = (1-K)P$.

    Consequence (a favourite True/False): after a measurement, the variance always goes down and ends below both $P$ and $R$. A variance that grows after an update is a wrong answer.

    Check 4a · $A = H = 1$, $Q = 0$, $P = 4$, $R = 4$. Find $K$ and $P_{\text{new}}$.

    Hint

    Equal uncertainties: who should get more weight?

    Answer

    $K = 4/8 = 1/2$ (equal trust). $P_{\text{new}} = 4\cdot4/8 = 2$, or $1/P_{\text{new}} = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2$. Two equally good pieces of information halve the variance.

    Check 4b · Same setup with $P = 6$, $R = 3$. Find $P_{\text{new}}$.

    Answer

    $P_{\text{new}} = 6\cdot3/9 = 2$. Check: $1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2$. Also $(1-K)P = (1/3)(6) = 2$. All three routes agree.

    Part 5 · Two sensors: batch and sequential

    The lecture's example: sensors $S_1$ and $S_2$ measure the same constant $x$, with $z_1 = x + v_1$ and $z_2 = x + v_2$. The noises are independent, zero-mean, with variances $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$. Lec 4 pp. 33–35

    Batch (both readings at once): choose $K_1, K_2$ to minimise the variance. The result is Lec 4 pp. 38–40 $$\hat x = \frac{\sigma_2^2\,z_1 + \sigma_1^2\,z_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}, \qquad P = \frac{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}.$$ Notice the weight on $z_1$ uses the other sensor's variance $\sigma_2^2$. So the less noisy sensor gets the bigger weight, and $P$ is below both $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$. Lec 4 p. 43

    Sequential (one reading after another): start from sensor 1, $\hat x_0 = z_1$ and $P_0 = \sigma_1^2$. Then apply Part 4 with $R = \sigma_2^2$: Lec 4 pp. 41–42 $$K = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}, \qquad \hat x = z_1 + K(z_2 - z_1).$$ Both routes give the same answer. (The tutor's comparison adds that batch needs more computation at once, while sequential handles readings as they arrive.)

    Mixing up $K$ with the weight on $z_1$. In the sequential form, $K$ multiplies the innovation $(z_2 - z_1)$; the weight on $z_1$ is $1 - K$.

    Check 5a · $z_1 = 10$ with $\sigma_1^2 = 1$, and $z_2 = 13$ with $\sigma_2^2 = 2$. Find the fused $\hat x$ and $P$.

    Hint

    Weights $\sigma_2^2 : \sigma_1^2 = 2 : 1$, so $z_1$ counts double.

    Answer

    $\hat x = (2\cdot10 + 1\cdot13)/(1+2) = 33/3 = 11$ and $P = (1\cdot2)/3 = 2/3$. Sequential check: $K = 1/3$, $\hat x = 10 + \tfrac13(3) = 11$ and $P = (1-\tfrac13)(1) = \tfrac23$. The estimate sits closer to the better sensor's reading.

    Check 5b · In optimal two-sensor fusion, the sensor with the larger variance receives the larger weight.

    Answer

    False. The weight on $z_1$ is $\sigma_2^2/(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$, so the measurement with the least variance gets the greatest weight. Lec 4 p. 43

    Part 6 · If a derivation appears: K in five lines

    Only needed if the quiz asks you to “derive the gain”. Every step uses one of two tools: substitute and drop cross terms because the quantities are uncorrelated (the V(X+Y) fact). Lec 4 pp. 26–29

    1. Error: $\tilde x_{k+1} = x_{k+1} - \hat x_{k+1}$. Substitute both equations: $\tilde x_{k+1} = (A - K H)\,\tilde x_k + w_k - K v_k$.
    2. Variance: square and take expectations. $\tilde x$, $w$ and $v$ are uncorrelated, so the cross terms vanish: $P_{k+1} = (A - KH)^2 P_k + Q + K^2 R$.
    3. Minimise over K: $\dfrac{\partial P_{k+1}}{\partial K} = -2H(A - KH)P_k + 2KR = 0$.
    4. Solve: $K(H^2P_k + R) = AHP_k$, so $K = \dfrac{AHP_k}{H^2P_k + R}$.
    5. Substitute back for the minimum variance: $P_{k+1} = A^2P_k + Q - \dfrac{(AHP_k)^2}{H^2P_k + R}$.

    Check 6a · On paper, without looking: write steps 1–4 above for the case $A = H = 1$, $Q = 0$, and show $K = P/(P+R)$.

    Answer

    $\tilde x_{k+1} = (1-K)\tilde x_k - Kv$, so $P_{k+1} = (1-K)^2P + K^2R$. Then $\dfrac{d}{dK}$: $-2(1-K)P + 2KR = 0$, so $K(P+R) = P$ and $K = \dfrac{P}{P+R}$. The marks are for saying why the cross term $2K(1-K)E[\tilde x v]$ is zero: $\tilde x$ and $v$ are uncorrelated and zero-mean.

    Part 7 · True/False conditions (the likeliest marks)

    • Optimal when the system is linear and the process and measurement noise are (unimodal) white Gaussian. Otherwise it is suboptimal, and the alternatives are the EKF, UKF, particle filter and MCMC/RANSAC. Lec 4 p. 45 p. 17
    • Recursive, so little storage is needed. Lec 4 p. 17
    • The optimal equations need the model parameters known. Lec 4 p. 22
    • It is used to fuse dynamic, low-level, redundant data in real time; it was originally a state-estimation method, later extended to tracking and fusion. Lec 4 pp. 17–18
    • The fused variance is below every individual variance. Lec 4 p. 43

    7a · The Kalman filter gives statistically optimal estimates for any nonlinear system.

    Answer

    False. It is optimal only for a linear system with white Gaussian noise; for nonlinear systems it is suboptimal, and the EKF, UKF or a particle filter is used. Lec 4 p. 45

    7b · The Kalman filter is appropriate for fusing dynamic, low-level redundant sensor data in real time.

    Answer

    True. That is its stated use in multi-sensor systems; its recursive form makes the real-time use practical. Lec 4 p. 17

    7c · After a Kalman measurement update, the error variance can become larger than before the measurement.

    Answer

    False. With the optimal gain, $P_{\text{new}} = (1-K)P \le P$ (for $A = H = 1$, $1/P_{\text{new}} = 1/P + 1/R$). Adding information never increases the uncertainty.

    7d · As the measurement noise variance $R \to 0$, the Kalman gain tends to 1 (with $A = H = 1$), so the estimate follows the measurement.

    Answer

    True. $K = P/(P+R) \to P/P = 1$, so $\hat x_{\text{new}} = \hat x + 1\cdot(z - \hat x) = z$. A perfect sensor is fully trusted. Lec 4 p. 29

    One-screen summary

    • Model: $x_{k+1} = Ax_k + w$ and $z = Hx + v$, with $w, v$ uncorrelated, zero-mean, white Gaussian (variances $Q$, $R$).
    • Update: $\hat x \leftarrow$ prediction $+\;K\,(z - H\hat x)$.
    • Gain: $K = \dfrac{AHP}{H^2P + R}$. With $A = H = 1$, $K = \dfrac{P}{P+R}$; small $R$ means large $K$.
    • Variance: with $A = H = 1$, $Q = 0$: $\dfrac1{P_{\text{new}}} = \dfrac1P + \dfrac1R$, and it always decreases.
    • Two sensors: $\hat x = \dfrac{\sigma_2^2z_1 + \sigma_1^2z_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}$ and $P = \dfrac{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}$; the better sensor gets the bigger weight; batch and sequential agree.
    • Conditions: optimal if linear and Gaussian (else EKF, UKF, particle filter); recursive, so little storage; parameters must be known; preferred over the weighted average.
