0 · Why two pictures instead of one

The arm is three-dimensional, but you never need to draw it in 3D. The reason is that the three joints split cleanly into two groups:

  • \(\theta_1\) (waist) swings the whole arm about the vertical column. It changes which vertical plane the arm lives in, and nothing else.
  • \(\theta_2,\theta_3\) (shoulder, elbow) move the arm inside that plane. Their axes are horizontal and parallel, so they act like a flat two-link arm.

So draw a top view to see what \(\theta_1\) does, and a side view of the arm's own vertical plane to see what \(\theta_2,\theta_3\) do. Every answer in parts (a)–(d) reads off one of those two pictures. Drag the sliders and watch which picture reacts.

Fixed link geometry, as given on the paper: \(d_1=4\), \(a_2=3\), \(a_3=2\), \(L_t=1\) — so \(L_3\equiv a_3+L_t=3\).

1 · Part (a) — the frames, and where their origins sit

Two rules generate every frame, and they are worth separating because students usually apply the first and guess the second:

  • \(z_{k-1}\) is the axis of joint \(k\). So \(z_0\) is the waist axis (vertical); \(z_1\) is the shoulder axis (horizontal); \(z_2\) is the elbow axis, which is parallel to the shoulder axis. Three joints, three axes — and note that this leaves \(z_3\) unaccounted for, because no joint sits at the end of the last link. Its direction is a convention you choose, not a physical fact; the detail box in part (d) works through it.
  • Each \(x_k\) is the common normal from \(z_{k-1}\) to \(z_k\). Between the two parallel axes that common normal runs straight along the link — which is exactly why \(a_2\) and \(a_3\) end up being the link lengths.

The origins then fall on physical landmarks. This is the part my written key originally got wrong, so it is worth watching move:

frameorigin \((x,y,z)\)landmarkdistance from previous

Drag \(\theta_2\) and watch the frame-2 origin travel: it is pinned to the elbow, a distance \(a_2=3\) from the shoulder, never sitting on top of frame 1.

Why \(a_1=0\) does not mean frames 1 and 2 coincide

\(a_1=0\) is a statement about row 1 only: the shoulder axis intersects the column axis, so there is no common normal to step along, and frame 1's origin stays on the column. It says nothing whatsoever about frame 2.

Where frame \(k\)'s origin lands is decided by the translation column of \({}^{k-1}T_k\), which is \((a_kc_k,\;a_ks_k,\;d_k)\): \(d_k\) steps along \(z_{k-1}\), then \(a_k\) steps along the new \(x_k\). Row 2 carries \(a_2=3\), so frame 2 is displaced a full link. The table above recomputes those distances live — they stay at \(3,3,2\) whatever the angles do, because they are link lengths, not projections.

Set \(\theta_2 = 90^\circ\). Where is the origin of frame 2?

2 · Part (b) — the D–H table

Three joints, so three rows, and every variable is a \(\theta\) because every joint is revolute. Click a row to highlight what it does in the side view.

\(k\)\(\theta_k\)\(d_k\)\(a_k\)\(\alpha_k\)variablecurrent value

The single most important entry is \(\alpha_1=+90^\circ\). It is the whole reason this arm can reach up and down instead of sweeping a flat disc.

Where \(\alpha_1=+90^\circ\) comes from, and what \(\alpha_1=0\) would cost you

\(\alpha_k\) is the angle from \(z_{k-1}\) to \(z_k\), measured about \(x_k\). Here \(z_0\) is vertical (the waist axis) and \(z_1\) is horizontal (the shoulder axis), so they are perpendicular: \(\alpha_1=\pm90^\circ\). Either sign is acceptable provided the rest of your table and diagram follow it consistently.

If you write \(\alpha_1=0\) instead, you are claiming the shoulder axis is parallel to the column. Then all three axes are parallel, every link stays in one horizontal plane, and the arm becomes a flat SCARA-like mechanism that cannot change the tool's height at all — the \(z\) coordinate of your arm matrix would collapse to the constant \(d_1\). That is why the key gives the row zero rather than partial credit.

Rows 2 and 3 then carry \(\alpha=0\) precisely because the shoulder and elbow axes are parallel — a twist there would be the opposite error.

Why is \(\alpha_2=0\) rather than \(\pm90^\circ\)?

3 · Part (c) — the three link transforms

Once the table is right, each matrix is pure substitution into the template — this is the cheapest mark on the paper and should never be left blank:

\[ {}^{k-1}T_k=\begin{bmatrix} c_k & -s_kc_{\alpha k} & s_ks_{\alpha k} & a_kc_k\\ s_k & c_kc_{\alpha k} & -c_ks_{\alpha k} & a_ks_k\\ 0 & s_{\alpha k} & c_{\alpha k} & d_k\\ 0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \]

Symbolic forms, for comparison with what you wrote:

\[ {}^{0}T_1=\begin{bmatrix}c_1&0&s_1&0\\ s_1&0&-c_1&0\\ 0&1&0&d_1\\ 0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad {}^{1}T_2=\begin{bmatrix}c_2&-s_2&0&a_2c_2\\ s_2&c_2&0&a_2s_2\\ 0&0&1&0\\ 0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad {}^{2}T_3=\begin{bmatrix}c_3&-s_3&0&a_3c_3\\ s_3&c_3&0&a_3s_3\\ 0&0&1&0\\ 0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \]
Reading \({}^{0}T_1\): the \(\alpha_1=90^\circ\) twist is what scrambles the columns

Substituting \(\alpha_1=90^\circ\) gives \(c_{\alpha1}=0\) and \(s_{\alpha1}=1\). Column 2 of the rotation block becomes \((-s_1\cdot 0,\ c_1\cdot 0,\ 1)=(0,0,1)\) and column 3 becomes \((s_1,-c_1,0)\). So after row 1 the frame's \(y\) axis points straight up and its \(z\) axis lies horizontal — which is the shoulder axis, as required.

Note also that \(d_1\) lands in the third slot of the translation column, \((0,0,d_1)\), because \(\mathrm{Trans}(z,d)\) always translates along \(z\). Putting a \(d\) into the \(x\) or \(y\) slot is the single most common arithmetic slip in part (c).

In \({}^{0}T_1\), which slot of the translation column holds \(d_1\)?

4 · Part (d) — the arm matrix, and the checks that catch sign errors

Multiply base-to-tool, left to right. Folding the tool into row 3 as \(a_3+L_t\) — as the key permits — takes you straight to the tool tip:

Symbolically, with \(L_3=a_3+L_t\) and \(c_{23}=\cos(\theta_2+\theta_3)\):

\[ {}^{0}T_{\text{tool}}=\begin{bmatrix} c_1c_{23} & -c_1s_{23} & s_1 & c_1(a_2c_2+L_3c_{23})\\ s_1c_{23} & -s_1s_{23} & -c_1 & s_1(a_2c_2+L_3c_{23})\\ s_{23} & c_{23} & 0 & d_1+a_2s_2+L_3s_{23}\\ 0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \]

Read the structure rather than memorising it: the bracket \(r=a_2c_2+L_3c_{23}\) is the radial reach you can see in the side view, and \(\theta_1\) merely paints it onto \(x\) and \(y\) through \(c_1,s_1\). The height \(z=d_1+a_2s_2+L_3s_{23}\) does not involve \(\theta_1\) at all — swinging the waist cannot raise the tool. Watch the \(\theta_1\) slider: the third row never moves.

Careful: \(z_3\) is not the reach direction — and it is not a joint axis either

It is natural to expect the frame's \(z\) to point along the tool, because the course's tool-frame convention \(R=[\,r_1\ r_2\ r_3\,]\) = [normal, sliding, approach] puts the approach vector third, i.e. along \(z\). But that convention describes a tool frame, not a D–H link frame.

The D–H rule is precise: \(z_{k-1}\) is the axis of joint \(k\). So \(z_0\) is the waist axis, \(z_1\) the shoulder axis, \(z_2\) the elbow axis — all three joints, accounted for by \(z_0,z_1,z_2\). \(z_3\) is not a joint axis at all: there is no joint at frame 3, and nothing downstream constrains it.

So why is it drawn parallel to \(z_2\)? Because we chose \(\alpha_3=0\). The last row's \(\alpha\) and \(d\) are not pinned by a next joint axis, so you pick them for convenience, and \(0\) is the simplest choice. Pick \(\alpha_3=\pm90^\circ\) and \(z_3\) points elsewhere; the arm matrix changes to match and is equally correct.

What is not a free choice is that the arm reaches along \(x_3\). That follows from the construction: \(\theta_3\) swings \(x\) onto link 3, and \(\mathrm{Trans}(x,a_3)\) then walks along it — which is exactly why the tool length \(a_3+L_t\) sits in the \(a\) column.

Check it at the exam values: \(R=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\\0&-1&0\\1&0&0\end{bmatrix}\). Column 3 is \((1,0,0)=+x_0\) — horizontal, perpendicular to the arm's plane. Column 1 is \((0,0,1)\), straight up, which is exactly where the tool points when \(\theta_2+\theta_3=90^\circ\). Drag \(\theta_1\) and watch column 3 swing around the column while the tool keeps pointing up.

Wanting \(z_3\) along the tool is a perfectly reasonable goal — it is what Question 2's pick and place matrices need. But \(\alpha_3\) cannot deliver it: \(\mathrm{Rot}(x,\alpha_3)\) holds \(x_3\) fixed and only spins \(y_3,z_3\) about it, so \(z_3\) can be \(z_2\), or the in-plane perpendicular, but never \(x_3\) itself. That is the real reason a gripper frame is an appended fixed rotation after \({}^{2}T_3\) — an extra frame, not a joint, with no row in the D–H table.

Why \(\det R=+1\) matters and \(R^{\mathsf T}R=I\) alone is not enough

\(R^{\mathsf T}R=I\) says the columns are unit length and mutually perpendicular. That is also true of a reflection — a frame that has been flipped inside out, which no physical rotation can produce. Reflections have \(\det R=-1\). Since a sign error in one column is exactly how you accidentally write a reflection, the determinant is the check that catches it, and it costs one line.

The physical check is worth just as much: \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) must equal the radial reach and \(z\) the height you can read straight off the side view. Both are shown above and both are recomputed from the matrix product, so if they ever disagree with the picture, the algebra is wrong.
