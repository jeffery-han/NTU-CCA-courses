    Part 1 · Last year's paper (reproduced)

    The wording of these 8 items is taken from the tutor's deck. The model answers are mine, grounded in the slides cited (the tutor's answer slides point to the same pages).

    1 · Can 8 points be used to obtain an essential matrix?

    Hint

    Which algorithm, and what condition does it place on the 3D points?

    Answer & guidance

    True (yes), with a condition.

    Yes. The 8-point algorithm estimates E from at least 8 matched points in two images, as long as the points are not coplanar (no four of the 3D points coplanar) and the camera translation is non-zero. Each match gives one linear equation $m_j^T E\, m_j^* = 0$ in the 9 entries of E, which is known only up to scale, so 8 equations are enough.

    Answering “yes” without the non-coplanar condition. That condition is what the marks are for. If all the points lie on a plane, E cannot be determined and you need the homography instead. Lec 3 p. 13 p. 43 tutor slides

    2 · Can 4 coplanar points be used to obtain a Homography matrix?

    Hint

    The 4-point algorithm has one extra condition besides “coplanar”.

    Answer & guidance

    True (yes), with a condition.

    Yes. The 4-point algorithm computes the Euclidean homography H from at least 4 coplanar points matched in two images, provided they are non-collinear (no three on a line). Each match gives 2 equations, and H has 8 unknowns after fixing its scale ($h_{33}$), so 4 points are enough.

    Leaving out “non-collinear”. Also, H requires the points to be coplanar, which is exactly the case where E fails. Lec 3 p. 29 p. 32 p. 43

    3 · Can both the Essential matrix (E) and the Homography matrix (H) be used to obtain R and a scaled translation x?

    Hint

    What does each decomposition end with, and why is the translation only “scaled”?

    Answer & guidance

    True.

    Yes. Decomposing E gives R and the translation up to an unknown scale, $x' = \lambda x$. Decomposing H gives R and $x/d^*$ (the translation divided by the plane's depth). In both cases the magnitude of the translation is lost (“loss of scale”), because one image pair cannot tell a large, distant scene from a small, near one.

    Extra detail for full marks: E gives four candidate solutions and keeps the one with positive depth; for H, two solutions usually pass the depth test and an extra view or prior knowledge is needed to choose. Lec 3 p. 19 p. 38 p. 15

    4 · Precision describes the difference between the measurement and the true value, while accuracy relates to the reproducibility of sensor results.

    Hint

    Read it slowly. Are the two definitions attached to the right words?

    Answer & guidance

    False: the definitions are swapped.

    Accuracy is how close the measurement is to the true value: accuracy $= 1 - |m-v|/v$. Precision (repeatability) is about reproducing the same result: precision $=$ range / standard deviation. Example: a sensor that gives the same wrong reading every time is precise but not accurate.

    This is a swap trap. Expect it reversed this year too. Lec 1 p. 25 (the tutor cites “p. 24”, but it is p. 25 in our copy)

    5 · Is Kalman filtering statistically optimal for linear systems?

    Hint

    Linear is one condition. What is the other one?

    Answer & guidance

    True, under the noise condition.

    Yes. If the system is linear and both the process and measurement noise are white, (unimodal) Gaussian, the Kalman filter gives the unique statistically optimal estimate (minimum error variance). If the system is nonlinear or the noise is non-Gaussian, it is no longer guaranteed to be optimal (hence the EKF).

    Saying just “yes, because it is linear”. You must state the Gaussian-noise assumption. Lec 4 p. 17 p. 45

    6 · If two random variables X and Y are (uncorrelated) independent, the variance of their sum equals the sum of their individual variances.

    Hint

    Expand $V(X+Y)$. Which extra term appears, and when is it zero?

    Answer & guidance

    True.

    $V(X+Y) = V(X) + V(Y) + 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$. Independence implies $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0$, so $V(X+Y)=V(X)+V(Y)$. Being uncorrelated is already enough; independence is the stronger condition.

    Be ready to write the full proof (Part 3). Lec 4 p. 15

    7 · Structure from Motion (SFM) is the study of the rotation and translation that separates these two camera views and the relative structure of the observed feature points.

    Hint

    What two things can be determined from two images of matched points?

    Answer & guidance

    True.

    Given two images of matched feature points, SFM determines (1) the rotation and translation between the two camera views and (2) the relative structure (depths up to a common scale) of the feature points. It assumes the features are already detected and matched.

    “Relative” matters. The structure and translation are recovered only up to scale. If a variant says “absolute depth”, it is false. Lec 3 p. 4

    8 (MCQ, 30 marks) · Which of the following are advantages of multi-sensor integration and fusion? Justify your answer in detail.  (A) Redundancy  (B) Accuracy  (C) Reliability

    Hint

    Lec 1 names four advantages, starting with redundancy. Which two benefits does redundancy itself lead to?

    Answer & guidance

    All three: A, B and C.

    Redundancy: several sensors (or one sensor over time) perceive the same feature, possibly with different fidelity. Accuracy: fusing this redundant information reduces overall uncertainty, so the feature is perceived more accurately. For example, optimal fusion of two sensors gives $1/p = 1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_2^2$, a smaller variance than either sensor alone. Reliability: if one sensor fails or errs, the others still provide the information.

    For 30 marks, also name the other advantages from the same slides: complementarity (features impossible to perceive with any single sensor), timeliness (faster, through processing parallelism) and lower cost (several cheap, low-quality sensors can match one expensive sensor). A distractor such as “less computation” would be false, because fusion costs extra processing. Lec 1 pp. 13–16 tutor slide 10

    Part 2 · The same bank, reversed and reworded

    If the bank is reused, this year's items will likely be these statements reversed or reworded. Items marked “(class)” are the lecturer's own in-class questions from Weeks 8–9.

    V1 · The 8-point algorithm still works if all the feature points lie on one plane.

    Answer

    False. E needs points that are not all coplanar (no four coplanar). For a planar scene, use the homography with the 4-point algorithm. Lec 3 p. 43

    V2 · The essential-matrix method works well even when the camera only rotates (zero translation).

    Answer

    False. $E = [x]_\times R$, so as $x \to 0$, E tends to 0 and the method degenerates. E requires non-zero translation. The homography has no such requirement. Lec 3 p. 43

    V3 · Decomposing E gives the translation between the views with its true magnitude.

    Answer

    False. Only a scaled translation $x' = \lambda x$ is recovered. The essential constraint $m^T E m^* = 0$ also holds for any multiple of E, so the scale is lost. Lec 3 p. 15 p. 19

    V4 · Decomposing the essential matrix gives a single (R, x) directly, with no need to check anything.

    Answer

    False. Because of the ± sign of E and the two possible rotations, there are four candidate solutions. Three would put some points behind a camera, so the positive-depth constraint picks the physically valid one. Lec 3 p. 19 pp. 22–24

    V5 · After the positive-depth test, the homography method may still leave two valid solutions, so an extra view or prior knowledge is needed.

    Answer

    True. In the general case two homography solutions pass the depth constraint. An extra view, or knowledge such as the plane normal, selects one. This is why the lecturer says H “requires an extra view or knowledge”. Lec 3 p. 43

    V6 · The Kalman filter gives statistically optimal estimates for any nonlinear system with any noise distribution.

    Answer

    False. It is optimal only if the system is linear and the noise is white Gaussian. Otherwise it is a sub-optimal approximation. Lec 4 p. 45

    V7 · The Kalman filter must store all past measurements to compute the current estimate.

    Answer

    False. It is recursive: the new estimate uses only the previous estimate, its variance and the new measurement. This is why it suits systems without large data storage. Lec 4 p. 17

    V8 · For any two random variables, $V(X+Y) = V(X) + V(Y)$.

    Answer

    False. In general $V(X+Y) = V(X) + V(Y) + 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$. The extra term vanishes only when X and Y are uncorrelated (for example, independent). Lec 4 p. 15

    V9 · Uncorrelated random variables are always independent.

    Answer

    False. Independent implies uncorrelated, but not the other way round. Zero covariance only rules out a linear relationship. (This is why the lecture's proof needs only “uncorrelated”.) Lec 4 pp. 14–15

    V10 · Optimally fusing two independent noisy measurements of the same quantity gives an estimate whose variance is smaller than either sensor's variance.

    Answer

    True. $1/p = 1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_2^2$, so $p = \sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2/(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2) < \min(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2)$. Redundant information reduces uncertainty. Lec 4 pp. 40–41

    V11 (class) · A sensor that gives the same wrong reading every time is precise but not accurate.

    Answer

    True. The readings repeat (small spread, so precise) but are offset from the true value (systematic error, so not accurate). Lec 1 p. 25

    V12 (class) · A robot can act intelligently in an unknown environment using only internal (proprioceptive) sensors.

    Answer

    False. Internal sensors measure only the robot's own state. Reacting to the environment needs external sensors. The purpose of external sensors is to let the machine “act intelligently”. Lec 1 pp. 12–13 pp. 17–18

    V13 (class) · Camera, LiDAR and ultrasonic sensors are all active sensors.

    Answer

    False. LiDAR and ultrasonic sensors are active (they emit a pulse and time the return). A normal camera is passive (it only receives light already in the scene). Lec 1 p. 19

    V14 (class) · Position found by integrating IMU accelerations drifts without bound over time.

    Answer

    True. Acceleration is integrated twice, so a constant bias $b$ grows into a position error of $\tfrac12 b t^2$. An absolute reference such as GPS is needed to correct it, which is a direct case for sensor fusion. Lec 1 p. 31

    V15 (class) · GPS is purely an external-state sensor.

    Answer

    False, by this course's convention. The lecturer said in Week 8: “we include GPS in both internal and external state sensors.” Lec 1 p. 17 lists it among the internal-state sensors too. Lec 1 p. 17

    V16 (class) · From a single image with a known calibration matrix, the 3D (Euclidean) position of a point can be recovered.

    Answer

    False. $m = A^{-1}p$ gives only the normalised coordinates $(x/z,\,y/z)$. Every point on the same ray has the same image point, so depth is ambiguous without additional information. Lec 3 p. 10

    V17 (class) · A straight edge that appears curved in an image is caused by defocus.

    Answer

    False. Lens distortion changes the geometry (straight lines bend). Defocus only blurs the image and does not bend lines. Lec 2 p. 20

    V18 · Multi-sensor fusion always reduces the computation needed compared with using one sensor.

    Answer

    False. Fusion adds processing. Its advantages are redundancy (giving accuracy and reliability), complementarity, timeliness through parallel processing, and lower total cost, not less computation. The tutor's Kalman comparison notes that batch processing gives a smaller error variance but needs more computation. Lec 1 pp. 13–16

    Part 3 · Derivations to write on paper

    D1 · Prove that $V(X+Y) = V(X) + V(Y)$ when X and Y are uncorrelated.

    Hint

    Start from $V(Z) = E[Z^2] - (E[Z])^2$ with $Z = X+Y$, and use linearity of $E$.

    Answer & guidance
    1. $V(X+Y) = E[(X+Y)^2] - (E[X+Y])^2$ (definition of variance).
    2. $= E[X^2 + 2XY + Y^2] - (E[X] + E[Y])^2$ (linearity of expectation).
    3. $= E[X^2] + E[Y^2] + 2E[XY] - (E[X])^2 - (E[Y])^2 - 2E[X]E[Y]$.
    4. Regroup: $= \big(E[X^2] - (E[X])^2\big) + \big(E[Y^2] - (E[Y])^2\big) + 2\big(E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]\big)$.
    5. $= V(X) + V(Y) + 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$, and $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = 0$ when X and Y are uncorrelated. ∎

    Forgetting the cross term $-2E[X]E[Y]$ when you square $(E[X] + E[Y])$. Lec 4 p. 15

    D2 · Two independent sensors measure the same constant. Their noise variances are $\sigma_1^2 = 2$ and $\sigma_2^2 = 6$. What is the variance of the optimally fused estimate? (No calculator.)

    Hint

    For fusion, the reciprocals of the variances (the information) add.

    Answer & guidance

    $\dfrac1p = \dfrac12 + \dfrac16 = \dfrac46 \Rightarrow p = \dfrac32 = 1.5$, which is smaller than both 2 and 6. The fused estimate is $\hat x = \dfrac{\sigma_2^2 z_1 + \sigma_1^2 z_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2} = \dfrac{6z_1 + 2z_2}{8}$, which weights the better sensor more.

    Derivation in one line: take $\hat x = (1-L)z_1 + L z_2$, so $p = (1-L)^2\sigma_1^2 + L^2\sigma_2^2$ (the errors are uncorrelated, so D1 applies). Setting $dp/dL = 0$ gives $L = \sigma_1^2/(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$. Lec 4 pp. 40–42

    D3 · In a table or a few lines, compare the essential-matrix and homography methods.

    Answer & guidance

    Points: E needs ≥ 8; H needs ≥ 4. Geometry: E needs points not all coplanar (no four coplanar); H needs coplanar points with no three collinear. Translation: E needs $x \ne 0$ (degenerates as $x \to 0$); H has no such requirement. Solutions: E gives 4 candidates and positive depth leaves 1; H usually leaves 2 after the depth test, so an extra view or prior knowledge is needed. Both: recover R and translation only up to scale. In the lecturer's experience, H is more accurate.

    • E / 8-point: ≥ 8 matched points, not coplanar (no four coplanar), non-zero translation, 4 solutions → positive depth → 1.
    • H / 4-point: ≥ 4 coplanar, non-collinear points; H fixed by $h_{33}$; 2 solutions survive → extra view or knowledge needed; more accurate.
    • Both give R and a scaled translation (E: $\lambda x$; H: $x/d^*$). Scale is lost, and depth is only relative.
    • SFM: from two views of matched points → R, t between the views and the relative structure.
    • Accuracy = closeness to the true value, $1 - |m-v|/v$. Precision = repeatability, range/σ. Expect them swapped in a trap.

    • Variance: $V(X+Y) = V(X) + V(Y) + 2\mathrm{Cov}$. Uncorrelated (implied by independent) makes the covariance term 0.
    • Fusion: $1/p = \sum 1/\sigma_i^2$, so the result is better than the best single sensor.
    • Multi-sensor advantages: redundancy (→ accuracy and reliability), complementarity, timeliness, lower cost.
