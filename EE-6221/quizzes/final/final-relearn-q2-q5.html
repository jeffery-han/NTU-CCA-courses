1 · Computed torque in three moves Exam focus

The one picture. The robot's equation is "push = mass × acceleration + everything else": \(M(q)\ddot q+\text{rest}=u\). Computed-torque ("partitioned") control splits the controller into two cascade blocks so that a messy nonlinear robot behaves like a simple unit mass (\(\ddot q=v\)) driven by a spring-damper tracker. Lec 2 pp. 6–13 (partitioned control, gains, resonance rule, disturbances) · Lec 2 pp. 20–31 (\(M(q)\), \(n\)-joint PD-computed torque) · Week 6 review sheet

What each of the three exam lines actually is (in Q2(a), always write and label all three):

Name on the slidesEquation to writeWhat it means & what goes inside
1. Control law
(model-based portion, Lec 2 pp. 8, 29)		\[u=\alpha v+\beta\]The torque/force sent to the motors. Set \(\alpha=M(q)\) (the mass or \(n\times n\) inertia matrix multiplying \(\ddot q\)) and \(\beta=\text{rest}\) (Coriolis/centrifugal, known friction, gravity, and measured contact force \(f\)—never an unknown disturbance \(d\)). Plugging \(u=\alpha v+\beta\) into \(M\ddot q+\text{rest}=u\) cancels \(\text{rest}\) and leaves \(M\ddot q=Mv\), i.e. the unit-mass plant \(\ddot q=v\).
2. Servo law
(servo portion, Lec 2 pp. 9, 30)		\[v=\ddot q_d+k_v\dot e+k_p e\](for \(n\) joints: \(v=\ddot q_d+K_v\dot e+K_p e\), with \(e=q_d-q\), \(\dot e=\dot q_d-\dot q\))How the computer calculates the commanded acceleration \(v\). \(\ddot q_d\) is the feedforward acceleration along the desired path; \(k_pe\) is a virtual spring pulling \(q\) toward \(q_d\); \(k_v\dot e\) is a virtual damper stopping overshoot. Once you compute the number \(v\), you plug it into \(u=\alpha v+\beta\).
3. Error equation
(closed-loop dynamics, Lec 2 pp. 9, 30)		\[\ddot e+k_v\dot e+k_p e=0\](with unknown disturbance \(d\): \(\alpha(\ddot e+K_v\dot e+K_p e)=d\))The proof that tracking error dies out (not a formula inside the controller). Matching this with \(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2=0\) gives the gain formulas \(k_p=\omega_n^2\) and \(k_v=2\zeta\omega_n\).

Pitfall — why writing "\(v=\ddot q\)" loses the servo mark (mistake #8): \(\ddot q=v\) is the plant result after \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) cancel the robot dynamics ("whatever acceleration \(v\) we command, the joint achieves it"). It does not define how \(v\) is computed from the error \(e=q_d-q\), and it gives you no number for \(v\) when the question asks you to evaluate \(u_1\)! Always write \(v=\ddot q_d+k_v\dot e+k_pe\).

Gains: the resonance rule. The loop must be slower than half the lowest structural resonance: \(\omega_n\le\tfrac12\omega_{res,\min}\) (Lec 2 pp. 10–11). \[\omega_n=\tfrac12\cdot(2\pi\cdot f_{\min}).\]

2 · Hybrid position/force control Exam focus

The one picture. A joint presses on a wall that behaves like a stiff spring: \(f=k_e(q-q_e)\), where \(q_e\) is where the wall starts. You can choose where the joint is or how hard it pushes, but not both, because the wall links them. So the paper splits the joints. Joints that move freely are position-controlled, exactly as in Part 1. The joint that touches the wall is force-controlled. Lec 2 pp. 38–54 (force control, hybrid position/force control)

Rebuild the force servo in three lines. You want the force error \(e_f=f_d-f\) to obey the same tidy equation as a position error: \(\ddot e_f+k_{vf}\dot e_f+k_{pf}e_f=0\). That needs \(\ddot f=\ddot f_d+k_{vf}\dot e_f+k_{pf}e_f\). The wall turns acceleration into force: \(\ddot f=k_e\ddot q\). And the inner loop makes \(\ddot q=v\). So

\[v_f=\frac{1}{k_e}\big(\ddot f_d+k_{vf}\dot e_f+k_{pf}e_f\big).\]

The \(1/k_e\) is there only because \(v\) must be an acceleration, while the bracket is a rate of change of force. If you forget it, the units are wrong. The rest of the law is unchanged: \(u=\alpha v+\beta\). The measured contact force \(f\) is a known force, so it goes in \(\beta\) (Lec 2 p. 40).

3 · An unknown disturbance on the force joint Exam focus

The one picture. An unknown constant push \(d\) is like a headwind. \(\beta\) cannot cancel it, because you cannot put a number you do not know into the controller. So it survives into the error equation. You have seen the position version: \(m(\ddot e+k_v\dot e+k_pe)=d\), giving \(e_{ss}=d/(mk_p)\) (Lec 2 pp. 11–13). The force version differs by one factor, \(k_e\).

Derive it. The plant has an extra \(d\): \(m\ddot q=mv-d\), so \(\ddot q=v-d/m\). Multiply by \(k_e\): \(\ddot f=k_ev-k_ed/m=\ddot f_d+k_{vf}\dot e_f+k_{pf}e_f-k_ed/m\). Rearranging gives

\[\ddot e_f+k_{vf}\dot e_f+k_{pf}e_f=\frac{k_e\,d}{m}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad e_{f,ss}=\frac{k_e\,d}{m\,k_{pf}}.\]

At steady state nothing changes, so the derivatives are 0 and only \(k_{pf}e_f\) is left. The fix is the same as for position: add \(k_{if}\int e_f\,dt\) to the bracket.

Worked example (Mock B Q2(c)). \(k_e=40\), \(m_3=4\), \(d_3=2\), \(k_{pf}=39.478\). The right-hand side is \(40\cdot2/4=20\), so \(e_{f,ss}=20/39.478=0.5066\) N. The force settles 0.51 N short of \(f_d\).

5 · The two constraint rows, rebuilt in 30 seconds Exam focus

The one picture. A standard wheel obeys two laws. Along its rolling direction, the hub's speed equals the rim speed \(r\dot\varphi\). Along its axle, the hub's speed is zero, because the wheel cannot skid sideways. Each law is "hub velocity · direction". So you only need two things: the hub velocity and the two directions.

  1. Hub velocity (rigid body, \(v_A=v_P+\dot\theta\times r_A\)): \(\big(\dot x_r-\dot\theta\,l\sin\alpha,\ \ \dot y_r+\dot\theta\,l\cos\alpha\big)\).
  2. Axle direction is the "natural" one, at angle \(\alpha+\beta\): \((\cos(\alpha+\beta),\ \sin(\alpha+\beta))\).
  3. Rolling direction is the axle turned by −90°: \((\sin(\alpha+\beta),\ -\cos(\alpha+\beta))\).

Take the dot products. The \(\dot\theta\) coefficient collapses with \(\sin(A-B)\) and \(\cos(A-B)\): for the axle, \(l(-\sin\alpha\cos(\alpha+\beta)+\cos\alpha\sin(\alpha+\beta))=l\sin\beta\). For rolling, \(l(-\sin\alpha\sin(\alpha+\beta)-\cos\alpha\cos(\alpha+\beta))=-l\cos\beta\). That gives the two slide rows (Lec 3 pp. 26–31):

\[\underbrace{[\sin(\alpha+\beta)\ \ -\cos(\alpha+\beta)\ \ -l\cos\beta]}_{\text{rolling}}\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I-r\dot\varphi=0,\qquad\underbrace{[\cos(\alpha+\beta)\ \ \sin(\alpha+\beta)\ \ l\sin\beta]}_{\text{sliding}}\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I=0.\]

Memory hook: sliding = cos, sin, + l sin. Rolling = that turned −90°: sin, −cos, − l cos. In Mock B you had sin and cos swapped between the rows and the wrong sign on \(l\cos\beta\) (mistake #13). You also left out \(R(\theta)\). The rows act on the robot-frame velocity \(\dot\xi_R=R(\theta)\dot\xi_I\), so write \(R(\theta)\dot\xi_I\), or say explicitly that you use robot-frame velocities.

Sanity check you can always do. For a fixed wheel at \((x_A,y_A)\) rolling along \(+X_R\), the hub velocity is \((\dot x_r-\dot\theta y_A,\ \dot y_r+\dot\theta x_A)\). So the rows must come out as rolling \([1,\ 0,\ -y_A]\) and sliding \([0,\ 1,\ x_A]\). If the general formula does not reproduce that, you have a sign wrong.

9 · Multi-sensor Kalman: information adds Exam focus

The one picture. You hold a guess with uncertainty \(p\). Each sensor offers another guess with uncertainty \(\sigma_i^2\). Trust each source in proportion to 1/variance, its "information". When you combine optimally, the information just adds. HGQ Lec 4 pp. 25–31 (scalar Kalman derivation) · HGQ Lec 4 pp. 33–44 (multiple sensors, sensor integration)

\[\boxed{\frac1{p_{k+1}}=\frac1{p_k}+\sum_i\frac1{\sigma_i^2}},\qquad\boxed{L_i=\frac{p_{k+1}}{\sigma_i^2}}.\]

The gain rule reads as "the new uncertainty divided by this sensor's own uncertainty". A good sensor (small \(\sigma_i^2\)) gets a big gain.

The four exam moves that produce it, which you must show:

  1. Error recursion. \(y_i-\hat x=\tilde x+v_i\), so \(\tilde x_{k+1}=(1-\sum L_i)\tilde x_k-\sum L_iv_i\).
  2. Square and take expectations. The cross terms vanish because the noises are independent and zero-mean: \(p_{k+1}=(1-\sum L_i)^2p_k+\sum L_i^2\sigma_i^2\).
  3. Minimise. \(\partial/\partial L_i=0\) gives \(L_i\sigma_i^2=(1-\sum L_j)p_k\), the same for every \(i\).
  4. Solve. Sum over \(i\) to get \(1-\sum L_j=1/(1+p_k\sum1/\sigma_j^2)\). Then \(p_{k+1}=(1-\sum L_j)p_k\), which is the boxed form.

One shared gain (\(L_i=L\)) cannot favour the good sensor. Then \(p_{k+1}=(1-nL)^2p_k+L^2\sum\sigma_i^2\), giving \(L=\dfrac{np_k}{n^2p_k+\sum\sigma_i^2}\) and \(\dfrac1{p_{k+1}}=\dfrac1{p_k}+\dfrac{n^2}{\sum\sigma_i^2}\). This is never better than separate gains, and equal only when all \(\sigma_i\) are equal.
