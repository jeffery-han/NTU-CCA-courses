1 · The four numbers and where each one lives Exam focus

Picture first. A D-H row describes one link: how to get from frame \(k-1\) to frame \(k\). That trip is always the same four moves, in this order:

  1. Spin about \(z_{k-1}\) by \(\theta_k\), until the old \(x\) points along the new \(x_k\).
  2. Slide along \(z_{k-1}\) by \(d_k\), up to the level of the new \(x_k\).
  3. Walk along \(x_k\) by \(a_k\), across to the new \(z_k\).
  4. Tilt about \(x_k\) by \(\alpha_k\), until the old \(z\) lines up with \(z_k\).

That is exactly \(T^k_{k-1}=R(\theta_k)\,T(d_k)\,T(a_k)\,R(\alpha_k)\), the formula you wrote at the bottom of your sheet. It is in the right order. Lec 1 pp. 53–65 (link coordinates, D-H algorithm, Example 2.10) · Lec 1 pp. 66–71 (the link transform \(T^k_{k-1}\))

Why it matters for you. Every dimension printed on the figure is either a slide along the old \(z\) (a \(d\)) or a walk along the new \(x\) (an \(a\)). There is no third place for a length to go. So if a dimension is not in your table, the table describes a different robot. That is how your tip ended up at \((0.54,0,0.32)\) m instead of \((0.71,0,0.37)\) m.

Pitfall from your sheet. Your \(\theta\) column held only the angles of the sketched pose (90°, −90°, …). For a revolute joint, \(\theta_k\) is the joint variable (Lec 1 p. 56), so write \(\theta_k=q_k\), or \(q_k+\) a constant offset if you choose one. A table of plain numbers has no variables in it, and that costs the "variables identified" mark.

3 · The sign of α Exam focus

Picture. \(\alpha_k\) is the tilt that carries \(z_{k-1}\) onto \(z_k\), turning about \(x_k\) (Lec 1 p. 60). Point your right thumb along \(x_k\). Your fingers curl in the positive direction. If a quarter-turn in that direction takes \(z_{k-1}\) to \(z_k\), then \(\alpha_k=+90^\circ\). If it takes \(z_{k-1}\) to \(-z_k\), then \(\alpha_k=-90^\circ\).

The same test without hands. For a quarter-turn, \(\alpha_k=+90^\circ\) exactly when \(z_{k-1}\times z_k=+x_k\), and \(-90^\circ\) when the cross product is \(-x_k\). On a page sketch, use this right-handed triple:

right \(\times\) up \(=\) ⊙ (out of the page)  ·  up \(\times\) ⊙ \(=\) right  ·  ⊙ \(\times\) right \(=\) up

Swapping the two factors flips the sign, and ⊗ (into the page) is \(-\)⊙.

Worked: \(\alpha_1\) on your frames. \(z_0\) is up, \(z_1\) is right, and \(x_1\) is ⊗. Then \(z_0\times z_1=\) up \(\times\) right \(=-(\text{right}\times\text{up})=-\)⊙ \(=\) ⊗ \(=+x_1\), so \(\alpha_1=+90^\circ\). That matches your table.

Why 0 is impossible for \(\alpha_5\). \(\alpha\) is the angle between \(z_{k-1}\) and \(z_k\). If they are perpendicular, the angle is \(\pm90^\circ\), whatever you pick for \(x_k\). Choosing \(x_k\) only decides the sign. \(\alpha=0\) means the two joint axes are parallel, which is true only for rows 3 and 6 on this arm.

7 · What goes in β (and what never does) Exam focus

Picture. Picture pushing a shopping trolley that has a spring and some friction attached. A smart controller pushes with exactly the opposite of every force it knows about, so those forces cancel. Then it scales what is left so the trolley behaves like a 1 kg block. That is the computed-torque law \[u=\alpha v+\beta,\] where \(\beta\) cancels the known forces and \(\alpha\) rescales the mass, "so that the system appears to be a unit mass" (Lec 2 p. 8). Lec 2 pp. 6–13 (partitioned control, disturbance rejection) · Lec 2 pp. 20–31 (mass matrix, computed torque for \(n\) joints) · Lec 2 pp. 38–41 (force control, \(\beta=f\))

Derive it once. Take the slide's block, \(m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx^3=u\) (Lec 2 p. 7). Choose \(\alpha=m\) and \(\beta=b\dot x+kx^3\). Then \(m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx^3=mv+b\dot x+kx^3\), so \(\ddot x=v\). Every term that \(\beta\) cancelled had to be something the controller can compute right now from measured \(x\) and \(\dot x\).

Term in the dynamicsGoes inWhy
\(\ddot q\) coefficients (\(m\), \(M(q)\))\(\alpha\)they multiply the acceleration; \(\alpha\) turns them into a unit mass
velocity, Coriolis, gravity, spring terms\(\beta\)known functions of measured \(q,\dot q\)
contact force \(f\), when it is measured or given by \(f=k_e(x-x_e)\)\(\beta\)Lec 2 p. 40 chooses \(\beta=f\)
unknown disturbance \(d\)neitheryou cannot cancel what you cannot compute

What happens to \(d\). It stays in the plant, so it survives into the error equation (Lec 2 pp. 11–13 (disturbance rejection)): \[m(\ddot e+k_v\dot e+k_pe)=d\quad\Rightarrow\quad e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{s\cdot d/s}{m(s^2+k_vs+k_p)}=\frac{d}{mk_p}.\] The fix is an integral term, \(v=\ddot x_d+k_v\dot e+k_pe+k_i\int e\,dt\), which puts an \(s\) in the numerator of \(E(s)/D(s)\) and drives \(e_{ss}\) to 0 (Lec 2 p. 13).

Reading that equation line by line

Picture. The slide's block, \(m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx^3+d=u\) (Lec 2 p. 11), now has an extra steady push \(d\) that the controller does not know about. Because \(d\) sits on the same side as the friction and spring, it is a load the motor must overcome, like a constant headwind. The PD part of \(v\) acts like a spring pulling the block toward \(x_d\). The block settles where that spring's pull exactly balances the headwind, which is slightly short of the target. That leftover gap is \(e_{ss}\).

SymbolMeaning
\(e=x_d-x\)tracking error; \(e>0\) means the block is short of the target
\(k_p,\;k_v\)PD gains chosen for the unit-mass system, \(k_p=\omega_n^2\), \(k_v=2\zeta\omega_n\) (Lec 2 p. 11)
\(d\)constant unknown disturbance force (N)
\(E(s),\,D(s)\)Laplace transforms of \(e(t)\) and \(d(t)\)
\(e_{ss}\)steady-state error, \(\lim_{t\to\infty}e(t)\)

Step 1: why the left side has an \(m\). \(\beta\) cancelled \(b\dot x+kx^3\), but nothing cancelled \(d\). What remains is \(m\ddot x+d=mv\). Moving \(m\ddot x\) to the right and writing \(v\) out gives \(m(\ddot x_d-\ddot x+k_v\dot e+k_pe)=d\), which is \(m(\ddot e+k_v\dot e+k_pe)=d\). (This is Check 7.5 below.) The controller scaled \(v\) by \(\alpha=m\), so the error dynamics carry the \(m\) as well, while \(d\) arrives unscaled.

Step 2: Laplace transform. Take zero initial conditions, which is what a transfer function assumes. Each derivative becomes a factor of \(s\): \(\ddot e\to s^2E\), \(\dot e\to sE\), \(e\to E\). So \[m(s^2+k_vs+k_p)\,E(s)=D(s)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{E(s)}{D(s)}=\frac{1}{m(s^2+k_vs+k_p)}.\]

Step 3: the disturbance as a step. A constant force \(d\) that switches on at \(t=0\) is a step of height \(d\), and \(\mathcal L\{d\cdot1(t)\}=d/s\). So \(E(s)=\dfrac{d/s}{m(s^2+k_vs+k_p)}\).

Step 4: final value theorem. \(\lim_{t\to\infty}e(t)=\lim_{s\to0}sE(s)\). It is valid here because the closed loop is stable: with \(k_v,k_p>0\), both roots of \(s^2+k_vs+k_p\) lie in the left half-plane. Multiplying by \(s\) cancels the \(1/s\) from the step: \[e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{s\cdot d/s}{m(s^2+k_vs+k_p)}=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{d}{m(s^2+k_vs+k_p)}=\frac{d}{m(0+0+k_p)}=\frac{d}{mk_p}.\] At \(s=0\) the \(s^2\) and \(k_vs\) terms vanish, so only \(k_p\) survives. In plain terms, at steady state nothing is moving or accelerating any more, so damping and inertia have no effect. Only the spring-like \(k_p\) term holds the block against \(d\).

Shortcut without Laplace (our own check, not on the slide). At steady state \(e\) is constant, so \(\dot e=\ddot e=0\). The error equation collapses to \(mk_pe_{ss}=d\), which again gives \(e_{ss}=d/(mk_p)\). This is Hooke's law: a spring of stiffness \(mk_p\) (N/m) under a force \(d\) stretches by \(d/(mk_p)\).

What the formula tells you.

  • \(e_{ss}\propto d\): twice the disturbance gives twice the error.
  • \(e_{ss}\propto1/k_p\): a stiffer loop gives a smaller error, but the error never reaches 0 for finite \(k_p\), and a large \(k_p\) is limited by the structural resonance: \(k_p=\omega_n^2\) with \(\omega_n\le0.5\,\omega_{res}\) (Lec 2 pp. 10–11).
  • \(k_v\) does not appear: damping shapes the transient but not where the block settles.
  • The sign is positive: \(d\) resists \(u\), so the block stops short of \(x_d\).

Why the integral term reaches 0. With \(k_i\int e\,dt\), the same steps give \(E(s)/D(s)=s/\big(m(s^3+k_vs^2+k_ps+k_i)\big)\) (Lec 2 p. 13). Then \(e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}s\cdot\frac{d}{s}\cdot\frac{s}{m(\cdots)}=\frac{0}{mk_i}=0\). Intuitively, as long as any error remains, \(\int e\,dt\) keeps growing and the controller pushes harder, so it can only stop growing when \(e=0\).
