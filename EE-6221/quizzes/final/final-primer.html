Q2 · Robot control Exam focus

C1. Partitioned (computed-torque) control 5 of 5 papers

Idea. A robot's dynamics are nonlinear and coupled. Computed torque cancels the model so that each joint behaves like a unit mass, then a linear PD servo drives that unit mass. Write the dynamics as \(M(q)\,\ddot q + (\text{everything else}) = \tau\) and split the controller into a model-based part and a servo part:

\[\tau=\alpha v+\beta,\qquad \alpha=M(q)\ \ (\text{what multiplies }\ddot q),\qquad \beta=\text{all other terms}\ \Rightarrow\ \ddot q=v.\]

Then \(v=\ddot q_d+k_v\dot e+k_p e\) with \(e=q_d-q\) gives the error equation \(\ddot e+k_v\dot e+k_p e=0\). Lec 2 pp. 6–9 (partitioned control, one joint) · Lec 2 pp. 20–31 (mass matrix \(M(q)\), computed torque for \(n\) joints) · lecturer's worked examples (single link, 2-link polar)

Slide example. \(m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx^3=u\): \(\alpha=m\), \(\beta=b\dot x+kx^3\). Exercise 1(a). \(3\theta\ddot\theta+5\dot\theta^2-2\cos\theta=\tau\): \(\alpha=3\theta\), \(\beta=5\dot\theta^2-2\cos\theta\). Exercise 2 (3 joints). \(\alpha=\mathrm{diag}(m_2r^2,\ m_1+m_2,\ m_2)\), \(\beta=[\,2m_2r\dot r\dot\theta,\ (m_1+m_2)gh,\ -m_2r\dot\theta^2\,]^T\). exercise sheet · solutions
Pitfall. In a coupled model such as \(2\ddot q_1+\ddot q_2+\dots=u_1\), the \(\ddot q_2\) term is inertia: it goes into \(\alpha\) as an off-diagonal entry, never into \(\beta\). Gravity constants (e.g. \(+100\)) and the contact force \(f\) go into \(\beta\).

C2. Choosing \(k_v\) and \(k_p\) 5 of 5 papers

Match \(s^2+k_vs+k_p=0\) to the standard form \(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2=0\):

\[k_v=2\zeta\omega_n,\qquad k_p=\omega_n^2,\qquad \zeta\ge 1,\qquad \omega_n\le 0.5\,\omega_{res}.\]

\(\zeta\ge1\) means no overshoot (an overshooting arm hits the workpiece). \(\omega_{res}\) is the lowest unmodelled structural resonance, so the closed loop never excites any of them. The lecturer in Week 6: “Remember these two specifications.” When the paper says “gains as high as possible” or gives no other rule, take \(\omega_n=0.5\,\omega_{res,\min}\). Lec 2 pp. 10–11 (performance specifications) · Lec 2 pp. 32–37 (2-link polar example) · Week 6 review sheet (setting the gains)

Lecture example. Resonance 12 rad/s, critically damped: \(\omega_n=6\), \(k_v=12\), \(k_p=36\). Exercise 2. 14.5 Hz is \(2\pi\cdot14.5=91.1\) rad/s, so \(\omega_n\le45.55\); the solution takes \(\omega_n=45\), giving \(k_v=90\), \(k_p=2025\).
Pitfalls. (1) A resonance in Hz must be multiplied by \(2\pi\) first. (2) Use the lowest resonance, not the one “for this joint”. (3) “Overdamped with \(\zeta=1.25\)” changes only \(k_v\); \(k_p=\omega_n^2\) is the same.

C3. Constant disturbance and integral action 4 of 5 papers

If an unmodelled force \(d\) acts, \(m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx^3+d=u\), the same controller leaves \(m(\ddot e+k_v\dot e+k_pe)=d\). By the final-value theorem with \(D(s)=d/s\):

\[e_{ss}=\lim_{s\to0}\frac{s\,d/s}{m(s^2+k_vs+k_p)}=\frac{d}{m\,k_p}\neq0.\]

Fix: add an integral term, \(v=\ddot x_d+k_v\dot e+k_pe+k_i\!\int e\,dt\). Now \(E(s)/D(s)=s/\big(m(s^3+k_vs^2+k_ps+k_i)\big)\), and the extra \(s\) in the numerator drives \(e_{ss}\) to 0. For several joints, \(\ddot E+K_v\dot E+K_pE=M^{-1}d\), so every joint the disturbance reaches has a steady error. C4. Force control 5 of 5 papers (inside hybrid)

Model the surface as a spring: \(f=k_e(x-x_e)\), with \(k_e\) the stiffness and \(x_e\) where the surface is. The dynamics \(m\ddot x+f=\alpha v+\beta\) with \(\alpha=m\), \(\beta=f\) give \(\ddot x=v\). Since \(\ddot f=k_e\ddot x\), the plant is \(\tfrac1{k_e}\ddot f=v\), so servo the force:

\[v=\frac1{k_e}\big(\ddot f_d+k_{vf}\dot e_f+k_{pf}e_f\big),\quad e_f=f_d-f\ \Rightarrow\ \ddot e_f+k_{vf}\dot e_f+k_{pf}e_f=0.\]

The force loop obeys the same gain rules (its own \(\zeta\), and \(\omega_n\le0.5\,\omega_{res}\)). With no force sensor, \(f\) and \(\dot f=k_e\dot x\) are computed from the measured position and velocity, which is only as good as the known \(k_e\) and \(x_e\). Lec 2 pp. 38–41 (force control)

C5. Hybrid position/force control 5 of 5 papers

In contact, the environment fixes one direction (the force subspace, normal to the surface) and leaves the others free (the motion subspace). Read the contact model: in \(f=25(q_3-0.2)\) only \(q_3\) changes the force, so \(q_3\) is force-controlled and the other coordinates are position-controlled.

  1. One model-based part for the whole robot: \(\alpha=M(q)\), \(\beta=\) everything else including \(f\), so \(\ddot q=v\).
  2. Motion coordinates: \(v_i=\ddot q_{id}+k_v\dot e_i+k_pe_i\), with the motion \(\zeta\).
  3. Force coordinate: \(v_3=\frac1{k_e}(\ddot f_d+k_{vf}\dot e_f+k_{pf}e_f)\), with the force \(\zeta\).
  4. Both loops: \(\omega_n\le0.5\,\omega_{res,\min}\). Write \(\tau=\alpha v+\beta\) out in full and state the error equations.

With constant disturbances on the motion joints, the error equations are \(\ddot E+K_v\dot E+K_pE=M^{-1}d\) (motion) and \(\ddot e_f+k_{vf}\dot e_f+k_{pf}e_f=0\) (force, if no disturbance reaches it): state the steady error and add integral action. Lec 2 pp. 42–54 (hybrid position/force control)

C6. When the model is wrong 23-24 S2, 25-26 S2

Two papers ask what happens when the controller's contact model is off. The slides do not cover this case; the result below is derived here from the slide equations (C4), so present it as your own derivation. Basis: Lec 2 pp. 38–41 (force control)

Stiffness misjudged (controller uses \(\hat k\), truth is \(k\)). The real plant gives \(\ddot f=k\,v=\rho(\ddot f_d+k_{vf}\dot e_f+k_{pf}e_f)\) with \(\rho=k/\hat k\), so

\[\ddot e_f+\rho k_{vf}\dot e_f+\rho k_{pf}e_f=(1-\rho)\ddot f_d.\]

For a constant \(f_d\) the error still goes to 0, but \(\omega_n'=\omega_n\sqrt\rho\) and \(\zeta'=\zeta\sqrt\rho\): the response changes, and \(\omega_n'\) can break the resonance limit.

No force sensor and \(x_e\) drifts (assumed \(x_e\), true \(x_e'\)). The computed force \(\hat f=k(x-x_e)\) is driven to \(f_d\), so the true force settles at \(f_d+k(x_e-x_e')\), a steady bias that integral action on \(\hat f\) cannot see. The fix is to measure the force (sensor feedback) or re-identify \(x_e\).

Q3(a) · Mobile-platform constraints Exam focus

M1. One fixed standard wheel 5 of 5 papers

Locate the wheel from the robot point \(P\) in polar form: distance \(l\) and angle \(\alpha\) from \(X_R\). \(\beta\) is the angle of the wheel plane relative to the chassis, so the wheel rolls along the direction \(\alpha+\beta\) rotated by \(-90^\circ\). Resolve the chassis velocity \((\dot x_r,\dot y_r,\dot\theta)\) along the rolling direction and along the axle:

\[\text{rolling: }[\sin(\alpha{+}\beta)\ \ {-\cos(\alpha{+}\beta)}\ \ {-l\cos\beta}]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I-r\dot\varphi=0\] \[\text{sliding: }[\cos(\alpha{+}\beta)\ \ \sin(\alpha{+}\beta)\ \ l\sin\beta]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I=0\]

The \(\dot\theta l\) term is the velocity the wheel gets from the chassis spinning about \(P\). Lec 3 pp. 20–25 (wheel constraints, fixed standard wheel, Example 2) · Week 7 review sheet

Example 2 (wheel 1 above \(P\), rolling along \(+X_R\)): \(\alpha=\pi/2\), \(\beta=0\) give rolling \([1\ \ 0\ \ {-l}]\) and sliding \([0\ \ 1\ \ 0]\). Example 4, wheel B below \(P\): \(\alpha=-\pi/2\), and \(\beta=\pi\) so that positive spin still drives \(+X_R\).
Pitfall. Read \(\beta\) so that positive \(\dot\varphi\) moves the robot forward. On the mirror-image wheel that means \(\beta=\pi\), which flips the sign of \(l\cos\beta\).

M2. Steered standard and castor wheels 5 of 5 papers

Steered standard wheel: the same two rows, but \(\beta=\beta(t)\) is a variable (the steering angle). Write the rows with \(\beta_s\), not with a number. Castor wheel: the steering axis is offset by \(d\) from the contact point. Rolling is unchanged, but sliding gains a term:

\[[\cos(\alpha{+}\beta)\ \ \sin(\alpha{+}\beta)\ \ l\sin\beta]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I+d\dot\beta=0.\]

For any chassis motion there is a \(\dot\varphi\) and a \(\dot\beta\) that satisfy both rows, so a castor imposes no real constraint on the chassis (the office chair). Lec 3 pp. 26–31 (steered standard and castor wheels) · Lec 3 p. 35 (Example 3, steered wheel)

Pitfall. For a castor, \(\beta\) is a variable like the steered wheel's, and the \(d\dot\beta\) term must appear in its sliding row. Writing the castor exactly like a steered wheel loses that mark.

M3. Assemble the platform 5 of 5 papers

Stack one rolling row per wheel and one sliding row per standard wheel (Example 4):

\[J_1(\beta_s)\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I-J_2\dot\varphi=0,\qquad C_1(\beta_s)\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I=0,\qquad J_2=\mathrm{diag}(r_1,\dots,r_N).\]

\(R(\theta)\dot\xi_I=[\dot x_r,\dot y_r,\dot\theta]^T\) is the velocity in the robot frame. Wheel speeds then follow row by row: \(\dot\varphi_i=j_i\,[\dot x_r,\dot y_r,\dot\theta]^T/r_i\). Q3(b) · Arm on the platform Refresh

I1. Tool-configuration vector, Jacobian, analytic IK 5 of 5 papers

The tool-configuration vector is \(w=[\,p;\ e^{q_n/\pi}\,r^3\,]\), with \(p\) the 4th column of \(T\) and \(r^3\) its 3rd column (the approach vector). The tool-configuration Jacobian is \(\partial w/\partial q\), one column per joint. For IK, solve the position equations in an order that isolates one joint at a time: usually \(q_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)\) from the first two rows, then the \(z\) row, then the remaining length. Q4 · Homography Refresh

H1. Which four-point groups work 5 of 5 papers

Each matched point gives 2 equations for the 8 unknowns of \(H_n=H/h_{33}\), so 4 coplanar points seen in both images are needed, and no three of them collinear (otherwise the \(8\times8\) system is singular). Count by \(\binom n4\) minus every group containing a collinear triple. If two poses share too few usable points, chain through the third pose: \(K\propto HG\), then divide by the (3,3) entry. Q5 · Multi-sensor Kalman Refresh

K1. One gain per sensor vs one shared gain 5 of 5 papers

Four moves every year: write \(\tilde x_{k+1}=(1-\sum L_i)\tilde x_k-\sum L_iv_{ik}\); square and take expectations, \(p_{k+1}=(1-\sum L_i)^2p_k+\sum L_i^2\sigma_i^2\); set each \(\partial p_{k+1}/\partial L_i=0\); substitute back:

\[L_i=\frac{p_k}{\sigma_i^2\,(1+p_k\sum_j1/\sigma_j^2)},\qquad \frac1{p_{k+1}}=\frac1{p_k}+\sum_i\frac1{\sigma_i^2}.\]

One shared gain for \(n\) sensors gives \(\frac1{p_{k+1}}=\frac1{p_k}+\frac{n^2}{\sum\sigma_i^2}\), which is never larger, so it is never better (equal only when all \(\sigma_i\) are equal), but it needs only one gain. To compare two sensor groups, compare \(\sum1/\sigma_i^2\): the group with the larger sum gives the smaller error. Week 11: two sensors is the key case, three work the same way.
