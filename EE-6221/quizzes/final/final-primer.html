Q1 · D-H Refresh

D1. Link-coordinate diagram and parameter table 5 of 5 papers

1. The big picture

A rigid-body robot arm is a chain of links connected by joints (revolute = rotating hinge, prismatic = sliding rail). To find where the tool tip sits in space, we attach one right-handed coordinate frame \(\{x_k, y_k, z_k\}\) to every link \(k\), then chain the \(4\times 4\) transforms \(T^1_0, T^2_1, \dots, T^n_{n-1}\) from base to tool. The Denavit–Hartenberg (D-H) convention fixes the rules for placing those frames so that each link-to-link transform needs only 4 numbers instead of 6.

How to read the diagram: \(z_{k-1}\) is joint \(k\)'s axis and \(z_k\) is joint \(k+1\)'s axis. The unique line perpendicular to both is the common normal \(x_k\). Moving from frame \(k-1\) to frame \(k\) requires two operations along/about \(z_{k-1}\) (\(\theta_k, d_k\)) followed by two operations along/about \(x_k\) (\(a_k, \alpha_k\)).

2. Symbol table

SymbolMeaningHow to measure it
\(z_{k-1}\)Axis of joint \(k\)Points along the rotation axis (revolute) or sliding axis (prismatic).
\(x_k\)Common normal from \(z_{k-1}\) to \(z_k\)Perpendicular to both \(z_{k-1}\) and \(z_k\), pointing from \(z_{k-1}\) toward \(z_k\).
\(\theta_k\)Joint angle (variable if revolute)Right-hand angle from \(x_{k-1}\) to \(x_k\), measured about \(z_{k-1}\).
\(d_k\)Joint distance (variable if prismatic)Signed distance from \(O_{k-1}\) to the intersection of \(x_k\) and \(z_{k-1}\), measured along \(z_{k-1}\).
\(a_k\)Link length (always constant)Distance from \(z_{k-1}\) to \(z_k\), measured along \(x_k\) (positive when \(x_k\) points from \(z_{k-1}\) to \(z_k\)).
\(\alpha_k\)Link twist (always constant)Right-hand angle from \(z_{k-1}\) to \(z_k\), measured about \(x_k\).

3. Step-by-step D-H algorithm and link transform

  1. Place every \(z\)-axis first: Put \(z_{k-1}\) along joint \(k\)'s axis for \(k = 1, \dots, n\). For the tool frame (frame \(n\)), align \(z_n\) with the tool approach vector (pointing out of the gripper).
  2. Place the base frame \(\{x_0, y_0, z_0\}\): Put \(O_0\) on \(z_0\) (conveniently at joint 1 or where \(z_0\) meets \(z_1\)), choose \(x_0\) convenient (often parallel to the base), and complete right-handed \(y_0 = z_0 \times x_0\).
  3. Place each \(x_k\) for \(k = 1, \dots, n-1\):
    • If \(z_{k-1}\) and \(z_k\) are skew (do not intersect, not parallel): \(x_k\) is their unique common normal, from \(z_{k-1}\) to \(z_k\).
    • If \(z_{k-1}\) and \(z_k\) intersect: \(O_k\) is at the intersection (\(a_k = 0\)), and \(x_k = \pm(z_{k-1} \times z_k)\) (perpendicular to both).
    • If \(z_{k-1}\) and \(z_k\) are parallel: any perpendicular line between them works; choose the one that passes through \(O_{k-1}\) so \(d_k = 0\).
  4. Complete \(y_k = z_k \times x_k\) (right-hand rule) and read off \((\theta_k, d_k, a_k, \alpha_k)\) row by row.
  5. Write each link transform \(T^k_{k-1}\): Multiplying the four elementary motions \(\mathrm{Rot}(z, \theta_k)\,\mathrm{Trans}(0,0,d_k)\,\mathrm{Trans}(a_k,0,0)\,\mathrm{Rot}(x,\alpha_k)\) yields the standard matrix:
\[T^k_{k-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_k & -\cos\alpha_k\sin\theta_k & \sin\alpha_k\sin\theta_k & a_k\cos\theta_k \\ \sin\theta_k & \cos\alpha_k\cos\theta_k & -\sin\alpha_k\cos\theta_k & a_k\sin\theta_k \\ 0 & \sin\alpha_k & \cos\alpha_k & d_k \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.\]
Why Q1 is structured this way in the exam: Marks split roughly 11–12 for the link-coordinate diagram and 8–9 for the parameter table plus the first three \(T^k_{k-1}\) (the paper does not ask you to multiply all 6 matrices by hand). Every sign in the table comes directly from your drawn arrows, so neat, consistent right-handed axes earn most of the marks.

Worked mini-example (first link of a 2-link arm with vertical base): Suppose joint 1 rotates about vertical \(z_0\) by \(\theta_1\), joint 2's axis \(z_1\) is horizontal (pointing out of the page) at height \(L_1\) above \(O_0\), intersecting \(z_0\). Then \(O_1\) is at height \(L_1\) on \(z_0\), \(x_1\) points along the link at \(\theta_1 = 0\), giving: \(\theta_1\) (variable), \(d_1 = L_1\), \(a_1 = 0\), \(\alpha_1 = +90^\circ\) (right-hand thumb along \(x_1\) rotates \(z_0\) to \(z_1\)). Plugging \(\cos 90^\circ = 0\), \(\sin 90^\circ = 1\) into \(T^1_0\) gives:

\[T^1_0 = \begin{bmatrix} C_1 & 0 & S_1 & 0 \\ S_1 & 0 & -C_1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & L_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.\]
Pitfalls. (1) Never forget to draw \(y_k\) or check right-handedness (\(x_k \times y_k = z_k\)) at every frame. (2) Check the sign of \(\alpha_k\) with your right hand: thumb along \(x_k\), fingers curl from \(z_{k-1}\) toward \(z_k\). (3) You drilled four full 6-DOF exam arms in Quiz 1 mock 5.

Q2 · Robot control Exam focus

C1. Partitioned (computed-torque) control 5 of 5 papers

1. The big picture

A real robot arm is hard to control with a plain PD controller because its equations of motion are nonlinear (gravity \(\cos q\), Coriolis \(\dot q_1 \dot q_2 \sin q_2\), nonlinear springs \(kx^3\)) and coupled (accelerating joint 2 creates a reaction torque on joint 1). A plain PD controller tuned for one pose behaves sluggishly or oscillates wildly at another pose.

Computed-torque (partitioned) control solves this in two clean layers:

  • Inner layer (model-based portion): Uses the known physics model of the robot to cancel all nonlinearities, gravity, friction, and joint coupling. After cancellation, every joint looks like a completely decoupled unit mass \(\ddot q_i = v_i\).
  • Outer layer (servo portion): Designs a simple linear PD controller \(v_i\) for that unit mass so the tracking error decays cleanly to zero at any pose.
Key insight: Putting \(\tau = \alpha v + \beta\) directly in front of the robot turns the combined green + black blocks into a pure double integrator \(\ddot q = v\).

2. Symbol table

SymbolMeaningRole in the equations
\(q,\ \dot q,\ \ddot q\)Joint position, velocity, acceleration (scalar or \(n\times 1\) vector)Measured state of the robot.
\(q_d,\ \dot q_d,\ \ddot q_d\)Desired trajectory (position, velocity, acceleration)Reference motion we want the robot to follow.
\(e = q_d - q\)Tracking error (and \(\dot e = \dot q_d - \dot q\), \(\ddot e = \ddot q_d - \ddot q\))Note the sign: desired minus actual.
\(\tau\) (or \(u\))Actuator torque / force vector sent to the motorsOutput of the complete controller.
\(\alpha = M(q)\)Inertia (mass) matrix multiplying \(\ddot q\)\(n\times n\) matrix (scalar \(m\) for 1 DOF).
\(\beta(q,\dot q)\)All non-acceleration terms: Coriolis, centrifugal, friction, gravity, contact forceEverything left over on the LHS besides \(M(q)\ddot q\).
\(v\)Auxiliary (virtual) acceleration inputOutput of the linear PD servo portion.
\(k_p,\ k_v\) (or \(K_p, K_v\))Position and velocity feedback gainsChosen to set the closed-loop damping and speed (see C2).

3. Step-by-step derivation

  1. Start with the robot's equation of motion: Collect every term multiplying \(\ddot q\) into \(M(q)\ddot q\), and lump everything else (Coriolis, friction, nonlinear springs, gravity, contact forces) into \(h(q,\dot q)\): \[M(q)\,\ddot q + h(q,\dot q) = \tau.\]
  2. Choose the model-based control law \(\tau = \alpha v + \beta\): Substitute it into the dynamics: \[M(q)\,\ddot q + h(q,\dot q) = \alpha v + \beta.\] If we choose \(\alpha = M(q)\) and \(\beta = h(q,\dot q)\), the \(h(q,\dot q)\) terms cancel identically, leaving \(M(q)\ddot q = M(q)v\). Because the mass matrix \(M(q)\) is always invertible, multiplying by \(M(q)^{-1}\) gives: \[\ddot q = v.\] The nonlinear coupled robot now behaves as \(n\) independent unit masses!
  3. Design the linear servo \(v\): We want the error \(e = q_d - q\) to satisfy a stable linear second-order ODE \(\ddot e + k_v \dot e + k_p e = 0\). Since \(\ddot e = \ddot q_d - \ddot q = \ddot q_d - v\), rearranging for \(v\) gives: \[v = \ddot q_d + k_v \dot e + k_p e.\]
  4. Check the closed-loop error equation: Substitute \(v\) back into \(\ddot q = v\): \[\ddot q = \ddot q_d + k_v \dot e + k_p e \;\Longrightarrow\; (\ddot q_d - \ddot q) + k_v \dot e + k_p e = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \ddot e + k_v \dot e + k_p e = 0.\] Notice that no nonlinear terms and no mass parameters remain in the error equation.
\[\tau = \alpha v + \beta,\qquad \alpha = M(q),\qquad \beta = \text{all other terms},\qquad v = \ddot q_d + K_v \dot e + K_p e \;\Longrightarrow\; \ddot e + K_v \dot e + K_p e = 0.\]

4. Worked examples

Slide example (1-DOF nonlinear mass-spring-damper): Given \(m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx^3 = u\).

Only \(m\) multiplies \(\ddot x\), so \(\alpha = m\) and \(\beta = b\dot x + kx^3\). Complete control law: \(u = m(\ddot x_d + k_v\dot e + k_p e) + b\dot x + kx^3\).

Exercise 1(a) (1-DOF rotary): Given \(3\theta\ddot\theta + 5\dot\theta^2 - 2\cos\theta = \tau\).

The inertia depends on \(\theta\): \(\alpha = 3\theta\), and everything else is \(\beta = 5\dot\theta^2 - 2\cos\theta\).

Exercise 2 (3-joint manipulator): The three equations give a diagonal mass matrix \(\alpha = \mathrm{diag}(m_2 r^2,\ m_1+m_2,\ m_2)\) and \(\beta = [\,2m_2 r\dot r\dot\theta,\ (m_1+m_2)gh,\ -m_2 r\dot\theta^2\,]^T\).

Coupled 2-DOF example (like past finals): Suppose \(2\ddot q_1 + \ddot q_2 + 4\dot q_1 = u_1\) and \(\ddot q_1 + 3\ddot q_2 + 10 = u_2\). Rewrite in matrix form:

\[\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \ddot q_1 \\ \ddot q_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 4\dot q_1 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{bmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\; \alpha = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix},\quad \beta = \begin{bmatrix} 4\dot q_1 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix}.\]

Then \(u_1 = 2v_1 + v_2 + 4\dot q_1\) and \(u_2 = v_1 + 3v_2 + 10\). Notice how \(v_2\) appears inside \(u_1\) because of the off-diagonal inertia term!

Pitfall. In a coupled model such as \(2\ddot q_1+\ddot q_2+\dots=u_1\), the \(\ddot q_2\) term is inertia: it goes into \(\alpha\) as an off-diagonal entry, never into \(\beta\). Constant gravity terms (e.g. \(+100\)) and contact forces \(f\) have no \(\ddot q\), so they always go into \(\beta\).

C2. Choosing \(k_v\) and \(k_p\) 5 of 5 papers

1. The big picture

Once computed torque gives us the linear error equation \(\ddot e + k_v \dot e + k_p e = 0\), we must pick numerical values for \(k_v\) and \(k_p\). Making the gains too small makes the robot sluggish; making them too large excites hidden mechanical vibrations (gears, belts, flexible links) that were ignored in the rigid-body model. Two simple rules govern every exam question.

Two specifications to memorize: (1) \(\zeta \ge 1\) avoids overshoot; (2) \(\omega_n \le 0.5\,\omega_{res,\min}\) stays at least a factor of 2 below the lowest structural resonance.

2. Symbol table

SymbolMeaningUnits
\(\zeta\)Damping ratio (\(\zeta = 1\) critically damped; \(\zeta \gt 1\) overdamped)dimensionless
\(\omega_n\)Closed-loop natural frequency (bandwidth / speed of response)rad/s
\(\omega_{res}\)Unmodelled structural resonance frequency of the robotrad/s (convert Hz via \(\omega = 2\pi f\))
\(k_v,\ k_p\)Derivative (velocity) and proportional (position) servo gains\(k_v\): s\(^{-1}\), \(k_p\): s\(^{-2}\)

3. Step-by-step derivation

  1. Take the characteristic polynomial of \(\ddot e + k_v \dot e + k_p e = 0\): \[s^2 + k_v s + k_p = 0.\]
  2. Compare term-by-term with the standard second-order prototype \(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_n s + \omega_n^2 = 0\): \[k_v = 2\zeta\omega_n,\qquad k_p = \omega_n^2.\]
  3. Apply the two physical constraints:
    • No overshoot: \(\zeta \ge 1\). (Critically damped \(\zeta = 1\) gives \(k_v = 2\omega_n = 2\sqrt{k_p}\), the fastest non-overshooting response.)
    • Do not excite structural vibration: \(\omega_n \le 0.5\,\omega_{res,\min}\), using the lowest resonance listed in the problem.
\[k_v = 2\zeta\omega_n,\qquad k_p = \omega_n^2,\qquad \zeta \ge 1,\qquad \omega_n \le 0.5\,\omega_{res,\min}.\]
Why each condition matters: An overshooting robot arm (\(\zeta \lt 1\)) swings past its target and crashes into the workpiece or fixture. Setting \(\omega_n\) near \(\omega_{res}\) pumps energy into flexible structural modes that our rigid-body model ignores, causing violent shaking or instability. The lecturer in Week 6 explicitly stressed: “Remember these two specifications.” When an exam says “gains as high as possible” or gives no other speed rule, choose the boundary value \(\omega_n = 0.5\,\omega_{res,\min}\).

4. Worked examples

Lecture example: Structural resonance is \(\omega_{res} = 12\text{ rad/s}\), critically damped (\(\zeta = 1\)).
Step 1: \(\omega_n = 0.5 \times 12 = 6\text{ rad/s}\).
Step 2: \(k_v = 2(1)(6) = 12\), \(k_p = 6^2 = 36\).

Exercise 2 (Hz to rad/s conversion): Lowest resonance is \(14.5\text{ Hz}\), critically damped.
Step 1: Convert to rad/s: \(\omega_{res} = 2\pi(14.5) = 91.11\text{ rad/s}\).
Step 2: \(\omega_n \le 0.5(91.11) = 45.55\text{ rad/s}\). The lecturer's solution picks the clean round number \(\omega_n = 45\), giving \(k_v = 2(1)(45) = 90\) and \(k_p = 45^2 = 2025\).

Pitfalls. (1) Always check units: Hz must be multiplied by \(2\pi\) before taking \(0.5\,\omega_{res}\). (2) Always use the lowest resonance across the whole robot, never a higher resonance “for joint 2”. (3) Changing \(\zeta\) (e.g. overdamped \(\zeta = 1.25\)) changes only \(k_v\); \(k_p = \omega_n^2\) stays unchanged.

C3. Constant disturbance and integral action 4 of 5 papers

1. The big picture

Our computed-torque law \(\tau = \alpha v + \beta\) cancels every term we knew about. What if an unknown constant disturbance \(d\) pushes on the robot (for example, an unmeasured payload weight or constant joint friction)? With PD control alone, the robot stops slightly short of its target because it needs a nonzero error \(e_{ss}\) to generate a steady restoring force \(m k_p e_{ss}\) that balances \(d\). Adding an integral term \(k_i \int e\,dt\) builds up torque automatically until \(e\) is driven all the way to zero.

2. Symbol table

SymbolMeaning
\(d\)Unknown constant disturbance force/torque (Laplace transform \(D(s) = d/s\)).
\(e_{ss}\)Steady-state tracking error, \(\lim_{t\to\infty} e(t) = \lim_{s\to 0} s E(s)\).
\(k_i\) (or \(K_i\))Integral gain added to the servo law \(v\) to eliminate steady-state error.

3. Step-by-step derivation

  1. Insert the unknown disturbance \(d\) into the plant: \[m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx^3 + d = u.\] We do not know \(d\), so the controller still applies \(u = m(\ddot x_d + k_v \dot e + k_p e) + b\dot x + kx^3\).
  2. Cancel the known terms: \(b\dot x + kx^3\) cancels, leaving: \[m\ddot x + d = m(\ddot x_d + k_v \dot e + k_p e) \;\Longrightarrow\; m(\ddot e + k_v \dot e + k_p e) = d.\]
  3. Compute the steady-state error under PD control: Take the Laplace transform with \(D(s) = d/s\): \[E(s) = \frac{D(s)}{m(s^2 + k_v s + k_p)} = \frac{d/s}{m(s^2 + k_v s + k_p)}.\] By the Final-Value Theorem (valid because \(k_v, k_p \gt 0\) makes all poles stable): \[e_{ss} = \lim_{s\to 0} s E(s) = \lim_{s\to 0} \frac{s \cdot (d/s)}{m(s^2 + k_v s + k_p)} = \frac{d}{m\,k_p} \neq 0.\]
  4. Fix with integral action (PID computed torque): Change the servo to \(v = \ddot x_d + k_v\dot e + k_p e + k_i\!\int e\,dt\). Now the error equation becomes: \[m\left(\ddot e + k_v\dot e + k_p e + k_i\!\int e\,dt\right) = d \;\Longrightarrow\; m\left(s^2 + k_v s + k_p + \frac{k_i}{s}\right)E(s) = \frac{d}{s}.\] Multiply numerator and denominator by \(s\): \[E(s) = \frac{s\,D(s)}{m(s^3 + k_v s^2 + k_p s + k_i)} = \frac{d}{m(s^3 + k_v s^2 + k_p s + k_i)}.\] Applying the Final-Value Theorem now gives: \[e_{ss} = \lim_{s\to 0} s E(s) = \lim_{s\to 0} \frac{s\,d}{m(s^3 + k_v s^2 + k_p s + k_i)} = 0.\]
  5. Multi-joint generalization: For \(n\) joints with disturbance vector \(d\), \(M(q)(\ddot E + K_v\dot E + K_p E) = d\), so: \[\ddot E + K_v \dot E + K_p E = M(q)^{-1} d.\] Even if \(d\) acts on only one joint, a non-diagonal \(M(q)^{-1}\) spreads steady-state error to every coupled joint. Adding \(K_i \int E\,dt\) drives the entire error vector \(E_{ss}\) to \(0\).
\[\text{PD only: } e_{ss} = \frac{d}{m\,k_p} \quad\big(\text{multi-joint: } E_{ss} = K_p^{-1}M^{-1}d\big);\qquad \text{PID } (+k_i\!\int e\,dt)\text{: } e_{ss} = 0.\]

Worked example (coupled 2-joint steady error): Suppose \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), \(K_p = 36 I\), and constant disturbance \(d = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\). First invert \(M\): \(\det M = 3(1) - 1(1) = 2\), so \(M^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\). Then \(M^{-1}d = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 2-1 \\ -2+3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\), and \(E_{ss} = \frac{1}{36}\begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.0139 \\ 0.0139 \end{bmatrix}\). Adding \(K_i \int E\,dt\) removes both errors.

Pitfall. When adding integral action, the characteristic polynomial becomes cubic: \(s^3 + k_v s^2 + k_p s + k_i = 0\). By Routh–Hurwitz, stability requires \(k_i \gt 0\) and \(k_v k_p \gt k_i\), so \(k_i\) must not be made arbitrarily large.

C4. Force control 5 of 5 papers (inside hybrid)

1. The big picture

When a robot polishes a car hood or pushes a peg into a hole, commanding position into a stiff surface is dangerous: even a 1 mm position error against a rigid wall produces a huge crushing force. Instead, we command the contact force \(f_d\) directly. We model the contact surface as a linear spring of stiffness \(k_e\) located at \(x_e\): pushing the robot to position \(x \gt x_e\) compresses the surface and creates force \(f = k_e(x - x_e)\).

Core bridge: Because \(f\) is proportional to \(x - x_e\), controlling acceleration \(\ddot x = v\) directly controls the second derivative of force \(\ddot f = k_e v\).

2. Symbol table

SymbolMeaning
\(k_e\)Environment (surface) stiffness (N/m).
\(x_e\)Undeformed surface location (constant unless the surface drifts).
\(f = k_e(x - x_e)\)Contact force exerted by the robot on the surface.
\(f_d,\ \dot f_d,\ \ddot f_d\)Desired force trajectory (often constant, so \(\dot f_d = \ddot f_d = 0\)).
\(e_f = f_d - f\)Force tracking error (desired minus actual).
\(k_{vf},\ k_{pf}\)Force-loop derivative and proportional gains (obeying the same \(\zeta, \omega_n\) rules as C2).

3. Step-by-step derivation

  1. Write the robot dynamics in contact: The surface pushes back on the robot with force \(f\), so: \[m\ddot x + f = u.\]
  2. Apply the model-based partition \(u = \alpha v + \beta\): Choose \(\alpha = m\) and \(\beta = f\) (include the contact force in \(\beta\) so it is cancelled!). Then \(m\ddot x + f = m v + f \implies \ddot x = v\).
  3. Convert \(\ddot x = v\) into a force plant: Differentiate \(f = k_e(x - x_e)\) twice (assuming constant \(x_e\)): \[\dot f = k_e \dot x,\qquad \ddot f = k_e \ddot x \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{k_e}\ddot f = v.\]
  4. Design the force servo \(v\): To make \(e_f = f_d - f\) obey \(\ddot e_f + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f = 0\), we need \(\ddot f = \ddot f_d + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f\). Divide by \(k_e\): \[v = \frac{1}{k_e}\big(\ddot f_d + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f\big).\]
  5. Check the closed-loop force error: Substituting \(v\) into \(\frac{1}{k_e}\ddot f = v\) cancels \(1/k_e\) and leaves: \[\ddot e_f + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f = 0.\] We then pick \(k_{vf} = 2\zeta_f\omega_{nf}\) and \(k_{pf} = \omega_{nf}^2\) with \(\zeta_f \ge 1\) and \(\omega_{nf} \le 0.5\,\omega_{res,\min}\).
\[u = m v + f,\qquad v = \frac{1}{k_e}\big(\ddot f_d + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f\big),\qquad e_f = f_d - f \;\Longrightarrow\; \ddot e_f + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f = 0.\]
With vs. without a force sensor: If a force sensor is mounted at the wrist, \(f\) is measured directly. If no force sensor is available (tested in 25-26 S2), we estimate \(f = k_e(x - x_e)\) and \(\dot f = k_e \dot x\) from the joint encoder and tachometer. That works only as long as our assumed \(k_e\) and \(x_e\) match the real surface (see C6).

Worked example: Surface stiffness \(k_e = 50\text{ N/m}\), lowest resonance \(10\text{ rad/s}\), critically damped force loop (\(\zeta_f = 1\)).
Step 1: \(\omega_n = 0.5(10) = 5\text{ rad/s}\), so \(k_{vf} = 2(1)(5) = 10\) and \(k_{pf} = 5^2 = 25\).

C5. Hybrid position/force control 5 of 5 papers

1. The big picture

In real contact tasks (writing on a whiteboard, wiping a table), you cannot control both position and force along the same axis at the same time. Instead, space splits into two orthogonal subspaces:

  • Force subspace (normal to the surface): the environment constrains motion here, so we control the contact force.
  • Motion subspace (tangent to the surface): the robot is free to slide here, so we control the position.

Every final exam Q2 puts C1–C4 together into one hybrid controller for a 2-, 3-, or 4-DOF robot.

How to identify the subspace instantly: Read the contact-force equation given in the question. Whichever coordinate appears inside \(f = k_e(q_j - q_{je})\) is the force-controlled coordinate; all remaining coordinates are position-controlled.

2. Complete 5-step exam procedure

  1. Split the coordinates: Look at the given force law, e.g. \(f = 25(q_3 - 0.2)\). Only \(q_3\) changes \(f\), so \(q_3\) is in the force subspace and \(q_1, q_2\) are in the motion subspace.
  2. Build one shared model-based layer for the whole robot: Collect all \(\ddot q\) terms into \(\alpha = M(q)\) and all remaining terms—including \(f\)—into \(\beta\). Setting \(\tau = \alpha v + \beta\) decouples every joint to \(\ddot q = v\).
  3. Write the motion-subspace servo laws: For each free coordinate \(i\), \[v_i = \ddot q_{id} + k_v \dot e_i + k_p e_i,\qquad e_i = q_{id} - q_i,\] using the motion loop's specified damping ratio \(\zeta_m\) and \(\omega_n \le 0.5\,\omega_{res,\min}\).
  4. Write the force-subspace servo law: For the contact coordinate (say \(q_3\)), \[v_3 = \frac{1}{k_e}\big(\ddot f_d + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f\big),\qquad e_f = f_d - f,\] using the force loop's specified damping ratio \(\zeta_f\) and the same \(\omega_n \le 0.5\,\omega_{res,\min}\).
  5. Write \(\tau = \alpha v + \beta\) out in full and state the error equations:
    • Without disturbances: \(\ddot e_i + k_v \dot e_i + k_p e_i = 0\) (motion) and \(\ddot e_f + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f = 0\) (force).
    • With constant disturbances \(d\) on the motion joints: \(\ddot E + K_v \dot E + K_p E = M^{-1} d\) (motion, giving steady error \(E_{ss} = K_p^{-1} M^{-1} d\)) and \(\ddot e_f + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f = 0\) (force, if no disturbance reaches the force joint). State that adding integral action \(K_i \int E\,dt\) drives \(E_{ss} \to 0\).

Worked example (2-DOF Cartesian \(xy\) robot): Dynamics \(m_1\ddot x + f = u_1\), \(m_2\ddot y = u_2\), with \(f = k_e(x - x_e)\). Here \(x\) is force-controlled and \(y\) is position-controlled.
Model-based part: \(\alpha = \mathrm{diag}(m_1, m_2)\), \(\beta = [f,\ 0]^T\).
Servo part: \(v_1 = \frac{1}{k_e}(\ddot f_d + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f)\) and \(v_2 = \ddot y_d + k_v \dot e_y + k_p e_y\).
Complete law: \(u_1 = \frac{m_1}{k_e}(\ddot f_d + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f) + f\), \(u_2 = m_2(\ddot y_d + k_v \dot e_y + k_p e_y)\).

Pitfall. Exam questions often give different damping ratios for the two loops (e.g. motion \(\zeta = 1.05\), force \(\zeta = 1\)). Keep \(k_v, k_p\) and \(k_{vf}, k_{pf}\) clearly labelled so you do not mix them up.

1. The big picture

Two official finals ask a follow-up “what if?” part: what happens when the controller's assumed contact model does not match the real surface? Note: the lecture slides do not derive these two mismatch cases; the derivations below are our own, built directly from the slide force-control equations (C4).

2. Case A: Surface stiffness is misjudged (\(\hat k \neq k\))

SymbolMeaning
\(\hat k\)Stiffness assumed inside the controller's servo law \(v = \frac{1}{\hat k}(\ddot f_d + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f)\).
\(k\)True stiffness of the physical surface (\(\ddot f = k\,\ddot x = k\,v\)).
\(\rho = k/\hat k\)Stiffness ratio (true divided by assumed).
  1. Plug the controller's \(v\) into the true plant \(\ddot f = k\,v\): \[\ddot f = k \cdot \frac{1}{\hat k}\big(\ddot f_d + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f\big) = \rho\big(\ddot f_d + k_{vf}\dot e_f + k_{pf} e_f\big).\]
  2. Form the error equation using \(\ddot e_f = \ddot f_d - \ddot f\): \[\ddot e_f + \rho k_{vf}\dot e_f + \rho k_{pf} e_f = (1 - \rho)\ddot f_d.\]
  3. Extract the actual closed-loop \(\omega_n'\) and \(\zeta'\): Compare \(s^2 + \rho k_{vf} s + \rho k_{pf}\) with \(s^2 + 2\zeta'\omega_n' s + (\omega_n')^2\), remembering \(k_{pf} = \omega_n^2\) and \(k_{vf} = 2\zeta\omega_n\): \[(\omega_n')^2 = \rho\,\omega_n^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \omega_n' = \omega_n\sqrt{\rho},\qquad 2\zeta'\omega_n' = \rho(2\zeta\omega_n) \;\Longrightarrow\; \zeta' = \zeta\sqrt{\rho}.\]
\[\ddot e_f + \rho k_{vf}\dot e_f + \rho k_{pf} e_f = (1 - \rho)\ddot f_d,\qquad \omega_n' = \omega_n\sqrt{\rho},\qquad \zeta' = \zeta\sqrt{\rho},\qquad \rho = \frac{k}{\hat k}.\]
Physical consequence: For a constant desired force (\(\ddot f_d = 0\)), the steady-state error still goes to \(0\) (as long as \(\rho \gt 0\)). However, if the true surface is stiffer than assumed (\(\rho \gt 1\)), \(\omega_n' = \omega_n\sqrt{\rho}\) exceeds the designed \(\omega_n\) and can violate the resonance limit \(\omega_n' \le 0.5\,\omega_{res}\)! If the surface is softer (\(\rho \lt 1\)), \(\zeta' = \zeta\sqrt{\rho}\) drops below \(1\) when designed at \(\zeta = 1\), introducing overshoot.

3. Case B: No force sensor and the surface position \(x_e\) drifts

  1. What the controller sees: Without a force sensor, the controller estimates force from position using its assumed surface location \(x_e\): \(\hat f = k(x - x_e)\).
  2. Where the controller drives the robot: The servo drives \(\hat f \to f_d\), so the robot settles at \(x = x_e + f_d / k\).
  3. What the true force actually is: Because the surface really sits at \(x_e'\), the true contact force is: \[f = k(x - x_e') = k\!\left(x_e + \frac{f_d}{k} - x_e'\right) = f_d + k(x_e - x_e').\]
\[\text{True steady force: } f_{ss} = f_d + k(x_e - x_e'),\qquad \text{unseen force bias} = k(x_e - x_e').\]
Why integral action cannot fix Case B: The integrator only sees \(\hat e_f = f_d - \hat f\), which is already \(0\)! The only true fixes are to add a force sensor (direct feedback of \(f\)) or re-identify \(x_e'\) before contact.

Q3(a) · Mobile-platform constraints Exam focus

M1. One fixed standard wheel 5 of 5 papers

1. The big picture

A wheeled mobile robot moves on a flat floor with 3 chassis velocities in its own body frame \(\{X_R, Y_R\}\): forward \(\dot x_r\), sideways \(\dot y_r\), and spin rate \(\dot\theta\). Every standard wheel attached to the chassis obeys two physical laws at its contact patch:

  • Rolling constraint: The component of chassis velocity along the wheel's rolling direction must equal the wheel's rim speed \(r\dot\varphi\) (no wheelspin/skidding).
  • Sliding constraint: The component of chassis velocity along the wheel's axle (sideways) must equal \(0\) (no sideways slip).
How the slides define \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\): \(P\) is the robot reference point, \(l\) is distance \(PA\), \(\alpha\) is the angle of \(PA\) from \(X_R\), and \(\beta\) is the angle from the extension of \(PA\) to the wheel's axle line. Positive wheel spin \(\dot\varphi\) drives the wheel along the rolling direction \((\alpha+\beta-90^\circ)\).

2. Symbol table

SymbolMeaning
\(l,\ \alpha\)Polar coordinates of wheel hub \(A\) relative to \(P\) in robot frame \(\{X_R, Y_R\}\).
\(\beta\)Fixed angle of the wheel plane relative to line \(PA\) (chosen so positive \(\dot\varphi\) rolls forward).
\(r,\ \dot\varphi\)Wheel radius and wheel angular velocity.
\(R(\theta)\dot\xi_I = [\dot x_r,\ \dot y_r,\ \dot\theta]^T\)Robot chassis velocity expressed in the robot's local frame \(\{X_R, Y_R\}\).

3. Step-by-step derivation (so you never guess a sign)

  1. Velocity of wheel hub \(A\) on the rigid chassis: Point \(A\) sits at \(\mathbf{r}_A = (l\cos\alpha,\ l\sin\alpha)\). Spinning the chassis at \(\dot\theta\) adds \(\dot\theta \hat k \times \mathbf{r}_A = (-\dot\theta l\sin\alpha,\ +\dot\theta l\cos\alpha)\), so: \[\mathbf{v}_A = \begin{bmatrix} \dot x_r - \dot\theta l\sin\alpha \\ \dot y_r + \dot\theta l\cos\alpha \end{bmatrix}.\]
  2. Project onto the rolling unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_\parallel = (\sin(\alpha+\beta),\ -\cos(\alpha+\beta))\): \[\mathbf{v}_A \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}}_\parallel = \dot x_r\sin(\alpha+\beta) - \dot y_r\cos(\alpha+\beta) - \dot\theta l\big(\sin\alpha\sin(\alpha+\beta) + \cos\alpha\cos(\alpha+\beta)\big).\] By the cosine difference identity \(\cos(A-B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B\), the bracket collapses cleanly to \(\cos\beta\)! Setting \(\mathbf{v}_A \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}}_\parallel = r\dot\varphi\) gives the rolling constraint.
  3. Project onto the axle unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_\perp = (\cos(\alpha+\beta),\ \sin(\alpha+\beta))\): \[\mathbf{v}_A \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}}_\perp = \dot x_r\cos(\alpha+\beta) + \dot y_r\sin(\alpha+\beta) + \dot\theta l\big(\cos\alpha\sin(\alpha+\beta) - \sin\alpha\cos(\alpha+\beta)\big).\] By the sine difference identity \(\sin(A-B) = \sin A\cos B - \cos A\sin B\), the bracket collapses to \(\sin\beta\)! Setting \(\mathbf{v}_A \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}}_\perp = 0\) gives the sliding constraint.
\[\text{Rolling: }\big[\sin(\alpha{+}\beta)\ \ {-\cos(\alpha{+}\beta)}\ \ {-l\cos\beta}\big]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I - r\dot\varphi = 0\] \[\text{Sliding: }\big[\cos(\alpha{+}\beta)\ \ \sin(\alpha{+}\beta)\ \ l\sin\beta\big]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I = 0\]

4. Worked examples

Example 2 (left wheel, above \(P\) on \(+Y_R\), rolling along \(+X_R\)): \(\alpha = \pi/2\), \(\beta = 0\).
Then \(\alpha+\beta = \pi/2\), \(\sin(\pi/2) = 1\), \(\cos(\pi/2) = 0\), \(\cos 0 = 1\), \(\sin 0 = 0\):
Rolling: \([1\ \ 0\ \ {-l}]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I - r\dot\varphi = 0 \implies \dot x_r - l\dot\theta = r\dot\varphi\). (Sanity check: turning left, \(\dot\theta \gt 0\), slows the left wheel — right!)
Sliding: \([0\ \ 1\ \ 0]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I = 0 \implies \dot y_r = 0\).

Example 4, Wheel B (right wheel, below \(P\) on \(-Y_R\), also rolling along \(+X_R\)): \(\alpha = -\pi/2\), and we set \(\beta = \pi\) so \(\alpha + \beta = +\pi/2\) and positive \(\dot\varphi\) still drives along \(+X_R\).
Then \(\cos\beta = \cos\pi = -1\), so \(-l\cos\beta = +l\):
Rolling: \([1\ \ 0\ \ {+l}]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I - r\dot\varphi = 0 \implies \dot x_r + l\dot\theta = r\dot\varphi\). (Sanity check: turning left speeds up the right wheel — right!)

Pitfall. Always sanity-check your signs with a physical test! Plug \(\dot x_r = 1, \dot y_r = 0, \dot\theta = 0\) into your rolling row: you should get \(+1\) if positive \(\dot\varphi\) moves the robot forward. On the mirror-image wheel, forgetting \(\beta = \pi\) flips the sign of \(-l\cos\beta\).

M2. Steered standard and castor wheels 5 of 5 papers

1. The big picture

Not every wheel is bolted at a fixed angle. Exam platforms mix fixed standard wheels with two rotating-axis wheel types:

  • Steered standard wheel: The vertical steering shaft passes directly through the wheel contact point \(A\). Turning the steering angle \(\beta(t)\) rotates the wheel in place without dragging the contact patch sideways.
  • Castor wheel (shopping-cart / office-chair wheel): The vertical swivel shaft at \(A\) is offset by a rigid arm of length \(d\) from the contact point \(B\). Swivelling the arm at rate \(\dot\beta\) swings the contact point sideways at speed \(d\dot\beta\).
Key geometric difference: Because the castor arm \(AB\) of length \(d\) lies along the wheel plane, rotating it at \(\dot\beta\) creates a sideways velocity \(d\dot\beta\) perpendicular to the wheel plane — i.e. purely in the sliding direction.

2. Exact equations for both wheel types

  1. Steered standard wheel: Replace the constant \(\beta\) in M1 with the time-varying steering angle \(\beta_s(t)\): \[\text{Rolling: }\big[\sin(\alpha{+}\beta_s)\ \ {-\cos(\alpha{+}\beta_s)}\ \ {-l\cos\beta_s}\big]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I - r\dot\varphi = 0\] \[\text{Sliding: }\big[\cos(\alpha{+}\beta_s)\ \ \sin(\alpha{+}\beta_s)\ \ l\sin\beta_s\big]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I = 0.\] Keep \(\beta_s\) symbolic in the equations (do not substitute a fixed number unless a later part asks for the steering angle at a specific motion).
  2. Castor wheel: Rolling is unchanged, while the sideways velocity at contact point \(B\) gains \(+d\dot\beta\): \[\text{Rolling: }\big[\sin(\alpha{+}\beta)\ \ {-\cos(\alpha{+}\beta)}\ \ {-l\cos\beta}\big]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I - r\dot\varphi = 0\] \[\text{Sliding: }\big[\cos(\alpha{+}\beta)\ \ \sin(\alpha{+}\beta)\ \ l\sin\beta\big]\,R(\theta)\dot\xi_I + d\dot\beta = 0.\]
Why a castor wheel never restricts the chassis: For any chassis velocity \((\dot x_r, \dot y_r, \dot\theta)\), we can pick \(\dot\varphi\) to satisfy the rolling equation and pick \(\dot\beta\) to satisfy the sliding equation (because \(d \neq 0\)). That is why an office chair can move in any direction immediately — its castors just swivel to follow.
Pitfall. On a platform with a castor wheel, \(\beta\) is a variable just like the steered wheel's, and the \(+d\dot\beta\) term must appear in its sliding row. Writing the castor identically to a steered wheel loses that mark.

1. The big picture

Once you have written the rolling and sliding rows for each individual wheel, stack them into two compact matrix equations for the whole mobile robot. The sliding equation \(C_1 R(\theta)\dot\xi_I = 0\) tells you which chassis motions are physically allowed (e.g. \(\dot y_r = 0\)); the rolling equation \(J_1 R(\theta)\dot\xi_I = J_2 \dot\varphi\) immediately gives the required spin speed \(\dot\varphi_i\) of every wheel.

2. Symbol table & stacked form

SymbolMeaning
\(J_1(\beta_s)\)Stacked rolling rows of all fixed and steered standard wheels (one row per wheel).
\(J_2 = \mathrm{diag}(r_1,\dots,r_N)\)Diagonal matrix of wheel radii.
\(C_1(\beta_s)\)Stacked sliding rows of all fixed and steered standard wheels (castors are omitted from \(C_1\) because they impose no chassis constraint, or written separately with \(+d\dot\beta\)).
\(\dot\varphi = [\dot\varphi_1,\dots,\dot\varphi_N]^T\)Vector of wheel angular speeds.

  1. Check feasible chassis motion with \(C_1\): Two parallel fixed wheels (like a differential-drive robot) both give the sliding row \([0\ 1\ 0]\), which forces \(\dot y_r = 0\): the robot cannot slide sideways.

Q4 · Homography Refresh

H1. Which four-point groups work 5 of 5 papers

1. The big picture

When a camera views points lying on a flat plane (a poster, floor, or calibration board) from two different poses, the pixel coordinates \((x, y)\) in image 1 and \((u, v)\) in image 2 are linked by a single \(3\times 3\) projective matrix \(H\) called a homography. Exam Q4 tests three things every year: (1) counting which 4-point subsets can uniquely determine \(H\), (2) writing out the \(8\times 8\) linear system for the normalized matrix \(H_n\), and (3) chaining homographies across a third camera pose when two poses do not share enough usable points.

The geometry rule: Any 4 points that form a non-degenerate quadrilateral (or triangle + interior point) work. As soon as 3 or more lie on one straight line, the 8 equations lose rank and fail.

2. Step-by-step derivation of the 4-point linear system

  1. Projective relation up to scale: In homogeneous coordinates, \(\lambda [u,\ v,\ 1]^T = H [x,\ y,\ 1]^T\). Multiplying \(H\) by any nonzero constant represents the same mapping, so we normalize by \(h_{33}\): \[H_n = \frac{H}{h_{33}} = \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\ h_{31} & h_{32} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{8 unknowns}).\]
  2. Eliminate the unknown scale \(\lambda\): The third row gives \(\lambda = h_{31}x + h_{32}y + 1\). Substitute \(\lambda\) into the first two rows to get 2 linear equations per matched point: \[h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13} - u x\,h_{31} - u y\,h_{32} = u,\] \[h_{21}x + h_{22}y + h_{23} - v x\,h_{31} - v y\,h_{32} = v.\]
  3. Stack 4 matched points: Four points give \(4 \times 2 = 8\) linear equations \(A h = b\) for the 8 unknowns \(h = [h_{11}, h_{12}, h_{13}, h_{21}, h_{22}, h_{23}, h_{31}, h_{32}]^T\). The matrix \(A\) is invertible if and only if all 4 points are visible in both images, coplanar, and no three are collinear.
  4. Counting valid groups: Given \(n\) coplanar points visible in both poses, start with all \(\binom{n}{4}\) 4-point subsets and subtract every subset that contains a collinear triple (a line with 3 points knocks out \(n - 3\) subsets; a line with 4 points knocks out \(\binom{4}{3}(n-4) + 1\) subsets — always check for overlaps between lines!).
  5. Chaining through a third pose: If pose \(a \to b\) has homography \(G\) and pose \(b \to c\) has homography \(H\), then even when poses \(a\) and \(c\) share fewer than 4 usable points, their homography is: \[K \propto H G \;\Longrightarrow\; K_n = \frac{H G}{(HG)_{33}}.\]
\[\text{Valid groups} = \binom{n}{4} - \#\{\text{groups with }\ge 3\text{ collinear}\};\qquad K_n = \frac{HG}{(HG)_{33}}.\]

Worked example (counting with two lines): Suppose 6 coplanar points have one line of 3 points \(\{1,2,3\}\) and no other collinear triples. Total 4-point groups: \(\binom{6}{4} = 15\). Bad groups must contain all of \(\{1,2,3\}\) plus 1 of the other 3 points \(\{4,5,6\}\), giving \(3\) bad groups. Valid groups: \(15 - 3 = 12\).

Pitfall. When a line has 4 points (e.g. \(\{1,2,3,4\}\)), do not double-count the single 4-point group \(\{1,2,3,4\}\) as four separate collinear triples! Also remember order matters in chaining: if \(p_b \propto G p_a\) and \(p_c \propto H p_b\), then \(p_c \propto H G p_a\) (not \(G H\)), and you must divide by the \((3,3)\) entry to get \(K_n\). Q5 · Multi-sensor Kalman Refresh

K1. One gain per sensor vs one shared gain 5 of 5 papers

1. The big picture

We want to estimate an unknown constant quantity \(x\) (\(x_{k+1} = x_k\)) using our prior estimate \(\hat x_k\) (with error variance \(p_k\)) plus \(n\) independent noisy sensors \(y_{ik} = x_k + v_{ik}\) (with noise variances \(\sigma_i^2\)). A better sensor has a smaller variance \(\sigma_i^2\), so it should receive a larger weight \(L_i\). The Kalman filter finds the exact gains \(L_i\) that minimize the posterior error variance \(p_{k+1}\).

Information adds: Define information (precision) as \(1/\text{variance}\). With optimal separate gains, the posterior information is simply the prior information plus the sum of every sensor's information.

2. Symbol table

SymbolMeaning
\(\tilde x_k = x_k - \hat x_k\)Estimation error at step \(k\), with zero mean and variance \(p_k = \mathbb{E}[\tilde x_k^2]\).
\(v_{ik}\)Zero-mean noise of sensor \(i\) (\(\mathbb{E}[v_{ik}^2] = \sigma_i^2\)), independent of \(\tilde x_k\) and other sensors.
\(L_i\)Kalman gain (weight) assigned to sensor \(i\)'s innovation \((y_{ik} - \hat x_k)\).
\(p_{k+1}\)Updated (posterior) error variance \(\mathbb{E}[\tilde x_{k+1}^2]\) — smaller is better.

3. The four-move exam derivation (separate gains)

  1. Write the update and subtract from \(x_{k+1} = x_k\): From \(\hat x_{k+1} = \hat x_k + \sum_{i=1}^n L_i(y_{ik} - \hat x_k)\) and \(y_{ik} - \hat x_k = \tilde x_k + v_{ik}\): \[\tilde x_{k+1} = \Big(1 - \sum_{i=1}^n L_i\Big)\tilde x_k - \sum_{i=1}^n L_i v_{ik}.\]
  2. Square and take expectations: All cross terms vanish because \(\tilde x_k\) and \(v_{ik}\) are independent and zero-mean: \[p_{k+1} = \Big(1 - \sum_{i=1}^n L_i\Big)^2 p_k + \sum_{i=1}^n L_i^2 \sigma_i^2.\]
  3. Minimize \(p_{k+1}\) by setting \(\partial p_{k+1}/\partial L_i = 0\): \[-2\Big(1 - \sum_j L_j\Big) p_k + 2 L_i \sigma_i^2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; L_i = \frac{p_k}{\sigma_i^2}\Big(1 - \sum_j L_j\Big).\] Sum this equation over all \(i = 1,\dots,n\) to find \(\sum_j L_j = p_k\big(\sum_j 1/\sigma_j^2\big)(1 - \sum_j L_j)\), which solves cleanly for \(1 - \sum_j L_j = \dfrac{1}{1 + p_k \sum_j 1/\sigma_j^2}\). Plugging back gives each gain: \[L_i = \frac{p_k}{\sigma_i^2\,\big(1 + p_k \sum_j 1/\sigma_j^2\big)}.\]
  4. Substitute back into \(p_{k+1}\): Using \(L_i \sigma_i^2 = (1 - \sum_j L_j)p_k\), the variance simplifies immediately to \(p_{k+1} = (1 - \sum_j L_j)p_k = \dfrac{p_k}{1 + p_k \sum_i 1/\sigma_i^2}\), or in reciprocal form: \[\frac{1}{p_{k+1}} = \frac{1}{p_k} + \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2}.\]

4. One shared gain vs. separate gains, and comparing sensor groups

  1. One shared gain (\(L_1 = \dots = L_n = L\)): Setting all gains equal gives \(p_{k+1} = (1 - nL)^2 p_k + L^2 \sum_i \sigma_i^2\). Differentiating with respect to \(L\) gives: \[L = \frac{n p_k}{n^2 p_k + \sum_i \sigma_i^2},\qquad \frac{1}{p_{k+1}^{\text{shared}}} = \frac{1}{p_k} + \frac{n^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2}.\]
  2. Why separate gains are never worse: By Cauchy–Schwarz, \(\big(\sum_i 1/\sigma_i^2\big)\big(\sum_i \sigma_i^2\big) \ge n^2\), so: \[\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2} \ge \frac{n^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; p_{k+1}^{\text{separate}} \le p_{k+1}^{\text{shared}},\] with equality only when all \(\sigma_i\) are equal. The trade-off: a single shared gain is simpler to implement (one gain to compute instead of \(n\)), but it wastes accuracy whenever one sensor is much noisier than another because it cannot down-weight the noisy sensor.
  3. Comparing two sensor groups (e.g. \(\{S_1, S_2\}\) vs \(\{S_3, S_4, S_5\}\)): Compute \(\sum 1/\sigma_i^2\) for each group. The group with the larger sum of inverse variances always yields the smaller \(p_{k+1}\).
\[\text{Separate gains: }\frac{1}{p_{k+1}} = \frac{1}{p_k} + \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2}\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad \text{Shared gain: }\frac{1}{p_{k+1}} = \frac{1}{p_k} + \frac{n^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2}.\]

Worked example (2 sensors, \(\sigma_1^2 = a^2\), \(\sigma_2^2 = 9a^2\)):
Separate gains: \(\sum 1/\sigma_i^2 = \frac{1}{a^2} + \frac{1}{9a^2} = \frac{10}{9a^2} \approx \frac{1.111}{a^2}\).
One shared gain: \(\frac{n^2}{\sum \sigma_i^2} = \frac{4}{a^2 + 9a^2} = \frac{4}{10a^2} = \frac{0.4}{a^2}\).
Since \(\frac{10}{9} \gt \frac{4}{10}\), separate gains give a much smaller posterior variance \(p_{k+1}\)! Notice also that in the shared-gain case, the noisy \(9a^2\) sensor drags the pair down below even using sensor 1 alone (\(1/a^2\)).

Pitfall. Always check whether the question gives sensor variance (\(\sigma_i^2\)) or standard deviation (\(\sigma_i\)). Week 11 transcript note: two sensors is the key case, and three sensors work identically. Full drill: Quiz 2 past-paper guided page.
